Commentary on Book I Postulate 2
================================

Back to :ref:`Book I Postulates <I.post>`.

**Καὶ πεπερασμένην εὐθεῖαν κατὰ τὸ συνεχὲς ἐπ’ εὐθείας ἐκβαλεῖν.**

*To produce a finite straight line continuously in a straight line.*

----

I translate πεπερασμένην by *finite*, because that is the received equivalent, and because any alternative word such as *limited*, *terminated*, if applied to a straight line, would equally fail to express what modern Italian geometers aptly call a *rectilineal segment*, that is, a straight line having *two* extremities.

Just as Post. 1 asserting the possibility of drawing a straight line from any one point to another must be held to declare at the same time that the straight line so drawn is unique, so Post. 2 maintaining the possibility of producing a finite straight line (a "rectilineal segment") continuously in a straight line must also be held to assert that the straight line can only be produced *in one way* at either end, or that the produced part in either direction is *unique*; in other words, that *two straight lines cannot have a common segment*. This latter assumption is not expressly appealed to by :person:`Euclid <euclid>` until XI. 1. But it is needed at the very beginning of Book I. :person:`Proclus <proclus>` (:cite:`p. 214, 18 <proclus:in-euclid:214.18>`) says that :person:`Zeno <zeno>` of Sidon, an Epicurean, maintained that the very first proposition I. 1 requires it to be admitted that "two straight lines cannot have the same segments"; otherwise $AC$, $BC$ might meet before they arrive at $C$ and have the rest of their length common, in which case the actual triangle formed by them and $AB$ would not be equilateral. The assumption that two straight lines cannot have a common segment is certainly necessary in I. 4, where one side of one triangle is placed on that side of the other
triangle which is equal to it, and it is inferred that the two coincide throughout their length: this would by no means follow if two straight lines could have a common segment. :person:`Proclus <proclus>` (:cite:`p. 215, 24 <proclus:in-euclid:215.24>`), while observing that Post. 2 clearly indicates that the produced portion must be *one*, attempts to prove it, but unsuccessfully. Both he and :person:`Simplicius <simplicius>` practically use the same argument. Suppose, says :person:`Proclus <proclus>`, that the straight lines :g:`AC <0211.g1:/ A C />`, :g:`AD <0211.g1:/ A D />` have :g:`AB <0211.g1:/ A B />` as a common segment. With centre :g:`B <0211.g1:B>` and radius :g:`BA <0211.g1:/ B A />` describe a circle (Post. 3) meeting :g:`AC <0211.g1:/ A C />`, :g:`AD <0211.g1:/ A D />` in :g:`C <0211.g1:C>`, :g:`D <0211.g1:D>`. Then, since :g:`ABC <0211.g1:/ A B C />` is a straight line through the centre, :g:`AEC <0211.g1:( A E C )>` is a semi-circle. Similarly, :g:`ABD <0211.g1:/ A B D />` being a straight line through the centre, :g:`AED <0211.g1:( A E D )>` is a semi-circle. Therefore :g:`AEC <0211.g1:( A E C )>` is :eq:`equal <0211.g1:( A E C ) = 0211.g1:( A E D )>` to :g:`AED <0211.g1:( A E D )>`: which is impossible.

:person:`Proclus <proclus>` observes that :person:`Zeno <zeno>` would object to this proof as really depending on the assumption that "two circumferences (of circles) cannot have one portion common"; for this, he would say, is assumed in the common proof by superposition of the fact that a circle is bisected by a diameter, since that proof takes it for granted that, if one part of the circumference cut off by the diameter, when applied to the other, does not coincide with it, it must necessarily fall either *entirely* outside or *entirely* inside it, whereas there is nothing to prevent their coinciding, not altogether, but in part only; and, until you really prove the bisection of a circle by its diameter, the above proof is not valid. :person:`Posidonius <posidonius>` is represented as having derided :person:`Zeno <zeno>` for not seeing that the proof of the bisection of a circle by its diameter goes on just as well if the circumferences fail to coincide *in part* only. But the true objection to the proof above given is that the proof of the bisection of a circle by any diameter *itself* assumes that two straight lines cannot have a common segment; for, if we wish to draw the diameter of a circle which has its extremity at a given point of the circumference we have to join the latter point to the centre (Post. 1) and then to *produce* the straight line so drawn till it meets the circle again (Post. 2), and it is necessary for the proof that the produced part shall be *unique*.

:person:`Saccheri <saccheri>` adopted the proper order when he gave, first the proposition that two straight lines cannot have a common segment, and after that the proposition that any diameter of a circle bisects the circle and its circumference.

Saccheri's proof of the former is very interesting as showing the thoroughness of his method, if not at the end entirely convincing. It is in five stages which I shall indicate shortly, giving the full argument of the first only.

Suppose, if possible, that :g:`AX <0211.g2:/ A X />` is a common segment of both the straight lines :g:`AXB <0211.g2:/ A X B />`, :g:`AXC <0211.g2:/ A X C />`, in one plane, produced beyond :g:`X <0211.g2:X>`. Then describe about :g:`X <0211.g2:X>` as centre, with radius :g:`XB <0211.g2:/ X B />` or :g:`XC <0211.g2:/ X C />`, the arc :g:`BMC <0211.g2:( B M C )>`, and draw through :g:`X <0211.g2:X>` to any point on it the straight line :g:`XM <0211.g2:/ X M />`.

.. picture:: 0211.g2.svg
   :alt: Saccheri's proof that two straight lines cannot have a common segment

(i) I maintain that, with the assumption made, *the line* :g:`AXM <0211.g2:/ A X M />` *is also a straight line which is drawn from the point* :g:`A <0211.g2:A>` *to the point* :g:`X <0211.g2:X>` *and produced beyond* :g:`X <0211.g2:X>`.

For, if this line were not straight, we could draw another straight line :g:`AM <0211.g2:/ A M />` which for its part would be straight. This straight line will either (*a*) cut one of the two straight lines :g:`XB <0211.g2:/ X B />`, :g:`XC <0211.g2:/ X C />` in a certain point :g:`K <0211.g2:K>` or (*b*) enclose one of them, for instance :g:`XB <0211.g2:/ X B />`, in the area bounded by :g:`AX <0211.g2:/ A X />`, :g:`XM <0211.g2:/ X M />` and :g:`APLM <0211.g2:/ A P L M />`.

.. _folio-i-0212:

.. folio:: 0212
   :page: 198

But the first alternative (*a*) obviously contradicts the foregoing lemma [that two straight lines cannot enclose a space], since in that case the two lines :g:`AXK <0211.g2:/ A X K />`, :g:`ATK <0211.g2:/ A T K />`, which by hypothesis are straight, would enclose a space.

The second possibility (*b*) is at once seen to involve a similar absurdity. For the straight line :g:`XB <0211.g2:/ X B />` must, when produced beyond :g:`B <0211.g2:B>`, ultimately meet :g:`APLM <0211.g2:/ A P L M />` in a point :g:`L <0211.g2:L>`. Consequently the two lines :g:`AXBL <0211.g2:/ A X B L />`, :g:`APL <0211.g2:/ A P L />`, which by hypothesis are straight, would again enclose a space. If however we were to assume that the straight line :g:`XB <0211.g2:/ X B />` produced beyond :g:`B <0211.g2:B>` will ultimately meet either the straight line :g:`XM <0211.g2:/ X M />` or the straight line :g:`XA <0211.g2:/ X A />` in another point, we should in the same way arrive at a contradiction.

From this it obviously follows that, on the assumption made, the line :g:`AXM <0211.g2:/ A X M />` is itself the straight line which was drawn from the point :g:`A <0211.g2:A>` to the point :g:`M <0211.g2:M>`; and that is what was maintained.

The remaining stages are in substance these.

(ii) *If the straight line* :g:`AXB <0211.g2:/ A X B />`, *regarded as rigid, revolves about* :g:`AX <0211.g2:/ A X />` *as axis, it cannot assume two more positions in the same plane, so that, for example, in one position* :g:`XB <0211.g2:/ X B />` *should coincide with* :g:`XC <0211.g2:/ X C />`, *and in the other with* :g:`XM <0211.g2:/ X M />`.

[This is proved by considerations of symmetry. :g:`AXB <0211.g2:/ A X B />` cannot be altogether "similar or equal to" :g:`AXC <0211.g2:/ A X C />`, if viewed from the same side (left or right) of both: otherwise they would coincide, which by hypothesis they do not. But there is nothing to prevent :g:`AXB <0211.g2:/ A X B />` viewed from one side (say the left) being "similar or equal to" :g:`AXC <0211.g2:/ A X C />` viewed from the other side (i.e. the right), so that :g:`AXB <0211.g2:/ A X B />` *can*, without any change, be brought into the position :g:`AXC <0211.g2:/ A X C />`.

:g:`AXB <0211.g2:/ A X B />` cannot however take the position of the other straight line :g:`AXM <0211.g2:/ A X M />` as well. If they were like on one side, they would coincide; if they were like on opposite sides, :g:`AXM <0211.g2:/ A X M />`, :g:`AXC <0211.g2:/ A X C />` would be like on the same side and therefore coincide.]

(iii) The other positions of :g:`AXB <0211.g2:/ A X B />` during the revolution must be above or below the original plane.

(iv) It is next maintained that *there is a point* :g:`D <0211.g2:D>` *on the arc* :g:`BC <0211.g2:( B C )>` *such that, if* :g:`XD <0211.g2:/ X D />` *is drawn*, :g:`AXD <0211.g2:/ A X D />` *is not only a straight line but is such that viewed from the left side it is exactly "similar or equal" to what it is when viewed from the right side*.

[*First*, it is proved that points :g:`M <0211.g2:M>`, :g:`F <0211.g2:F>` can be found on the arc, corresponding in the same way as :g:`B <0211.g2:B>`, :g:`C <0211.g2:C>` do, but nearer together, and of course :g:`AXM <0211.g2:/ A X M />`, :g:`AXF <0211.g2:/ A X F />` are both straight lines.

*Secondly*, similar corresponding points can be found still nearer together, and so on continually, until either (*a*) we come to *one* point :g:`D <0211.g2:D>` such that :g:`AXD <0211.g2:/ A X D />` *is exactly like itself when the right and left sides are compared*, or (*b*) there are *two* ultimate points of this sort :g:`M <0211.g2:M>`, :g:`F <0211.g2:F>`, so that *both* :g:`AXM <0211.g2:/ A X M />`, :g:`AXF <0211.g2:/ A X F />` *have this property*.

*Thirdly*, (*b*) is ruled out by reference to the definition of a straight line.
Hence (*a*) only is true, and there is only *one* point :g:`D <0211.g2:D>` such as described.]

(v) Lastly, :person:`Saccheri <saccheri>` concludes that the straight line :g:`AXD <0211.g2:/ A X D />` so determined "is *alone* a straight line, and the *immediate* prolongation from :g:`A <0211.g2:A>` beyond :g:`X <0211.g2:X>` to :g:`D <0211.g2:D>`," relying again on the definition of a straight line as "lying evenly."

:person:`Simson <simson>` deduced the proposition that *two straight lines cannot have a common segment* as a corollary from I. 11; but his argument is a complete *petitio principii*, as shown by :person:`Todhunter <todhunter>` in his note on that proposition.

:person:`Proclus <proclus>` (:cite:`p. 217, 10 <proclus:in-euclid:217.10>`) records an ancient proof also based on the proposition I. 11. :person:`Zeno <zeno>`, he says, propounded this proof and then criticised it.

Suppose that two straight lines :g:`AC <0213.g1:/ A C />`, :g:`AD <0213.g1:/ A D />` have a common segment :g:`AB <0213.g1:/ A B />`, and let :g:`BE <0213.g1:/ B E />` be drawn at :eq:`right angles <0213.g1:< E B A ) = 0213.g1:< E B C )>` to :g:`AC <0213.g1:/ A C />`.
Then the angle :g:`EBC <0213.g1:< E B C )>` is :eq:`right <0213.g1:< E B C ) = 0213.g1:< E B A )>`.
If then the angle :g:`EBD <0213.g1:< E B D )>` is also right, the two angles will be :eq:`equal <0213.g1:< E B C ) = 0213.g1:< E B D )>`: which is impossible.
If the angle :g:`EBD <0213.g1:< E B D )>` is not right, draw :g:`BF <0213.g1:/ B F />` at right angles to :g:`AD <0213.g1:/ A D />`; therefore the angle :g:`FBA <0213.g1:< F B A )>` is right.
But the angle :g:`EBA <0213.g1:< E B A )>` is :eq:`right <0213.g1:< E B A ) = 0213.g1:< E B C )>`.
Therefore the angles :g:`EBA <0213.g1:< E B A )>`, :g:`FBA <0213.g1:< F B A )>` are :eq:`equal <0213.g1:< E B A ) = 0213.g1:< F B A )>`: which is impossible.

.. picture:: 0213.g1.svg
   :alt: Zeno and Posidonius on straight lines with a common segment

:person:`Zeno <zeno>` objected to this, says :person:`Proclus <proclus>`, because it assumed the later proposition I. 11 for its proof. :person:`Posidonius <posidonius>` said that there was no trace of such a proof to be found in the text-books of Elements, and that it was only invented by :person:`Zeno <zeno>` for the purpose of slandering contemporary geometers. :person:`Posidonius <posidonius>` maintains further that even this proof has something to be said for it. There must be some straight line at right angles to each of the two straight lines :g:`AC <0213.g1:/ A C />`, :g:`AD <0213.g1:/ A D />` (the very definition of right angles assumes this): "*suppose then it happens to be the straight line we have set up.*" Here then we have an ancient instance of a defence of *hypothetical construction*, but in such apologetic terms ("it is possible to say *something* even for this proof") that we may conclude that in general it would not have been accepted by geometers of that time as a legitimate means of proving a proposition.
:person:`Todhunter <todhunter>` proposed to deduce that *two straight lines cannot have a common segment* from I. 13. But this will not serve either, since, as before mentioned, the assumption is really required for I. 4.
It is best to make it a postulate.
