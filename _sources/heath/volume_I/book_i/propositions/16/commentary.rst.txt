Commentary on Proposition I.16
==============================

Back to :ref:`Proposition I.16 <I.16>`.

.. folio:: 0294
   :page: 280

As is well known, this proposition is not universally true under the :person:`Riemann <riemann>` hypothesis of a space endless in extent but not infinite in size. On this hypothesis a straight line is a “closed series” and returns on itself; and two straight lines which have one point of intersection have another point of intersection also, which bisects the whole length of the straight line measured from the first point on it to the same point again; thus the axiom of Euclidean geometry that two straight lines do not enclose a space does not hold. If $4\Delta$ denotes the finite length of a straight line measured from any point once round to the same point again, $2\Delta$ is the distance between the two intersections of two straight lines which meet. Two points :g:`A <0285.g1:A>`, :g:`B <0285.g1:B>` do not determine one sole straight line unless the distance between them is different from $2\Delta$. In order that there may only be one perpendicular from a point :g:`C <0285.g1:C>` to a straight line :g:`AB <0285.g1:[ A B ]>`, :g:`C <0285.g1:C>` must not be one of the two “poles” of the straight line.

Now, in order that the proof of the present proposition may be universally valid, it is necessary that :g:`CF <0293.g1:/ C F />` should always fall within the angle :g:`ACD <0293.g1:< A C D )>` so that the angle :g:`ACF <0293.g1:< A C F )>` may be less than the angle :g:`ACD <0293.g1:< A C D )>`. But this will not always be so on the :person:`Riemann <riemann>` hypothesis. For, (1) if :g:`BE <0293.g1:/ B E />` is equal to $\Delta$, so that :g:`BF <0293.g1:/ B F />` is equal to $2\Delta$, :g:`F <0293.g1:F>` will be the second point in which :g:`BE <0293.g1:/ B E />` and :g:`BD <0293.g1:/ B D />` intersect; i.e. :g:`F <0293.g1:F>` will lie on :g:`CD <0293.g1:/ C D />`, and the angle :g:`ACF <0293.g1:< A C F )>` will be *equal* to the angle :g:`ACD <0293.g1:< A C D )>`. In this case the exterior angle :g:`ACD <0293.g1:< A C D )>` will be *equal* to the interior angle :g:`BAC <0293.g1:< B A C )>`. (2) If :g:`BE <0293.g1:/ B E />` is greater than $\Delta$ and less than $2\Delta$, so that :g:`BF <0293.g1:/ B F />` is greater than $2\Delta$ and less than $4\Delta$, the angle :g:`ACF <0293.g1:< A C F )>` will be *greater* than the angle :g:`ACD <0293.g1:< A C D )>`, and therefore the angle :g:`ACD <0293.g1:< A C D )>` will be *less* than the interior angle :g:`BAC <0293.g1:< B A C )>`. Thus, e.g., in the particular case of a right-angled triangle, the angles other than the right angle may be (1) both acute, (2) one acute and one obtuse, or (3) both obtuse according as the perpendicular sides are (1) both less than $\Delta$, (2) one less and the other greater than $\Delta$, (3) both greater than $\Delta$.

:person:`Proclus <proclus>` tells us (p. 307, 1—12) that some combined this theorem with the next in *one* enunciation thus: *In any triangle, if one side be produced, the exterior angle of the triangle is greater than either of the interior and opposite angles, and any two of the interior angles are less than two right angles*, the combination having been suggested by the similar enunciation of :person:`Euclid <euclid>` I. 32, *In any triangle, if one of the sides be produced, the exterior angle is equal to the two interior and opposite angles, and the three interior angles of the triangle are equal to two right angles.*

The present proposition enables :person:`Proclus <proclus>` to prove what he did not succeed in establishing conclusively in his note on I. 12, namely that *from one point there cannot be drawn to the same straight line three straight lines equal in length*.

For, if possible, let :g:`AB <0295.g1:/ A B />`, :g:`AC <0295.g1:/ A C />`, :g:`AD <0295.g1:/ A D />` be all :eq:`equal <0295.g1:/ A B / = 0295.g1:/ A C / = 0295.g1:/ A D />`, :g:`B <0295.g1:B>`, :g:`C <0295.g1:C>`, :g:`D <0295.g1:D>` being in a straight line. Then, since :g:`AB <0295.g1:/ A B />`, :g:`AC <0295.g1:/ A C />` are :eq:`equal <0295.g1:/ A B / = 0295.g1:/ A C />`, the angles :g:`ABC <0295.g1:< A B C )>`, :g:`ACB <0295.g1:< A C B )>` are :eq:`equal <0295.g1:< A B C ) = 0295.g1:< A C B )>`. Similarly, since :g:`AB <0295.g1:/ A B />`, :g:`AD <0295.g1:/ A D />` are :eq:`equal <0295.g1:/ A B / = 0295.g1:/ A D />`, the angles :g:`ABD <0295.g1:< A B D )>`, :g:`ADB <0295.g1:< A D B )>` are :eq:`equal <0295.g1:< A B D ) = 0295.g1:< A D B )>`. Therefore the angle :g:`ACB <0295.g1:< A C B )>` is :eq:`equal <0295.g1:< A C B ) = 0295.g1:< A D C )>` to the angle :g:`ADC <0295.g1:< A D C )>`, i.e. the exterior angle to the interior and opposite angle: which is impossible.

.. picture:: 0295.g1.svg
   :alt: Proclus Proof of Impossibility of Three Equal Lines to Same Straight Line

:person:`Proclus <proclus>` next (p. 308, 14 sqq.) undertakes to prove by means of I. 16 that, *if a straight line falling on two straight lines make the exterior angle equal to the interior and opposite angle, the two straight lines will not form a triangle or meet*, for in that case the same angle would be both greater and equal.

The proof is really equivalent to that of Eucl. I. 27. If :g:`BE <0295.g2:/ B E />` falls on the two straight lines :g:`AB <0295.g2:/ A B />`, :g:`CD <0295.g2:/ C D />` in such a way that the angle :g:`CDE <0295.g2:< C D E )>` is :eq:`equal <0295.g2:< C D E ) = 0295.g2:< A B D )>` to the interior and opposite angle :g:`ABD <0295.g2:< A B D )>`, :g:`AB <0295.g2:/ A B />` and :g:`CD <0295.g2:/ C D />` cannot form a triangle or meet. For, if they did, then (by I. 16) the angle :g:`CDE <0295.g2:< C D E )>` would be greater than the angle :g:`ABD <0295.g2:< A B D )>`, while by the hypothesis it is at the same time :eq:`equal <0295.g2:< C D E ) = 0295.g2:< A B D )>` to it.

.. picture:: 0295.g2.svg
   :alt: Proclus Equivalent of I. 27 Figure

Hence, says :person:`Proclus <proclus>`, in order that :g:`BA <0295.g2:/ B A />`, :g:`DC <0295.g2:/ D C />` may form a triangle it is necessary for them to *approach* one another in the sense of being turned round one pair of corresponding extremities, e.g. :g:`B <0295.g2:B>`, :g:`D <0295.g2:D>`, so that the other extremities :g:`A <0295.g2:A>`, :g:`C <0295.g2:C>` come nearer. This may be brought about in one of three ways: (1) :g:`AB <0295.g2:/ A B />` may remain fixed and :g:`CD <0295.g2:/ C D />` be turned about :g:`D <0295.g2:D>` so that the angle :g:`CDE <0295.g2:< C D E )>` increases; (2) :g:`CD <0295.g2:/ C D />` may remain fixed and :g:`AB <0295.g2:/ A B />` be turned about :g:`B <0295.g2:B>` so that the angle :g:`ABD <0295.g2:< A B D )>` becomes smaller; (3) both :g:`AB <0295.g2:/ A B />` and :g:`CD <0295.g2:/ C D />` may move so as to make the angle :g:`ABD <0295.g2:< A B D )>` smaller and the angle :g:`CDE <0295.g2:< C D E )>` larger at the same time. The reason, then, of the straight lines :g:`AB <0295.g2:/ A B />`, :g:`CD <0295.g2:/ C D />` coming to form a triangle or to meet is (says :person:`Proclus <proclus>`) *the movement of the straight lines*.

Though he does not mention it here, :person:`Proclus <proclus>` does in another passage (p. 371, 2—10, quoted on p. 207 above) hint at the possibility that, while I. 16 may remain universally true, either of the straight lines :g:`BA <0295.g2:/ B A />`, :g:`DC <0295.g2:/ D C />` (or both together) may be turned through any angle not greater than a certain finite angle and yet may not meet (the Bolyai-Lobachewsky hypothesis).
