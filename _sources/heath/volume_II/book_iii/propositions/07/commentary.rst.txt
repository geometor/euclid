Commentary on Proposition III.7
===============================

Back to :ref:`Proposition III.7 <III.7>`.

.. folio:: 0021
   :page: 15

:person:`De Morgan <de-morgan>` points out that there is an unproved assumption in this demonstration. We draw straight lines *from* :g:`$F$ <0021.g1:F>`, as :g:`$FB$ <0021.g1:/ F B />`, :g:`$FC$ <0021.g1:/ F C />`, such that the angle :g:`$DFB$ <0021.g1:< D F B )>` is greater than the angle :g:`$DFC$ <0021.g1:< D F C )>` and then assume, with respect to the straight lines drawn *from the centre* :g:`$E$ <0021.g1:E>` to :g:`$B$ <0021.g1:B>`, :g:`$C$ <0021.g1:C>`, that the angle :g:`$DEB$ <0021.g1:< D E B )>` is greater than the angle :g:`$DEC$ <0021.g1:< D E C )>`. This is most easily proved, I think, by means of the converse of part of the theorem about the lengths of different straight lines drawn to a given straight line from an external point which was mentioned above in the note on III. 2. This converse would be to the effect that, *If two unequal straight lines be drawn from a point to a given straight line which are not perpendicular to the straight line, the greater of the two is the further from the perpendicular from the point to the given straight line.* This can either be proved from its converse by :term:`*reductio ad absurdum* <reductio-ad-absurdum>`, or established directly by means of I. 47. Thus, in the accompanying figure, :g:`$FB$ <0021.g1:/ F B />` must cut :g:`$EC$ <0021.g1:/ E C />` in some point :g:`$M$ <0021.g1:M>`, since the angle :g:`$BFE$ <0021.g1:< B F E )>` is less than the angle :g:`$CFE$ <0021.g1:< C F E )>`.

.. picture:: 0021.g1.svg
   :alt: Proposition 7 Commentary Diagram

Therefore :g:`$EM$ <0021.g1:/ E M />` is less than :g:`$EC$ <0021.g1:/ E C />`, and therefore than :g:`$EB$ <0021.g1:/ E B />`.

.. _folio-ii-0022:

.. folio:: 0022
   :page: 16

Hence the point :g:`$B$ <0021.g1:B>` in which :g:`$FB$ <0021.g1:/ F B />` meets the circle is further from the foot of the perpendicular from :g:`$E$ <0021.g1:E>` on :g:`$FB$ <0021.g1:/ F B />` than :g:`$M$ <0021.g1:M>` is;

.. assertion::

   therefore the angle :g:`$BEF$ <0021.g1:< B E F )>` is greater than the angle :g:`$CEF$ <0021.g1:< C E F )>`.

Another way of enunciating the first part of the proposition is that of Mr H. M. Taylor, viz. "Of all straight lines drawn to a circle from an internal point not the centre, the one which passes through the centre is the greatest, and the one which when produced passes through the centre is the least; and of any two others the one which *subtends the greater angle at the centre* is the greater." The substitution of the *angle subtended at the centre* as the criterion no doubt has the effect of avoiding the necessity of dealing with the unproved assumption in Euclid's proof referred to above, and the similar substitution in the enunciation of the first part of III. 8 has the effect of avoiding the necessity for dealing with like unproved assumptions in Euclid's proof, as well as the complication caused by the distinction in Euclid's enunciation between lines falling from an external point on the *convex circumference* and on the *concave circumference* of a circle respectively, terms which are not defined but taken as understood.

Mr Nixon (:cite:`*Euclid Revised* <nixon:euclid-revised>`) similarly substitutes as the criterion the angle subtended at the centre, but gives as his reason that the words "nearer" and "more remote" in Euclid's enunciation are scarcely clear enough without some definition of the sense in which they are used. Smith and Bryant make the substitution in III. 8, but follow Euclid in III. 7.

On the whole, I think that Euclid's plan of taking straight lines drawn from the point which is not the centre direct to the circumference and making greater or less angles *at that point* with the straight line containing it and the centre is the more instructive and useful of the two, since it is such lines drawn in any manner to the circle from the point which are immediately useful in the proofs of later propositions or in resolving difficulties connected with those proofs.

:person:`Heron <heron>` again (:person:`an-Nairīzī <an-nairizi>`, ed. Curtze, pp. 114—5) has a note on this proposition which is curious. He first of all says that Euclid proves that lines *nearer the centre* are greater than those more remote *from it*. This is a different view of the question from that taken in Euclid's proposition as we have it, in which the lines are not nearer to and more remote from the *centre* but from *the line through the centre*. Euclid takes lines inclined to the latter line at a greater or less angle; :person:`Heron <heron>` introduces distance *from the centre* in the sense of Deff. 4, 5, i.e. in the sense of *the length of the perpendicular* drawn to the line from the centre, which Euclid does not use till III. 14, 15. :person:`Heron <heron>` then observes that in Euclid's proposition the lines compared are all drawn on one side of the line through the centre, and sets himself to prove the same truth of lines on *opposite* sides which are more or less distant *from the centre*. The new point of view necessitates a quite different line of proof, anticipating the methods of later propositions.

The first case taken by :person:`Heron <heron>` is that of two straight lines such that the perpendiculars from the centre on them fall on the lines themselves and not in either case on the line produced.

.. picture:: 0022.g1.svg
   :alt: Heron Case 1 Diagram

Let :g:`$A$ <0022.g1:A>` be the given point, :g:`$D$ <0022.g1:D>` the centre, and let :g:`$AE$ <0022.g1:/ A E />` be nearer the centre than :g:`$AF$ <0022.g1:/ A F />`, so that the perpendicular :g:`$DG$ <0022.g1:/ D G />` on :g:`$AE$ <0022.g1:/ A E />` is less than the perpendicular :g:`$DH$ <0022.g1:/ D H />` on :g:`$AF$ <0022.g1:/ A F />`.

.. container:: center

   Then sqs. on :g:`$DG$ <0022.g1:/ D G />`, :g:`$GE$ <0022.g1:/ G E />` = sqs. on :g:`$DH$ <0022.g1:/ D H />`, :g:`$HF$ <0022.g1:/ H F />`, and sqs. on :g:`$DG$ <0022.g1:/ D G />`, :g:`$GA$ <0022.g1:/ G A />` = sqs. on :g:`$DH$ <0022.g1:/ D H />`, :g:`$HA$ <0022.g1:/ H A />`. But sq. on :g:`$DG$ <0022.g1:/ D G />` < sq. on :g:`$DH$ <0022.g1:/ D H />`.

.. _folio-ii-0023:

.. folio:: 0023
   :page: 17

.. container:: center

   Therefore sq. on :g:`$GE$ <0023.g1:/ G E />` > sq. on :g:`$HF$ <0023.g1:/ H F />`, and sq. on :g:`$GA$ <0023.g1:/ G A />` > sq. on :g:`$HA$ <0023.g1:/ H A />`, whence :g:`$GE$ <0023.g1:/ G E />` > :g:`$HF$ <0023.g1:/ H F />`, :g:`$GA$ <0023.g1:/ G A />` > :g:`$HA$ <0023.g1:/ H A />`.

Therefore, by addition, :g:`$AE$ <0023.g1:/ A E />` > :g:`$AF$ <0023.g1:/ A F />`.

The other case taken by :person:`Heron <heron>` is that where one perpendicular falls on the line produced, as in the annexed figure. In this case we prove in like manner that

.. container:: center

   :g:`$GE$ <0023.g1:/ G E />` > :g:`$HF$ <0023.g1:/ H F />`, and :g:`$GA$ <0023.g1:/ G A />` > :g:`$AH$ <0023.g1:/ A H />`.

.. picture:: 0023.g1.svg
   :alt: Heron Case 2 Diagram

Thus :g:`$AE$ <0023.g1:/ A E />` is greater than the sum of :g:`$HF$ <0023.g1:/ H F />`, :g:`$AH$ <0023.g1:/ A H />`, whence, :term:`*a fortiori* <a-fortiori>`, :g:`$AE$ <0023.g1:/ A E />` is greater than the difference of :g:`$HF$ <0023.g1:/ H F />`, :g:`$AH$ <0023.g1:/ A H />`, i.e. than :g:`$AF$ <0023.g1:/ A F />`.

:person:`Heron <heron>` does not give the third possible case, that, namely, where *both* perpendiculars fall on the lines produced. The fact is that, in this case, the foregoing method breaks down. Though :g:`$AE$ <0023.g2:/ A E />` be nearer to the centre than :g:`$AF$ <0023.g2:/ A F />` in the sense that :g:`$DG$ <0023.g2:/ D G />` is less than :g:`$DH$ <0023.g2:/ D H />`,

.. assertion::

   :g:`$AE$ <0023.g2:/ A E />` is not greater but *less* than :g:`$AF$ <0023.g2:/ A F />`.

.. picture:: 0023.g2.svg
   :alt: Heron Case 3 Diagram

Moreover this cannot be proved by the same method as before.
For, while we can prove that

.. container:: center

   :g:`$GE$ <0023.g2:/ G E />` > :g:`$HF$ <0023.g2:/ H F />`, :g:`$GA$ <0023.g2:/ G A />` > :g:`$AH$ <0023.g2:/ A H />`,

we cannot make any inference as to the comparative length of :g:`$AE$ <0023.g2:/ A E />`, :g:`$AF$ <0023.g2:/ A F />`.

To judge by :person:`Heron <heron>`'s corresponding note to III. 8, he would, to prove this case, practically prove III. 35 first, i.e. prove that, if :g:`$EA$ <0023.g2:/ E A />` be produced to :g:`$K$ <0023.g2:K>` and :g:`$FA$ <0023.g2:/ F A />` to :g:`$L$ <0023.g2:L>`,

.. assertion::

   rect. :g:`$FA$ <0023.g2:/ F A />`, :g:`$AL$ <0023.g2:/ A L />` = rect. :g:`$EA$ <0023.g2:/ E A />`, :g:`$AK$ <0023.g2:/ A K />`,

from which he would infer that, since :g:`$AK$ <0023.g2:/ A K />` > :g:`$AL$ <0023.g2:/ A L />` by the first case,

.. assertion::

   :g:`$AE$ <0023.g2:/ A E />` < :g:`$AF$ <0023.g2:/ A F />`.

An excellent moral can, I think, be drawn from the note of :person:`Heron <heron>`. Having the appearance of supplementing, or giving an alternative for, Euclid's proposition, it cannot be said to do more than confuse the subject. Nor was it necessary to find a new proof for the case where the two lines which are compared are on *opposite* sides of the diameter, since Euclid shows that for each line from the point to the circumference on one side of the diameter there is another of the same length equally inclined to it on the other side.
