Commentary on Book V Definition 5
=================================

Back to :ref:`Book V Definitions <V.def>`.

**Ἐν τῷ αὐτῷ λόγῳ μεγέθη λέγεται εἶναι πρῶτον πρὸς δεύτερον καὶ τρίτον πρὸς τέταρτον, ὅταν τὰ τοῦ πρώτου καὶ τρίτου ἰσάκις πολλαπλάσια τῶν τοῦ δευτέρου καὶ τετάρτου ἰσάκις πολλαπλασίων καθ᾽ ὁποιονοῦν πολλαπλασιασμὸν ἑκάτερον ἑκατέρου ἢ ἅμα ὑπερέχῃ ἢ ἅμα ἴσα ἢ ἅμα ἐλλείπῃ ληφθέντα κατάλληλα.**

In my translation of this definition I have compromised between an attempted literal translation and the more expanded version of :person:`Simson <simson>`. The difficulty in the way of an exactly literal translation is due to the fact that the words (καθ᾽ ὁποιονοῦν πολλαπλασιασμὸν) signifying that the equimultiples *in each case* are any equimultiples *whatever* occur only once in the Greek, though they apply *both* to τὰ... :term:`ἰσάκις <isakis>` :term:`πολλαπλάσια <multiple>` in the nominative and τῶν... :term:`ἰσάκις <isakis>` πολλαπλασίων in the genitive. I have preferred "alike" to "simultaneously" as a translation of ἅμα because "simultaneously" might suggest that time was of the essence of the matter, whereas what is meant is that any particular comparison made between the equimultiples must be made between *the same* equimultiples of the two pairs respectively, not that they need to be compared *at the same time*.

:person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` has an allusion to a definition of "the same ratio" in :cite:`*Topics* VIII. 3, 158 b 29 <aristotle:topics:viii-3-158-b-29>`: "In mathematics too some things appear to be not easy to prove (γράφεσθαι) for want of a definition, e.g. that the parallel to the side which cuts a plane [a parallelogram] divides the straight line [the other side] and the area similarly. But, when the definition is expressed, the said property is immediately manifest; for the areas and the straight lines *have the same ἀνταναίρεσις, and this is the definition of 'the same ratio.'"* Upon this passage Alexander says similarly, "This is the definition of proportionals which the ancients used: *magnitudes are proportional to one another which have* (or show) *the same ἀνθυφαίρεσις*, and :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` has called the latter :term:`ἀνταναίρεσις <antanairesis>`." :person:`Heiberg <heiberg>` (:cite:`*Mathematisches zu Aristoteles* <heiberg:mathematisches-zu-aristoteles>`, p. 22) thinks that :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` is alluding to the fact that the proposition referred to could not be rigorously proved so long as the Pythagorean definition applicable to commensurable magnitudes only was adhered to, and is quoting the definition belonging to the complete theory of :person:`Eudoxus <eudoxus>`; whence, in view of the positive statement of :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` that the definition quoted *is* the definition of "the same ratio," it would appear that the Euclidean definition (which :person:`Heiberg <heiberg>` describes as a careful and exact paraphrase of :term:`ἀνταναίρεσις <antanairesis>`) is Euclid's own. I do not
feel able to subscribe to this view, which seems to me to involve very grave difficulties. The Euclidean definition is regularly appealed to in Book V. as the criterion of magnitudes being in proportion, and the use of it would appear to constitute the whole essence of the new general theory of proportion; if then this theory is due to :person:`Eudoxus <eudoxus>`, it seems impossible to believe that the definition was not also due to him. Certainly the definition given by :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` would be no substitute for it; :term:`ἀνθυφαίρεσις <anthyphairesis>` and :term:`ἀνταναίρεσις <antanairesis>` are words almost as vague and "metaphysical" (as :person:`Barrow <barrow>` would say) as the words used to define *ratio*, and it is difficult to see how any mathematical facts could be deduced from such a definition. Consider for a moment the etymology of the words. ὑφαίρεσις or ἀναίρεσις means "removal," "taking away" or "destruction" of a thing; and the prefix ἀντὶ indicates that the "taking away" from one magnitude *answers to*, corresponds with, alternates with, the "taking away" from the other. So far therefore as the etymology goes, the word seems rather to suggest the "taking away" of corresponding *fractions*, and therefore to suit the old imperfect theory of proportion rather than the new one. Thus Waitz (*ad loc.*) paraphrases the definition as meaning that "as many parts as are taken from one magnitude, so many are at the same time taken from the other as well." A possible explanation would seem to be that, though :person:`Eudoxus <eudoxus>` had formulated the new definition, the old one was still current in the text-books of :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>`'s time, and was taken by him as being a good enough illustration of what he wished to bring out in the passage of the *Topics* referred to.

From the revival of learning in Europe onwards the Euclidean definition of proportion was the subject of much criticism. :person:`Campanus <campanus>` had failed to understand it, had in fact misinterpreted it altogether, and he may have misled others such as :person:`Ramus <ramus>` (:math:`1515`—:math:`72`), always a violently hostile critic of Euclid. Among the objectors to it was no less a person than Galileo. For particulars of the controversies on the subject down to Thomas Simpson (*Elem. of Geometry*, Lond. :math:`1800`) the reader is referred to the Excursus at the end of the second volume of :person:`Camerer <camerer>`'s Euclid (:math:`1825`). For us it is interesting to note that the unsoundness of the usual criticisms of the definition was never better exposed than by :person:`Barrow <barrow>`. Some of the objections, he pointed out (:cite:`*Lect. Cantabr.* <barrow:lectiones-cantabrigienses>` VII. of :math:`1666`), are due to misconception on the part of their authors as to the nature of a definition. Thus Euclid is required by these objectors (e.g. :person:`Tacquet <tacquet>`) to do the impossible and to show that what is predicated in the definition is true of the thing defined, as if any one should be required to show that the name "circle" was applicable to those figures alone which have their radii all equal! As we are entitled to assign to such figures and such figures only the name of "circle," so Euclid is entitled ("quamvis non temere nec imprudenter at certis de causis iustis illis et idoneis") to describe a certain property which four magnitudes may have, and to call magnitudes possessing that property magnitudes "in the same ratio." Others had argued from the occurrence of the other definition of proportion in VII. Def. 20 that Euclid was dissatisfied with the present one; :person:`Barrow <barrow>` pointed out that, on the contrary, it was the fact that VII. Def. 20 was not adequate to cover the case of incommensurables which made Euclid adopt the present definition here. Lastly, he maintains, against those who descant on the "obscurity" of V. Def. 5, that the supposed obscurity is due, partly no doubt to the inherent difficulty of the subject of incommensurables, but also to faulty translators, and most of all to lack of effort in the learner to grasp thoroughly the meaning of words which, in themselves, are as clearly expressed as they could be.

To come now to the merits of the case, the best defence and explanation
of the definition that I have seen is that given by :person:`De Morgan <de-morgan>`. He first translates it, observes that it applies equally to commensurable or incommensurable quantities because no attempt is made to measure one by an aliquot part of another, and then proceeds thus.

.. _folio-ii-0128:

.. folio:: 0128
   :page: 122

"The two questions which must be asked, and satisfactorily answered, previously to its [the definition's] reception, are as follows:

1. What right had Euclid, or any one else, to expect that the preceding most prolix and unwieldy statement should be received by the beginner as the definition of a relation the perception of which is one of the most common acts of his mind, since it is performed on every occasion where similarity or dissimilarity of figure is looked for or presents itself?

2. If the preceding question should be clearly answered, how can the definition of proportion ever be used; or how is it possible to compare every one of the infinite number of multiples of :math:`A` with every one of the multiples of :math:`B`?

To the first question we reply that not only is the test proposed by Euclid tolerably simple, when more closely examined, but that it is, or might be made to appear, an easy and natural consequence of those fundamental perceptions with which it may at first seem difficult to compare it."

To elucidate this :person:`De Morgan <de-morgan>` gives the following illustration.

Suppose there is a straight colonnade composed of equidistant columns (which we will understand to mean the vertical lines forming the axes of the columns respectively), the first of which is at a distance from a bounding wall equal to the distance between consecutive columns. In front of the colonnade let there be a straight row of equidistant railings (regarded as meaning their axes), the first being at a distance from the bounding wall equal to the distance between consecutive railings. Let the columns be numbered from the wall, and also the railings. We suppose of course that the column distance (say, :g:`C <0128.g1:C>`) and the railing distance (say, :g:`R <0128.g1:R>`) are different and that they may bear to each other any ratio, commensurable or incommensurable; i.e. that there need not go any exact number of railings to any exact number of columns.

.. picture:: 0128.g1.svg
   :alt: Folio 0128 Figure 1

If the construction be supposed carried on to any extent, a spectator can, by mere inspection, and without measurement, compare :g:`C <0128.g1:C>` with :g:`R <0128.g1:R>` to any degree of accuracy. For example, since the 10th railing falls between the 4th and 5th columns, :g:`10R <0128.g1:10R>` is greater than :g:`4C <0128.g1:4C>` and less than :g:`5C <0128.g1:5C>`, and therefore :g:`R <0128.g1:R>` lies between :math:`\frac{4}{10}\text{ths}` of :g:`C <0128.g1:C>` and :math:`\frac{5}{10}\text{ths}` of :g:`C <0128.g1:C>`. To get a more accurate notion, the ten-thousandth railing may be taken; suppose it falls between the 4674th and 4675th columns. Therefore :math:`10,000R` lies between :math:`4674C` and :math:`4675C`, or :g:`R <0128.g1:R>` lies between :math:`\frac{4674}{10000}` and :math:`\frac{4675}{10000}` of :g:`C <0128.g1:C>`. There is no limit to the degree of accuracy thus obtainable; and the ratio of :g:`R <0128.g1:R>` to :g:`C <0128.g1:C>` is determined when the order of distribution of the railings among the columns is assigned *ad infinitum*; or, in other words, when the position of *any* given railing can be found, as to the numbers of the columns between which it lies. Any alteration, however small, in the place of the first railing must at last affect the order of distribution. Suppose e.g. that the first railing is moved from the wall by one part in a thousand of the distance between the columns; then the second railing is pushed forward by :math:`\frac{2}{1000}C`, the third by :math:`\frac{3}{1000}C`, and so on, so that
the railings after the thousandth are pushed forward by more than :g:`C <0128.g1:C>`; i.e. the order with respect to the columns is disarranged.

.. _folio-ii-0129:

.. folio:: 0129
   :page: 123

Now let it be proposed to make a model of the preceding construction in which :math:`c` shall be the column distance and :math:`r` the railing distance. It needs no definition of proportion, nor anything more than the conception which we have of that term prior to definition (and with which we must show the agreement of any definition that we may adopt), to assure us that :g:`C <0128.g1:C>` must be to :g:`R <0128.g1:R>` in the same proportion as :math:`c` to :math:`r` if the model be truly formed. Nor is it drawing too largely on that conception of proportion to assert that the distribution of the railings among the columns in the model must be everywhere the same as in the original; for example, that the model would be *out of proportion* if its 37th railing fell between the 18th and 19th columns, while the 37th railing of the original fell between the 17th and 18th columns. Thus the dependence of Euclid's definition upon common notions is settled; for the obvious relation between the construction and its model which has just been described contains the collection of conditions, the fulfilment of which, according to Euclid, constitutes proportion. According to Euclid, whenever :math:`mC` exceeds, equals, or falls short of :math:`nR`, then :math:`mc` must exceed, equal, or fall short of :math:`nr`; and, by the most obvious property of the constructions, according as the :math:`m`\ th column comes after, opposite to, or before the :math:`n`\ th railing in the original, the :math:`m`\ th column must come after, opposite to, or before the :math:`n`\ th railing in the correct model.

Thus the test proposed by Euclid is necessary. It is also sufficient. For admitting that, to a given original with a given column-distance in the model, there is one correct model railing distance (which must therefore be that which distributes the railings among the columns as in the original), we have seen that any other railing distance, however slightly different, would at last give a different distribution; that is, the correct distance, and the correct distance only, satisfies all the conditions required by Euclid's definition.

The use of the word *distribution* having been well learnt, says :person:`De Morgan <de-morgan>`, the following way of stating the definition will be found easier than that of Euclid. "Four magnitudes, :math:`A` and :math:`B` of one kind, and :math:`C` and :math:`D` of the same or another kind, are proportional when all the multiples of :math:`A` can be distributed among the multiples of :math:`B` in the same intervals as the corresponding multiples of :math:`C` among those of :math:`D`." Or, whatever numbers :math:`m, n` may be, if :math:`mA` lies between :math:`nB` and :math:`(n+1)B`, :math:`mC` lies between :math:`nD` and :math:`(n+1)D`.

It is important to note that, if the test be always satisfied from and after any given multiples of :math:`A` and :math:`C`, it must be satisfied before those multiples. For instance, let the test be always satisfied from and after :math:`100A` and :math:`100C`; and let :math:`5A` and :math:`5C` be instances for examination. Take any multiple of :math:`5` which will exceed :math:`100`, say :math:`50` times five; and let it be found on examination that :math:`250A` lies between :math:`678B` and :math:`679B`; then :math:`250C` lies between :math:`678D` and :math:`679D`. Divide by :math:`50`, and it follows that :math:`5A` lies between :math:`13\frac{28}{50}B` and :math:`13\frac{29}{50}B`, and :term:`*a fortiori* <a-fortiori>` between :math:`13B` and :math:`14B`. Similarly, :math:`5C` lies between :math:`13\frac{28}{50}D` and :math:`13\frac{29}{50}D`, and therefore between :math:`13D` and :math:`14D`. Or :math:`5A` lies in the same interval among the multiples of :math:`B` in which :math:`5C` lies among the multiples of :math:`D`. And so for any multiple of :math:`A, C` less than :math:`100A, 100C`.

There remains the second question relating to the infinite character of the definition; four magnitudes :math:`A, B, C, D` are not to be called proportional until it is shown that *every* multiple of :math:`A` falls in the same intervals among the multiples of :math:`B` in which the same multiple of :math:`C` is found among the multiples of :math:`D`. Suppose that the distribution of the railings among the
columns should be found to agree in the model and the original as far as the millionth railing. This proves only that the railing distance of the model does not err by the millionth part of the corresponding column distance. We can thus fix limits to the disproportion, if any, and we may make those limits as small as we please, by carrying on the method of *observation*; but we cannot *observe* an infinite number of cases and so enable ourselves to affirm proportion absolutely. Mathematical methods however enable us to avoid the difficulty. We can take *any multiples whatever* and work with them as if they were particular multiples. :person:`De Morgan <de-morgan>` gives, as an instance to show that the definition of proportion can in practice be used, notwithstanding its infinite character, the following proof of a proposition to the same effect as Eucl. VI. 2.

.. _folio-ii-0130:

.. folio:: 0130
   :page: 124

.. picture:: 0130.g1.svg
   :alt: Folio 0130 Figure 1

"Let :g:`OAB <0130.g1:< O A B >>` be a triangle to one side :g:`AB <0130.g1:/ A B />` of which :g:`ab <0130.g1:/ a b />` is drawn parallel, and on :g:`OA <0130.g1:[ O A ]>` produced set off :g:`AA_2 <0130.g1:/ A A_2 />`, :g:`A_2A_3 <0130.g1:/ A_2 A_3 />` etc. :eq:`equal <0130.g1:/ A A_2 / = 0130.g1:/ A_2 A_3 / = 0130.g1:/ O A />` to :g:`OA <0130.g1:/ O A />`, and :g:`aa_2 <0130.g1:/ a a_2 />`, :g:`a_2a_3 <0130.g1:/ a_2 a_3 />` etc. :eq:`equal <0130.g1:/ a a_2 / = 0130.g1:/ a_2 a_3 / = 0130.g1:/ O a />` to :g:`Oa <0130.g1:/ O a />`.

Through every one of the points so obtained draw parallels to :g:`AB <0130.g1:/ A B />`, meeting :g:`OB <0130.g1:[ O B ]>` produced in :g:`b_2 <0130.g1:b_2>`, :g:`B_2 <0130.g1:B_2>` etc.

Then it is easily proved that :g:`bb_2 <0130.g1:/ b b_2 />`, :g:`b_2b_3 <0130.g1:/ b_2 b_3 />` etc. are severally :eq:`equal <0130.g1:/ b b_2 / = 0130.g1:/ b_2 b_3 / = 0130.g1:/ O b />; 0130.g1:/ B B_2 / = 0130.g1:/ B_2 B_3 / = 0130.g1:/ O B />` to :g:`Ob <0130.g1:/ O b />`, and :g:`BB_2 <0130.g1:/ B B_2 />`, :g:`B_2B_3 <0130.g1:/ B_2 B_3 />` etc. to :g:`OB <0130.g1:/ O B />`.

Consequently a distribution of the multiples of :g:`OA <0130.g1:/ O A />` among the multiples of :g:`Oa <0130.g1:/ O a />` is made on one line, and of :g:`OB <0130.g1:/ O B />` among those of :g:`Ob <0130.g1:/ O b />` on the other.

The examination of this distribution in all its extent (which is impossible, and hence the apparent difficulty of using the definition) is rendered unnecessary by the known property of parallel lines. For, since :g:`A_3 <0130.g1:A_3>` lies between :g:`a_3 <0130.g1:a_3>` and :g:`a_4 <0130.g1:a_4>`, :g:`B_3 <0130.g1:B_3>` must lie between :g:`b_3 <0130.g1:b_3>` and :g:`b_4 <0130.g1:b_4>`; for, if not, the line :g:`A_3B_3 <0130.g1:/ A_3 B_3 />` would cut either :g:`a_3b_3 <0130.g1:/ a_3 b_3 />` or :g:`a_4b_4 <0130.g1:/ a_4 b_4 />`.

Hence, without inquiring where :math:`A_m` *does* fall, we know that, if it fall between :math:`a_n` and :math:`a_{n+1}`, :math:`B_m` must fall between :math:`b_n` and :math:`b_{n+1}`; or, if :math:`m \cdot OA` fall in magnitude between :math:`n \cdot Oa` and :math:`(n+1)Oa`, then :math:`m \cdot OB` must fall between :math:`n \cdot Ob` and :math:`(n+1)Ob`."

:person:`Max Simon <simon>` remarks (:cite:`*Euclid und die sechs planimetrischen Bücher* <simon:euclid-1901>`, p. 110), after :person:`Zeuthen <zeuthen>`, that Euclid's definition of equal ratios is word for word the same as :person:`Weierstrass <weierstrass>`' definition of equal numbers. So far from agreeing in the usual view that the Greeks saw in the irrational no *number*, Simon thinks it is clear from Eucl. V. that they possessed a notion of number in all its generality as clearly defined as, nay almost identical with, :person:`Weierstrass <weierstrass>`' conception of it.

Certain it is that there is an exact correspondence, almost coincidence, between Euclid's definition of equal ratios and the modern theory of irrationals due to :person:`Dedekind <dedekind>`. Premising the ordinal arrangement of natural numbers in ascending order, then enlarging the sphere of numbers by including (1) negative numbers as well as positive, (2) fractions, as :math:`a/b`, where :math:`a, b` may
be any natural numbers, provided that :math:`b` is not zero, and arranging the fractions ordinally among the other numbers according to the definition :

.. _folio-ii-0131:

.. folio:: 0131
   :page: 125

.. math::

   \text{let } \frac{a}{b} \text{ be } < = > \frac{c}{d} \text{ according as } ad \text{ is } < = > bc,

:person:`Dedekind <dedekind>` arrives at the following definition of an irrational number.

An irrational number :math:`\alpha` is defined whenever a law is stated which will assign every given rational number to one and only one of two classes :math:`A` and :math:`B` such that (1) every number in :math:`A` precedes every number in :math:`B`, and (2) there is no last number in :math:`A` and no first number in :math:`B`; the definition of :math:`\alpha` being that it is the one number which lies between all numbers in :math:`A` and all numbers in :math:`B`.

Now let :math:`x/y` and :math:`x'/y'` be equal ratios in Euclid's sense.

Then :math:`\frac{x}{y}` will divide all rational numbers into two groups :math:`A` and :math:`B`;

:math:`\frac{x'}{y'}` ,, ,, ,, :math:`A'` and :math:`B'`.

Let :math:`\frac{a}{b}` be any rational number in :math:`A`, so that

.. math::

   \frac{a}{b} < \frac{x}{y}.

This means that :math:`ay < bx`.

But Euclid's definition asserts that in that case :math:`ay' < bx'` also.

Hence also

.. math::

   \frac{a}{b} < \frac{x'}{y'};

therefore every member of group :math:`A` is also a member of group :math:`A'`.

Similarly every member of group :math:`B` is a member of group :math:`B'`.

For, if :math:`\frac{a}{b}` belong to :math:`B`,

.. math::

   \frac{a}{b} > \frac{x}{y},

which means that :math:`ay > bx`.

But in that case, by Euclid's definition, :math:`ay' > bx'`;

therefore also

.. math::

   \frac{a}{b} > \frac{x'}{y'}.

Thus, in other words, :math:`A` and :math:`B` are coextensive with :math:`A'` and :math:`B'` respectively ;

therefore :math:`\frac{x}{y} = \frac{x'}{y'}`, according to :person:`Dedekind <dedekind>`, as well as according to Euclid.

If :math:`x/y`, :math:`x'/y'` happen to be *rational*,
then one of the groups, say :math:`A`, includes :math:`x/y`,
and one of the groups, say :math:`A'`, includes :math:`x'/y'`.

In this case :math:`\frac{a}{b}` might *coincide* with :math:`\frac{x}{y}`;

that is

.. math::

   \frac{a}{b} = \frac{x}{y},

which means that

.. math::

   ay = bx.

.. _folio-ii-0132:

.. folio:: 0132
   :page: 126

Therefore, by Euclid's definition, :math:`ay' = bx'` ;

so that

.. math::

   \frac{a}{b} = \frac{x'}{y'}.

Thus the groups are again coextensive.

In a word, Euclid's definition divides all rational numbers into two *coextensive* classes, and therefore defines equal ratios in a manner exactly corresponding to :person:`Dedekind <dedekind>`'s theory.

Alternatives for Eucl. V. Def. 5.
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

:person:`Saccheri <saccheri>` records in his :cite:`*Euclides ob omni naevo vindicatus* <saccheri:euclides-vindicatus>` that a distinguished geometer of his acquaintance proposed to substitute for Euclid's the following definition :

"A first magnitude has to a second the same ratio that a third has to a fourth when the first contains the aliquot parts of the second, *according to any number* [i.e. with any denominator] *whatever*, the same number of times as the number of times the third contains the same aliquot parts of the fourth" ; on which :person:`Saccheri <saccheri>` remarks that he sees no advantage in this definition, which presupposes the notion of *division*, over that of Euclid which uses *multiplication* and the notions of *greater*, *equal*, and *less*.

This definition was, however, practically adopted by Faifofer (:cite:`*Elementi di geometria* <de-paolis:elementi-di-geometria>`, 3 ed., 1882) in the following form :

"Four magnitudes taken in a certain order form a proportion when, by measuring the first and the third respectively by any equi-submultiples whatever of the second and of the fourth, equal quotients are obtained."

:person:`Ingrami <ingrami>` (:cite:`*Elementi di geometria* <de-paolis:elementi-di-geometria>`, 1904) takes multiples of the first and third instead of submultiples of the second and fourth :

"Given four magnitudes in predetermined order, the first two homogeneous with one another, and likewise also the last two, the magnitudes are said to form a proportion (or to be in proportion) when *any multiple* of the first contains the second the same number of times that the equimultiple of the third contains the fourth."

:person:`Veronese <veronese>`'s definition (:cite:`*Elementi di geometria* <de-paolis:elementi-di-geometria>`, Pt. II., 1905) is like that of Faifofer ; :person:`Enriques <enriques>` and Amaldi (:cite:`*Elementi di geometria* <de-paolis:elementi-di-geometria>`, 1905) adhere to Euclid's.

Proportionals of VII. Def. 20 a particular case.
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

It has already been observed that Euclid has nowhere proved (though the fact cannot have escaped him) that the proportion of numbers is included in the proportion of magnitudes as a special case. This is proved by :person:`Simson <simson>` as being necessary to the 5th and 6th propositions of Book X. :person:`Simson <simson>`'s proof is contained in his propositions C and D inserted in the text of Book V. and in the notes thereon. Proposition C and the note on it prove that, *if four magnitudes are proportionals according to* VII. *Def.* 20, *they are also proportionals according to* V. *Def.* 5. Prop. D and the note prove the partial converse, namely that, if four magnitudes are proportionals according to the 5th definition of Book V., *and if the first be any multiple, or any part, or parts, of the second, the third is the same multiple, part, or parts, of the fourth*. The proofs use certain results obtained in Book V.

Prop. C is as follows :

*If the first be the same multiple of the second, or the same part of it, that the third is of the fourth, the first is to the second as the third is to the fourth.*
Let the first :g:`A <0133.g1:A>` be the same *multiple* of :g:`B <0133.g1:B>` the second that :g:`C <0133.g1:C>` the third is of the fourth :g:`D <0133.g1:D>` ;

.. _folio-ii-0133:

.. folio:: 0133
   :page: 127

:g:`A <0133.g1:A>` is to :g:`B <0133.g1:B>` as :g:`C <0133.g1:C>` is to :g:`D <0133.g1:D>`.

.. picture:: 0133.g1.svg
   :alt: Folio 0133 Figure 1

Take of :g:`A <0133.g1:A>`, :g:`C <0133.g1:C>` any equimultiples whatever :g:`E <0133.g1:E>`, :g:`F <0133.g1:F>` ; and of :g:`B <0133.g1:B>`, :g:`D <0133.g1:D>` any equimultiples whatever :g:`G <0133.g1:G>`, :g:`H <0133.g1:H>`.

Then, because :g:`A <0133.g1:A>` is the same multiple of :g:`B <0133.g1:B>` that :g:`C <0133.g1:C>` is of :g:`D <0133.g1:D>`,
and :g:`E <0133.g1:E>` is the same multiple of :g:`A <0133.g1:A>` that :g:`F <0133.g1:F>` is of :g:`C <0133.g1:C>`,
:g:`E <0133.g1:E>` is the same multiple of :g:`B <0133.g1:B>` that :g:`F <0133.g1:F>` is of :g:`D <0133.g1:D>`. [V. 3]

Therefore :g:`E <0133.g1:E>`, :g:`F <0133.g1:F>` are the same multiples of :g:`B <0133.g1:B>`, :g:`D <0133.g1:D>`.

But :g:`G <0133.g1:G>`, :g:`H <0133.g1:H>` are equimultiples of :g:`B <0133.g1:B>`, :g:`D <0133.g1:D>` ;
therefore, if :g:`E <0133.g1:E>` be a greater multiple of :g:`B <0133.g1:B>` than :g:`G <0133.g1:G>` is, :g:`F <0133.g1:F>` is a greater multiple of :g:`D <0133.g1:D>` than :g:`H <0133.g1:H>` is of :g:`D <0133.g1:D>` ;

that is, if :g:`E <0133.g1:E>` be greater than :g:`G <0133.g1:G>`, :g:`F <0133.g1:F>` is greater than :g:`H <0133.g1:H>`.

In like manner,
if :g:`E <0133.g1:E>` be :eq:`equal <0133.g1:E = 0133.g1:G>` to :g:`G <0133.g1:G>`, or less, :g:`F <0133.g1:F>` is :eq:`equal <0133.g1:F = 0133.g1:H>` to :g:`H <0133.g1:H>`, or less than it.

But :g:`E <0133.g1:E>`, :g:`F <0133.g1:F>` are equimultiples, any whatever, of :g:`A <0133.g1:A>`, :g:`C <0133.g1:C>` ;
and :g:`G <0133.g1:G>`, :g:`H <0133.g1:H>` any equimultiples whatever of :g:`B <0133.g1:B>`, :g:`D <0133.g1:D>`.

Therefore :g:`A <0133.g1:A>` is to :g:`B <0133.g1:B>` as :g:`C <0133.g1:C>` is to :g:`D <0133.g1:D>`. [V. Def. 5]

Next, let the first :g:`A <0133.g2:A>` be the same *part* of the second :g:`B <0133.g2:B>` that the third :g:`C <0133.g2:C>` is of the fourth :g:`D <0133.g2:D>` :

:g:`A <0133.g2:A>` is to :g:`B <0133.g2:B>` as :g:`C <0133.g2:C>` is to :g:`D <0133.g2:D>`.

.. picture:: 0133.g2.svg
   :alt: Folio 0133 Figure 2

For :g:`B <0133.g2:B>` is the same multiple of :g:`A <0133.g2:A>` that :g:`D <0133.g2:D>` is of :g:`C <0133.g2:C>` ;
wherefore, by the preceding case,

:g:`B <0133.g2:B>` is to :g:`A <0133.g2:A>` as :g:`D <0133.g2:D>` is to :g:`C <0133.g2:C>` ;

and, *inversely*, :g:`A <0133.g2:A>` is to :g:`B <0133.g2:B>` as :g:`C <0133.g2:C>` is to :g:`D <0133.g2:D>`.

[For this last inference :person:`Simson <simson>` refers to his Proposition B. That proposition is very simply proved by taking any equimultiples :math:`E`, :math:`F` of :g:`B <0133.g2:B>`, :g:`D <0133.g2:D>` and any equimultiples :math:`G`, :math:`H` of :g:`A <0133.g2:A>`, :g:`C <0133.g2:C>` and then arguing as follows :

Since :g:`A <0133.g2:A>` is to :g:`B <0133.g2:B>` as :g:`C <0133.g2:C>` is to :g:`D <0133.g2:D>`,
:math:`G`, :math:`H` are *simultaneously* greater than, equal to, or less than :math:`E`, :math:`F` respectively ; so that
:math:`E`, :math:`F` are *simultaneously* less than, equal to, or greater than :math:`G`, :math:`H` respectively,
and therefore [Def. 5] :g:`B <0133.g2:B>` is to :g:`A <0133.g2:A>` as :g:`D <0133.g2:D>` is to :g:`C <0133.g2:C>`.]

We have now only to add to Prop. C the case where :g:`AB <0134.g1:/ A B />` contains the *same parts* of :g:`CD <0134.g1:/ C D />` that :g:`EF <0134.g1:/ E F />` does of :g:`GH <0134.g1:/ G H />` :
in this case likewise :g:`AB <0134.g1:/ A B />` is to :g:`CD <0134.g1:/ C D />` as :g:`EF <0134.g1:/ E F />` to :g:`GH <0134.g1:/ G H />`.

Let :g:`CK <0134.g1:/ C K />` be a part of :g:`CD <0134.g1:/ C D />`, and :g:`GL <0134.g1:/ G L />` the same part of :g:`GH <0134.g1:/ G H />` ; let :g:`AB <0134.g1:/ A B />` be the same multiple of :g:`CK <0134.g1:/ C K />` that :g:`EF <0134.g1:/ E F />` is of :g:`GL <0134.g1:/ G L />`.

Therefore, by Prop. C,

:g:`AB <0134.g1:/ A B />` is to :g:`CK <0134.g1:/ C K />` as :g:`EF <0134.g1:/ E F />` to :g:`GL <0134.g1:/ G L />`.

.. picture:: 0134.g1.svg
   :alt: Folio 0134 Figure 1

And :g:`CD <0134.g1:/ C D />`, :g:`GH <0134.g1:/ G H />` are equimultiples of :g:`CK <0134.g1:/ C K />`, :g:`GL <0134.g1:/ G L />`, the second and fourth.

Therefore :g:`AB <0134.g1:/ A B />` is to :g:`CD <0134.g1:/ C D />` as :g:`EF <0134.g1:/ E F />` to :g:`GH <0134.g1:/ G H />` [:person:`Simson <simson>`'s Cor. to V. 4, which however is the particular case of V. 4 in which the "equimultiples" of one pair are the pair itself, i.e. the pair multiplied by unity].

To prove the partial converse we begin with Prop. D.

*If the first be to the second as the third to the fourth, and if the first be a multiple or part of the second, the third is the same multiple or the same part of the fourth.*

Let :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` be to :g:`B <0134.g2:B>` as :g:`C <0134.g2:C>` is to :g:`D <0134.g2:D>` ;
and, first, let :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` be a multiple of :g:`B <0134.g2:B>` ;

:g:`C <0134.g2:C>` is the same multiple of :g:`D <0134.g2:D>`.

Take :g:`E <0134.g2:E>` :eq:`equal <0134.g2:E = 0134.g2:A>` to :g:`A <0134.g2:A>`, and whatever multiple :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` or :g:`E <0134.g2:E>` is of :g:`B <0134.g2:B>`, make :g:`F <0134.g2:F>` the same multiple of :g:`D <0134.g2:D>`.

Then, because :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` is to :g:`B <0134.g2:B>` as :g:`C <0134.g2:C>` is to :g:`D <0134.g2:D>`,
and of :g:`B <0134.g2:B>` the second and :g:`D <0134.g2:D>` the fourth equimultiples have been taken :g:`E <0134.g2:E>` and :g:`F <0134.g2:F>`,

:g:`A <0134.g2:A>` is to :g:`E <0134.g2:E>` as :g:`C <0134.g2:C>` is to :g:`F <0134.g2:F>`. [V. 4, Cor.]

But :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` is :eq:`equal <0134.g2:A = 0134.g2:E>` to :g:`E <0134.g2:E>` ;

therefore :g:`C <0134.g2:C>` is :eq:`equal <0134.g2:C = 0134.g2:F>` to :g:`F <0134.g2:F>`.

[In support of this inference :person:`Simson <simson>` cites his Prop. A, which however we can directly deduce from V. Def. 5 by taking any, but *the same*, equimultiples of all four magnitudes.]

.. picture:: 0134.g2.svg
   :alt: Folio 0134 Figure 2

Now :g:`F <0134.g2:F>` is the same multiple of :g:`D <0134.g2:D>` that :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` is of :g:`B <0134.g2:B>` ;

therefore :g:`C <0134.g2:C>` is the same multiple of :g:`D <0134.g2:D>` that :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` is of :g:`B <0134.g2:B>`.

Next, let the first :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` be a part of the second :g:`B <0134.g2:B>` ;

:g:`C <0134.g2:C>` the third is the same part of the fourth :g:`D <0134.g2:D>`.

Because :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` is to :g:`B <0134.g2:B>` as :g:`C <0134.g2:C>` is to :g:`D <0134.g2:D>`,
*inversely*, :g:`B <0134.g2:B>` is to :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` as :g:`D <0134.g2:D>` is to :g:`C <0134.g2:C>`. [Prop. B]

But :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` is a part of :g:`B <0134.g2:B>` ; therefore :g:`B <0134.g2:B>` is a multiple of :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` ;
and, by the preceding case, :g:`D <0134.g2:D>` is the same multiple of :g:`C <0134.g2:C>`,
that is, :g:`C <0134.g2:C>` is the same part of :g:`D <0134.g2:D>` that :g:`A <0134.g2:A>` is of :g:`B <0134.g2:B>`.

We have, again, only to add to Prop. D the case where :g:`AB <0135.g1:/ A B />` contains any parts of :g:`CD <0135.g1:/ C D />`, and :g:`AB <0135.g1:/ A B />` is to :g:`CD <0135.g1:/ C D />` as :g:`EF <0135.g1:/ E F />` to :g:`GH <0135.g1:/ G H />` ;
then shall :g:`EF <0135.g1:/ E F />` contain the same parts of :g:`GH <0135.g1:/ G H />` that :g:`AB <0135.g1:/ A B />` does of :g:`CD <0135.g1:/ C D />`.

For let :g:`CK <0135.g1:/ C K />` be a part of :g:`CD <0135.g1:/ C D />`, and :g:`GL <0135.g1:/ G L />` the same part of :g:`GH <0135.g1:/ G H />` ; and let :g:`AB <0135.g1:/ A B />` be a multiple of :g:`CK <0135.g1:/ C K />`.

:g:`EF <0135.g1:/ E F />` shall be the same multiple of :g:`GL <0135.g1:/ G L />`.

Take :g:`M <0135.g1:M>` the same multiple of :g:`GL <0135.g1:/ G L />` that :g:`AB <0135.g1:/ A B />` is of :g:`CK <0135.g1:/ C K />` ;
therefore :g:`AB <0135.g1:/ A B />` is to :g:`CK <0135.g1:/ C K />` as :g:`M <0135.g1:M>` is to :g:`GL <0135.g1:/ G L />`. [Prop. C]

.. picture:: 0135.g1.svg
   :alt: Folio 0135 Figure 1

And :g:`CD <0135.g1:/ C D />`, :g:`GH <0135.g1:/ G H />` are equimultiples of :g:`CK <0135.g1:/ C K />`, :g:`GL <0135.g1:/ G L />` ;
therefore :g:`AB <0135.g1:/ A B />` is to :g:`CD <0135.g1:/ C D />` as :g:`M <0135.g1:M>` is to :g:`GH <0135.g1:/ G H />`.

But, by hypothesis, :g:`AB <0135.g1:/ A B />` is to :g:`CD <0135.g1:/ C D />` as :g:`EF <0135.g1:/ E F />` is to :g:`GH <0135.g1:/ G H />` ;
therefore :g:`M <0135.g1:M>` is :eq:`equal <0135.g1:M = 0135.g1:/ E F />` to :g:`EF <0135.g1:/ E F />`, [V. 9]
and consequently :g:`EF <0135.g1:/ E F />` is the same multiple of :g:`GL <0135.g1:/ G L />` that :g:`AB <0135.g1:/ A B />` is of :g:`CK <0135.g1:/ C K />`.
