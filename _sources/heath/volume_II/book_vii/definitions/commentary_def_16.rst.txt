Commentary on Book VII Definition 16
====================================

Back to :ref:`Book VII Definitions <VII.def>`.

Ὅταν δὲ δύο ἀριθμοὶ πολλαπλασιάσαντες ἀλλήλους ποιῶσί τινα, ὁ γενόμενος :term:`ἐπίπεδος <plane-number>` καλεῖται, πλευραὶ δὲ αὐτοῦ οἱ πολλαπλασιάσαντες ἀλλήλους :term:`ἀριθμοί <number>`.

The words *plane* and *solid* applied to numbers are of course adapted from their use with reference to geometrical figures. A number is therefore called *linear* (γραμμικός) when it is regarded as in one dimension, as being a length
(μῆκος). When it takes another dimension in addition, namely *breadth* (πλάτος), it is in two dimensions and becomes *plane* (:term:`ἐπίπεδος <plane-number>`). The distinction between a *plane* and a *plane number* is marked by the use of the neuter in the former case, and the masculine, agreeing with :term:`ἀριθμός <number>`, in the latter case. So with a *square* and a *square number*, and so on. The most obvious form of a plane number is clearly that corresponding to a rectangle in geometry; the number is the product of two linear numbers regarded as *sides* (:term:`πλευραί <side-of-number>`) forming the length and breadth respectively. Such a number is, as :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` says, "so many times so many," and a plane is its counterpart (μίμημα). So :person:`Plato <plato>`, in the :cite:`*Theaetetus* (147 E—148 B) <plato:theaetetus:147-e-148-b>`, says: "We divided all numbers into two kinds, (1) that which can be expressed as equal multiplied by equal (τὸν δυνάμενον ἶσον :term:`ἰσάκις <isakis>` γίγνεσθαι), and which, likening its form to the square, we called *square* and *equilateral*; (2) that which is intermediate, and includes 3 and 5 and every number which cannot be expressed as equal multiplied by equal, but is either less times more or more times less, being always contained by a greater and a less side, which number we likened to the oblong figure (προμήκει σχήματι) and called an *oblong number*.... Such *lines* therefore as square the equilateral and *plane number* [i.e. which can form a plane number with equal sides, or a square] we defined as *length* (μῆκος); but such as square the oblong (here :term:`ἑτερομήκης <heteromekes-arithmos>`) [i.e. the square of which is equal to the oblong] we called *roots* (δυνάμεις) as not being commensurable with the others in length, but only in the plane areas (ἐπιπέδοις), to which the squares on them are equal (ἃ δύνανται)." This passage seems to make it clear that :person:`Plato <plato>` would have represented numbers as Euclid does, by straight lines proportional in length to the numbers they represent (so far as practicable); for, since 3 and 5 are with :person:`Plato <plato>` oblong numbers, and lines with him represent the sides of oblong numbers (since a line represents the "root," the square on which is equal to the oblong), it follows that the unit representing the smaller side must have been represented as a line, and 3, the larger side, as a line of three times the length. But there is another possible way of representing numbers, not by lines of a certain length, but by points disposed in various ways, in straight lines or otherwise. :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` tells us (p. 56, 27) that "in old days they represented the quantuplicities of number in a more natural way (φυσικώτερον) by splitting them up into units, and not, as in our day, by symbols" (συμβολικῶς). :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` too (:cite:`*Metaph.* 1092 b 10 <aristotle:met:1092-b-10>`) mentions one Eurytus as having settled what number belonged to what, such a number to a man, such a number to a horse, and so on, "copying their shapes" (reading τούτων, with Zeller) "*with pebbles* (ταῖς ψήφοις), *just as those do who arrange numbers in the forms of triangles or squares*." We accordingly find numbers represented in :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` and :person:`Theon of Smyrna <theon-of-smyrna>` by a number of α's ranged like points according to geometrical figures. According to this system, any number could be represented by points in a straight line, in which case, says :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` (p. 56, 26), we shall call it rectilinear because it is *without breadth and only advances in length* (ἀπλατῶς ἐπὶ μόνον τὸ μῆκος πρόεισιν). The prime number was called by :person:`Thymaridas <thymaridas>` *rectilinear par excellence*, because it was without breadth and in one dimension only (ἐφ᾽ ἓν μόνον διστάμενος). By this must be meant the impossibility of representing, say, 3 as a plane number, in :person:`Plato <plato>`'s sense, i.e. as a product of two numbers corresponding to a rectangle in geometry; and this view would appear to rest simply upon the representation of a number by *points*, as distinct from lines. Three dots in a straight line would have *no breadth*; and if breadth were introduced in the sense of producing a rectangle, i.e. by placing the same

number of dots in a second line below the first line, the first *plane* number would be 4, and 3 would not be a plane number at all, as :person:`Plato <plato>` says it is. It seems therefore to have been the alternative representation of a number by points, and not lines, which gave rise to the different view of a plane number which we find in :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` and the rest. By means of separate points we can represent numbers in geometrical forms other than rectangles and squares. One dot with two others symmetrically arranged below it shows a triangle, which is a figure *in two dimensions* as much as a rectangle or parallelogram is. Similarly we can arrange certain numbers in the form of regular pentagons or other polygons. According therefore to this mode of representation, 3 is the first *plane* number, being a *triangular number*. The method of formation of triangular, square, pentagonal and other polygonal numbers is minutely described in :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` (:cite:`II. 8 <nicomachus:intro-arith:ii-8>`—11), who distinguishes the separate series of *gnomons* belonging to each, i.e. gives the law determining the number which has to be added to a polygonal number with :math:`n` in a side, in order to make it into a number of the same form but with :math:`n + 1` in a side (the addend being of course the gnomon). Thus the gnomonic series for triangular numbers is 1, 2, 3, 4, 5...; that for squares 1, 3, 5, 7...; that for pentagonal numbers 1, 4, 7, 10..., and so on. The subject need not detain us longer here, as we are at present only concerned with the different views of what constitutes a *plane number*.

Of *plane numbers* in the Platonic and Euclidean sense we have seen that :person:`Plato <plato>` recognises two kinds, the *square* and the *oblong* (:term:`προμήκης <promekes-arithmos>` or :term:`ἑτερομήκης <heteromekes-arithmos>`). Here again Euclid's successors, at all events, subdivided the class more elaborately. :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>`, :person:`Theon of Smyrna <theon-of-smyrna>`, and :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` divide *plane* numbers with unequal sides into (1) :term:`ἑτερομήκεις <heteromekes-arithmos>`, the nearest thing to squares, viz. numbers in which the greater side exceeds the less side by 1 only, or numbers of the form :math:`n(n + 1)`, e.g. :math:`1 \cdot 2`, :math:`2 \cdot 3`, :math:`3 \cdot 4`, etc. (according to :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>`), and (2) :term:`προμήκεις <promekes-arithmos>`, or those whose sides differ by 2 or more, i.e. are of the form :math:`n(n + m)`, where :math:`m` is not less than 2 (:person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` illustrates by :math:`2 \cdot 4`, :math:`3 \cdot 6`, etc.). :person:`Theon of Smyrna <theon-of-smyrna>` (p. 30, 8—14) makes :term:`προμήκεις <promekes-arithmos>` include :term:`ἑτερομήκεις <heteromekes-arithmos>`, saying that their sides may differ by 1 or more; he also speaks of *parallelogram*-numbers as those which have one side different from the other by 2 or more; I do not find this latter term in :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` or :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>`, and indeed it seems superfluous, as *parallelogram* is here only another name for *oblong*. :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` (p. 74, 23 sqq.), always critical of Euclid, attacks him again here for confusing the subject by supposing that the :term:`ἑτερομήκης <heteromekes-arithmos>` number is the product of any two different numbers multiplied together, and by not distinguishing the oblong (:term:`προμήκης <promekes-arithmos>`) from it: "for his definition declares the same number to be square and also :term:`ἑτερομήκης <heteromekes-arithmos>`, as for example 36, 16 and many others: which would be equivalent to the odd number being the same thing as the even." No importance need be attached to this exaggerated statement; it is in any case merely a matter of words, and it is curious that Euclid does not in fact use the word :term:`ἑτερομήκης <heteromekes-arithmos>` of *numbers* at all, but only of geometrical oblong figures as opposed to squares, so that :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` can apparently only have inferred that he used it in an unorthodox manner from the geometrical use of the term in the definitions of Book I. and from the fact that he does not give the two subdivisions of *plane numbers* which are not square, but seems only to divide *plane numbers* into square and not-square. The argument that :term:`ἑτερομήκεις <heteromekes-arithmos>` numbers are a *natural*, and therefore *essential*, subdivision :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` appears to found on the method of successive addition by which they can be evolved; as square numbers are obtained by successively adding
odd numbers as gnomons, so :term:`ἑτερομήκεις <heteromekes-arithmos>` are obtained by adding *even* numbers as gnomons. Thus :math:`1 \cdot 2 = 2`, :math:`2 \cdot 3 = 2 + 4`, :math:`3 \cdot 4 = 2 + 4 + 6`, and so on.
