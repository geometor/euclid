Index of Persons & Commentators¶

Ancient geometers, Neoplatonic commentators, Renaissance humanists, and modern editors in the transmission of Euclid’s Data.

Euclid of Alexandria (fl. c. 300 BCE)¶

Geometer · Author of Elements, Data, Phaenomena & Optics

Alexandrian mathematician called ὁ Στοιχειωτής (‘the Writer of Elements’) by Marinus and Proclus; authored both the synthetic Elements (13 books) and the analytical foundation Data (Δεδομένα).

Marinus of Neapolis (c. 440 – c. 495 CE)¶

Neoplatonic Philosopher & Mathematician · Successor of Proclus

Samaritan-born Neoplatonic philosopher from Flavia Neapolis who succeeded Proclus as Scholarch of the Athenian Academy in 485 CE and composed the surviving Protheoria (philosophical introduction) to Euclid’s Data.

Pappus of Alexandria (c. 290 – c. 350 CE)¶

Alexandrian Geometer · Author of the Synagoge

Late antique Alexandrian mathematician whose Synagoge Book VII places Euclid’s Data first among the thirty-three books of the Treasury of Analysis (ἀναλυόμενος τόπος) and records an older 90-proposition recension.

Apollonius of Perga (c. 240 – c. 190 BCE)¶

Hellenistic Geometer · Author of Conics & On Vergings

Called ‘the Great Geometer’; cited by Marinus (p. 234) for defining the ‘given’ (δεδομένον) as the ‘ordered’ or ‘determinate’ (τεταγμένον) in On Vergings (Περὶ νεύσεων) and his General Treatise.

Diodorus of Alexandria (1st century BCE)¶

Mathematician, Astronomer & Gnomonist

Pupil of Posidonius and author of the Analemma; cited by Marinus (p. 234) for defining the given epistemically as the ‘known’ (γνώριμον), including optical rays and angles even when irrational.

Claudius Ptolemy (c. 100 – c. 170 CE)¶

Astronomer & Mathematician · Author of the Almagest

Alexandrian astronomer cited by Marinus (pp. 234–236) for treating as ‘given’ (ῥητόν) those quantities whose numerical measure is known either exactly or by astronomical approximation, and whose chord table relies on Data Props. 87–88 and 93.

Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287 – 212 BCE)¶

Mathematician, Physicist & Engineer

Cited repeatedly by Marinus (pp. 240–248) via the Archimedean spiral (ἕλιξ) to distinguish properties that are determinate by nature (τεταγμένα) before human discovery from those rendered procurable (πόριμα).

Proclus Diadochus (412 – 485 CE)¶

Neoplatonic Scholarch · Teacher of Marinus

Head of the Athenian Academy, teacher of Marinus of Neapolis, and author of the Commentary on Book I of Euclid’s Elements, whose classification of geometric problems informs Marinus’s Protheoria.

Theon of Alexandria (c. 335 – c. 405 CE)¶

Alexandrian Mathematician & Editor of Euclid

Prepared fourth-century teaching recensions of both Euclid’s Elements and Euclid’s Data; Menge (1896) demonstrated the superiority of the non-Theonine Vatican archetype (Vat. gr. 190, P) over the Theonine vulgate.

Bartholomaeus Zamberti (1473 – after 1539)¶

Venetian Humanist & Translator of Euclid

Published the first Latin translation of the Greek corpus of Euclid (including the Data and Marinus’s Protheoria) at Venice in 1505; praised by Simson for preserving the non-singularity clause of Data Prop. 23.

Claude Hardy (c. 1604 – 1678)¶

French Mathematician & Orientalist · First Greek Editor of the Data

Colleague of Descartes, Mersenne, and Fermat who published the editio princeps of the Greek text of Euclid’s Data and Marinus’s Commentary with Latin translation and scholia at Paris in 1625.

David Gregory (1659 – 1708)¶

Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford · Editor of Euclid’s Opera

Scottish mathematician and Newtonian who edited the folio Oxford Greek-Latin Euclidis quae supersunt omnia (1703), incorporating Edward Bernard’s manuscript collations of the Data.

Sir Isaac Newton (1642 – 1727)¶

Lucasian Professor of Mathematics · Author of the Principia

Revered ancient geometrical analysis (Pappus, Apollonius, and Euclid’s Data) above Cartesian algebra; critiqued by Simson on Prop. XIII (Menge 24) regarding given vs. invariant ratios in Principia Book I, Lemma XVII.

Robert Simson (1687 – 1768)¶

Professor of Mathematics at Glasgow · Critical Restorer of Euclid’s Data

Master of classical geometrical analysis who appended his reconstructed English edition of Euclid’s Data to the second edition of his Elements (1762), introducing explicit fourth-proportional diorismoi, re-ordering Unit 1 and Unit 4–5 propositions, and appending new propositions by Earl Stanhope and Matthew Stewart.

Philip Stanhope, 2nd Earl Stanhope (1714 – 1786)¶

British Mathematician & Fellow of the Royal Society

Patron of Robert Simson who communicated Proposition XCVIII [F] in Simson’s Data—the exterior angle-bisector counterpart to Euclid’s Data Proposition 93 (Simson XCVII).

Matthew Stewart (1717 – 1785)¶

Professor of Mathematics at Edinburgh · Pupil of Simson

Discoverer of Stewart’s Theorem (Some General Theorems of Considerable Use in the Higher Parts of Mathematics, 1746) who communicated Proposition C of Simson’s Data generalizing Euclid’s Data Proposition 94.

Johan Ludvig Heiberg (1854 – 1928)¶

Danish Classical Philologist · Co-Editor of Euclidis Opera Omnia

Established the critical Teubner text of Euclid’s Elements (1883–1888) from Codex Vaticanus Graecus 190 (P) and co-directed Euclidis Opera Omnia with Heinrich Menge.

Heinrich Menge (1838 – 1904)¶

German Classical Philologist · Critical Editor of Euclidis Data (1896)

Edited Volume VI of the Teubner Euclidis Opera Omnia (1896), establishing the authoritative 94-proposition non-Theonine Greek text of Euclid’s Data from Vat. gr. 190 alongside Marinus’s Protheoria and the ancient scholia.

Sir Thomas Little Heath (1861 – 1940)¶

Historian of Greek Mathematics & Translator of Euclid’s Elements

Author of The Thirteen Books of Euclid’s Elements (1908/1926) and A History of Greek Mathematics (1921), whose propositional analysis of the Data bridges Menge’s Greek text with Book II and VI geometric algebra.

Julius Petersen (1839 – 1910)¶

Danish Mathematician · Systematizer of Constructive Locus Methods

Author of Methods and Theories for the Solution of Problems of Geometrical Constructions (1879), recasting Euclid’s Data into the two-locus intersection paradigm, parallel translation, rotation, and homothecy.

Nathan Altshiller-Court (1881 – 1968)¶

Geometer · Author of College Geometry (1952)

Professor of Mathematics at the University of Oklahoma whose College Geometry systematizes classical locus, similitude, power-of-a-point, and angle-bisector constructions descending directly from Euclid’s Data.

Christian Marinus Taisbak (1934 – 2020)¶

Danish Historian of Greek Mathematics

Modern scholar of Greek geometry who analyzed the theatrical/dialogical epistemology of ‘being given’ in Euclid’s Data (2003).
