Magnitudes & Ratios (Propositions 1–24) — Philological & Critical Notes¶

1. Inhomogeneous Magnitudes & Simson’s Diorismos on D.2, D.7, and D.8¶

Unit 1 (D.1–D.24) operates at the universal level of Elements Book V magnitudes (μεγέθη): lines, plane areas, solids, and angles. In his critical notes on D.2 (pp. 456–457), Robert Simson pointed out a subtle foundational boundary that Euclid leaves tacit: in the Elements, constructing a fourth proportional $C : D = A : B$ is proved constructively only for straight lines ([VI.12]), not directly for arbitrary solids or inhomogeneous magnitudes.

Simson observed that whenever a given ratio between two homogeneous magnitudes can be exhibited as the ratio of two given straight lines $L_1 : L_2$ (as is always the case for constructible plane and solid problems in geometry), [VI.12] supplies the required fourth proportional rigorously. Similarly, in D.8 (magnitudes having given ratios to the same magnitude have a given ratio to one another), $A$ and $C$ must be homogeneous with one another (both lines, or both areas, etc.) in order to stand in a Eudoxian ratio ([V.def.3]).

2. The Affine-Linear Calculus (Defs. 11–12 & D.10–D.21) and Simson’s Additions¶

Propositions D.10–D.21 constitute an extraordinary piece of Greek symbolic compression. By packaging the two-parameter affine relation $y = k x + c$ ($c > 0$ given in magnitude, $k > 0$ given in ratio) into the single predicate δοθέντι μεῖζον ἢ ἐν λόγῳ ([Data Def. 11]) and $y = k x - c$ into [Data Def. 12], Euclid proves that affine functions of an unknown magnitude form a closed algebraic groupoid:

  • Additive & Subtractive Shifts (D.10–D.11): Notice that in Heinrich Menge's non-Theonine text (1896, pp. 16–22), both D.10 and D.11 contain two parts—a direct theorem and its converse! In the converse of D.10, subtracting the given offset $c$ from $x + y = c + k y$ splits into two cases according as $c < x$ (leaving $x = c + (k-1)y$) or $c > x$ (producing $x + (c - x) = c$ where $y : (c - x)$ is given, i.e., $x = c - k' y$). Because Euclid in Vat. gr. 190 avoided phrasing the second branch via Def. 12 directly, Simson (Props. XIV–XXIV) split and expanded the entire suite symmetrically across all sign combinations (Props. XIV–XV and Props. XXI–XXII).

  • Composition & Elimination (D.13, D.17–D.19): D.19 proves closure under composition ($A = k_1 B + c_1$ and $B = k_2 C + c_2 \implies A = (k_1 k_2) C + (c_1 + k_1 c_2)$), while D.17 and D.18 eliminate a shared variable between two affine equations.

  • The Doublet D.21: As Simson noted (p. 458), D.21 (adding magnitudes in a given ratio to two given magnitudes) is mathematically identical to the first part of D.14 (adding two given magnitudes to magnitudes in a given ratio) with the roles of the summands swapped; Simson therefore omitted D.21, whereas Menge retained it on the manuscript authority of Vat. gr. 190.

3. Non-Singularity in D.23 (det != 0) & Simson’s Critique of Newton on D.24¶

Two philological gems crown the close of * Determinant Non-Singularity in D.23: In D.23, if whole $AB = AE + EB$ has a given ratio $r$ to whole $CD = CF + FD$, while $AE : CF = r_1$ and $EB : FD = r_2$ are given, the crucial Greek proviso μὴ τοὺς αὐτοὺς δέ ("yet not the same ratios", $r_1 \neq r_2$) is the exact classical statement of matrix non-singularity! Algebraically, $(r_1 - r) CF + (r_2 - r) FD = 0$; if $r_1 = r_2 = r$, the two equations collapse into one ($0 = 0$) and $CF : FD$ is indeterminate (ἄτακτον), whereas $r_1 \neq r_2$ forces $CF : FD = (r - r_2) : (r_1 - r)$ uniquely. Bartolomeo Zamberti's 1505 Latin translation omitted the negation non, rendering the theorem false until Claude Hardy (1625) and David Gregory (1703) restored the Greek condition.

  • Continued Proportion & Quadratic Root Extraction in D.24: D.24 proves that if $A : B = B : C$ and $A : C$ is given, then $A : B$ is given ($A/B = \sqrt{A/C}$). Notably, Unit 1 here steps from general magnitudes to straight lines (εὐθεῖαι) so that [VI.13] can construct the geometric mean $E = \sqrt{D \cdot F}$. Late Byzantine manuscripts appended an alternative proof (Ἄλλως, relegated by Menge to the Appendix) that tried to dispense with squares, and Isaac Newton cited a similar shortcut in Principia Book I, Lemma XVII; Simson (pp. 459–460) demonstrated that the scholiast’s Ἄλλως tacitly begged the question, vindicating Euclid’s authentic proof.

Position, Angles & Parallel Loci (Propositions 25–38) — Philological & Critical Notes¶

1. Shifting Proofs (Μεταπεσεῖται) vs. Constructive Proofs in D.25–D.30¶

The opening propositions of Unit 2 (D.25–D.30) exhibit a striking stylistic feature in the Greek manuscripts: instead of constructing an auxiliary copy, they argue by reductio ad absurdum using the kinematic verb μεταπεσεῖται ("it will shift / fall elsewhere"), appealing directly to [Data Def. 4] ("always occupying the same place").

Robert Simson (pp. 460–462), consistent with his repair of Definition 4 (requiring procurability alongside uniqueness), replaced several shifting arguments with explicit straightedge-and-compass constructions ([Post. 1], [Post. 3], [I.23], [I.31]) and inserted a companion converse (Simson Prop. XXVII: if one extremity of a straight line given in magnitude be given, and the line also pass through another given point, or be given in position…). Modern foundational scholarship (Taisbak (2003)) recognizes that Euclid’s μεταπεσεῖται idiom intentionally isolates topological/incidence invariance once elementary constructibility is already guaranteed by Books I and III.

2. Multi-Valued Intersections & Diorismos in D.31 and D.33¶

In D.31 (drawing a segment of given length $r$ from a given point $A$ to a position-given line $CD$), the circle $(A, r)$ ([Data Def. 6]) meets $CD$ in:

  • Zero points if $r < d(A, CD)$ (requiring the diorismos $r \ge d(A, CD)$);

  • One point (tangent foot of the perpendicular) if $r = d(A, CD)$; or

  • Two symmetric points $B_1, B_2$ on $CD$ if $r > d(A, CD)$.

As Marinus already explained in Protheoria § 4, Greek analysis treats a finitely bifurcated intersection—unique on each side of the perpendicular axis of symmetry—as genuinely ordered and given (μοναχῶς ἐφʼ ἑκάτερα), in sharp contrast to an indeterminate (ἄτακτον) continuum of solutions. Exactly the same two-branch symmetry governs D.33.

3. Petersen’s Two-Locus & Parallel-Translation Paradigm (D.25–D.38)¶

Nearly twenty-two centuries after Euclid, Julius Petersen opened his celebrated Danish problem-solving classic Methods and Theories for the Solution of Problems of Geometrical Constructions (1879) by distilling Unit 2 of Euclid’s Data into two master heuristics:

  1. Method of Loci (Chapter I): Drop one constraint on an unknown point $X$; its locus is a position-given line (D.28–D.30, D.35–D.38) or circle ([Data Def. 6]). Restore the second constraint to obtain a second position-given locus; D.25 then determines $X$ as their intersection.

  2. Parallel Translation & Homothety (Chapters II–III): Propositions D.32–D.33 translate a floating segment $EF$ between parallel lines $AB \parallel CD$ to a chosen anchor point $G \in AB$, while D.35–D.38 apply central dilation (homothety) from a fixed centre $A$ (or transversal dilation between parallels) to transform a position-given line into a parallel position-given locus. Note also that Simson (Props. XXXIX–XL) consolidated Euclid’s external/internal pairs D.35 + D.36 into Prop. XXXIX and D.37 + D.38 into Prop. XL.

Triangles & Polygons Given in Species (Propositions 39–55) — Philological & Critical Notes¶

1. Triangle Rigidity modulo Similarity & Petersen’s Method of Similarity (D.39–D.42)¶

Unit 3 shifts from position in the plane to intrinsic shape—figures given in species ([Data Def. 3]), i.e., determined up to similarity (angles given and pairwise side-ratios given). Propositions D.39–D.42 lift the congruence and similarity theorems of Books I and VI into constructive shape-determination operators:

  • D.39 (SSS $\to$ species, via [I.22] & [I.8]);

  • D.40 (AAA / AA $\to$ species, via [I.32] & [VI.4]);

  • D.41 (SAS-ratio $\to$ species, via [VI.6]);

  • D.42 (SSS-ratios $\to$ species, via [VI.5]).

Notice Euclid’s constructive technique throughout: he sets out an arbitrary reference segment $DE$ given in position and magnitude, builds a concrete prototype $\triangle DEF$ whose vertices are determined in position by Unit 2, reads off its given angles and ratios, and transfers species to $\triangle ABC$ by Book VI similarity. This is the exact archetype of Petersen's Method of Similarity (1879, Chapter II).

2. Unambiguous Right-Triangle HL (D.43) vs. General SSA Bifurcation (D.44)¶

The contrast between D.43 and D.44 (illustrated interactively in data_03_triangle_species.svg) showcases Euclid’s command of diorismos:

  • In D.43, $\angle BAC = 90^\circ$ is opposite the hypotenuse $BC$, while the ratio $BC : BA$ ($> 1$ by [I.19]) is given. Since the remaining angle $\angle ACB$ is necessarily acute ([I.17]), inscribing $DG$ in the Thales semicircle $DGE$ ([III.31]) yields a single, unambiguous species ([VI.7]).

  • In D.44, $\angle ABC$ is given while the ratio $AB : AC$ of the sides about another angle $\angle BAC$ is given (SSA). When $AC < AB$ ($AC \ge AB \sin B$), the circle $(A, AC)$ cuts ray $BC$ in two distinct points $C_1, C_2$ symmetric across the altitude foot $P$, with $\angle AC_1 B + \angle AC_2 B = 180^\circ$! Euclid’s proof drops the altitude $AD \perp BC$, reduces $\triangle ADC$ to D.43, and handles the acute and obtuse branches separately (χωρὶς δὲ τοῦ συμπτώματος), matching [VI.7].

3. Angle-Bisector Harmonic Reduction (D.45–D.46) vs. Simson’s Construction¶

Propositions D.45 and D.46 determine the species of $\triangle ABC$ from one angle and the ratio $(BA + AC) : BC$ of the sum of two sides to the third side:

  • Euclid’s Proof via Elements VI.3: Bisecting $\angle BAC$ by $AD$ gives $BA : AC = BD : DC$ ([VI.3]), whence alternando and componendo $(BA + AC) : BC = BA : BD$! In D.46 (where $\angle ABC$ is given), $\angle ABD$ lies between $BA$ and $BD$, so D.41 applies immediately without ambiguity. In D.45 (where $\angle BAC$ is given), $\angle BAD = \frac{1}{2}\angle BAC$ is opposite $BD$; since $BD < BC$ and $\angle ADB = \angle DAC + \angle C > \angle BAD$, $\angle ABD$ can actually take two supplementary-like configurations only up to swapping $AB$ and $AC$!

  • Simson’s Isosceles-Extension Proof & Added Prop. L: Simson (Props. XLVIII–L, pp. 394–397, 463) noted that extending $CA$ to $E$ with $AE = AB$ makes $EC = BA + AC$ directly and $\angle AEB = \frac{1}{2}\angle BAC$, reducing D.45 (Simson XLVIII) cleanly to D.44 on $\triangle EBC$ and inspiring Simson’s companion Prop. L for the difference of the sides $(BA - AC) : BC$ (via cutting off $AE' = AB$ internally).

4. Polygon Triangulation & Quadratic Area-to-Side Extraction (D.47–D.55)¶

The second half of Unit 3 (D.47–D.55) links 1D linear segments to 2D rectilinear areas:

  • D.47 proves that every species-given $n$-gon triangulates into $(n-2)$ species-given triangles by ear-clipping diagonals (D.41).

  • D.48–D.51 establish that any two species-given figures on the same or ratio-given segments have a given area ratio, culminating in D.52 (species-given figure on a magnitude-given segment is given in area, calibrated against the unit square $AB^2$).

  • D.54 and D.55 invert the quadratic scaling map $S \propto x^2$! Using D.24 (which extracted the square root of a ratio via the geometric mean [VI.13]), D.54 deduces linear side-ratios from area-ratios, and D.55 proves the grand converse of D.52: any figure given both in species and in magnitude has all its sides given in magnitude—the exact analytical counterpart of [Elements VI.25] (constructing a figure similar to one given figure and equal in area to another).

Application of Areas, Quadratics & Cosine Projections (Propositions 56–67) — Philological & Critical Notes¶

1. Geometric Algebra of Application of Areas: Parabolē, Elleipsis, and Hyperbolē (D.57–D.60)¶

Unit 4 (D.56–D.67) translates the constructive application of areas (παραβολὴ τῶν χωρίων, Elements II.5–6 & VI.27–29) into analytical root-determination theorems:

  • Parabolē (παραβολή, D.57): Exact application $a \cdot x = S$ in a given angle $\\theta$ solves the linear equation $x = S / (a \sin\\theta)$.

  • Elleipsis (ἔλλειψις, D.58): Elliptic application $(a - x)\cdot (\lambda x) = S$ solves the quadratic equation $\lambda x^2 - \lambda a x + S = 0$ by completing the gnomon on the half-segment $a/2$ ([II.5], [VI.28]), subject to the discriminant diorismos $S \le \lambda (a/2)^2$ ([VI.27]).

  • Hyperbolē (ὑπερβολή, D.59): Hyperbolic application $(a + x)\cdot (\lambda x) = S$ solves $\lambda x^2 + \lambda a x = S$ unconditionally via [II.6] and [VI.29]. When $S = a^2$ and the excess is a square ($\lambda = 1$), $x(a + x) = a^2$ determines the golden section $x = a/\phi \in \mathbb{Q}(\phi)$ ([II.11], [VI.30])!

As Proclus (In Eucl. 419.15) and Pappus record, Apollonius of Perga borrowed the very names parabola, ellipse, and hyperbola directly from this Pythagorean-Euclidean triad.

2. Area-to-Species Bridges (D.61–D.63) & Textual Notes¶

Propositions D.61 and D.62 bridge area-ratios back to species: if a parallelogram $CD$ in a given angle is applied either to a side of a species-given figure (D.61) or to a segment having a given ratio to a side of a species-given figure (D.62), and the area ratio is given, then $CD$ itself is given in species. Simson (Props. LXIX–LXX, p. 464) retained both (D.61–62) while omitting D.63 (species-given figures on the three sides of a species-given triangle have given ratios to one another) as an immediate corollary of D.51; Menge preserved D.63 from Vat. gr. 190.

3. The Euclidean Law of Cosines, Sine Area Formula & Half-Angle Excess (D.64–D.67)¶

Propositions D.64–D.67 form the trigonometric heart of Greek plane geometry (visualized in data_05_cosine_projection.svg):

  • Obtuse & Acute Cosine Laws (D.64–D.65): By combining [Elements II.12] and [II.13] with species-given right-triangle projections (D.40), Euclid proves that $|a^2 + c^2 - b^2| : (a c) = 2\, BD : AB = 2|\cos B|$ is given whenever $\angle B$ is given—without ever needing symbolic trigonometric tables!

  • Sine Area Law (D.66): Proves $(AB \cdot BC) : \\triangle ABC = 2/\sin B$ is given whenever $\angle B$ is given.

  • Compound-Side Quadratic Excess (D.67, Simson Prop. LXXVI): Proves $((b + c)^2 - a^2) : \\triangle ABC$ is given whenever $\angle A$ is given. Algebraically, $(b+c)^2 - a^2 = 2bc(1 + \cos A)$ while $\\triangle ABC = \frac{1}{2}bc\sin A$, so their constant ratio is $4(1+\cos A)/\sin A = 4\cot(A/2)$! In Vat. gr. 190 (Menge pp. 122–126), Euclid establishes this synthetically without trigonometry by extending $BA$ to $D$ with $AD = AC$ and applying [II.6].

Proportional Parallelograms, Triangles & Four-Line Relations (Propositions 68–83) — Philological & Critical Notes¶

1. Compound Ratios & Oblique Shear Invariance (D.68–D.75)¶

Unit 5 (D.68–D.83) lifts the theory of equiangular parallelograms ([Elements VI.14], [VI.23]) to arbitrary given angles. Euclid’s key geometric operator in D.69, D.70, D.73, and D.74 is an area-preserving Cavalieri shear ([I.35]) on a fixed base $BC$: shearing a parallelogram from given angle $\\theta_1$ to given angle $\\theta_2$ introduces a corner triangle $\\triangle ABP$ whose angles are given, so its slant-side ratio $BA : BP = \sin\\theta_2 / \sin\\theta_1$ is given by D.40!

In his philological commentary (pp. 464–466), Robert Simson showed that late Greek scribes had slightly garbled the reciprocally-proportional clauses of D.73 and D.74, and consolidated them cleanly in Simson Props. LXIII–LXVII.

2. Manuscript Doublets (D.77–D.78) & the Inscribed-Angle Locus Theorem (D.79–D.80)¶

  • Doublets in Codex P: D.77 repeats the enunciation of D.54 with an alternative square-calibration proof, and D.78 specializes D.62 to rectangles; Simson omitted both as scholiastic doublets, whereas Menge (pp. 148–152) printed them in their traditional numbering so all ancient citations from Pappus remain intact.

  • The Inscribed-Angle & Parallel-Chord Masterpiece (D.79–D.80): D.80 (Simson Prop. LXXVIII) proves that $\\triangle ABC$ is given in species if $\angle A$ and the ratio $(AB \cdot AC) : BC^2$ are given. Euclid prepares this via the pure locus lemma D.79: two triangles with equal vertex angles $\angle A = \angle D$ and equal base-to-altitude ratios $BC : AG = EF : DH$ can be superposed onto the same circular segment ([III.21], [III.33]), where equal altitudes cut the arc in a parallel line symmetric across the perpendicular bisector! Simson admired this cluster so deeply that he added four companion theorems (Props. LXXVII, LXXIX–LXXXI).

3. Four-Line Proportional Permutations & Apollonian Analysis (D.81–D.83)¶

Propositions D.81–D.83 provide the algebraic rewrite rules for proportions $A : B = C : D$ when one or more terms are replaced by lines having given ratios to them. As Pappus (Synagoge Book VII) demonstrates across his lemmas to Apollonius's Cutting off of a Ratio (Λόγου ἀποτομή), Cutting off of an Area (Χωρίου ἀποτομή), and Determinate Section (Διωρισμένη τομή), D.82 and D.83 allow an analyst to absorb arbitrary given multiplicative constants into auxiliary segments without breaking a four-term proportion.

Hyperbolic/Elliptic Systems, Circles, Chords & Angle Bisectors (Propositions 84–94) — Philological & Critical Notes¶

1. Canonical Vieta Systems (D.84–D.85) & the Biquadratic Theorem (D.86)¶

Unit 6 opens (D.84–D.86) with the three most famous algebraic propositions in the Data—solving simultaneous two-variable quadratic systems:

  1. Product & Difference (D.84): $x y \sin\\theta = S$ and $y - x = d \implies$ apply $S$ to $d$ with species-given rhombus excess (D.59). When $\\theta = 90^\circ$ and $S = d^2$, $x(x + d) = d^2$ yields the golden ratio $y/x = \phi$!

  2. Product & Sum (D.85): $x y \sin\\theta = S$ and $x + y = s \implies$ apply $S$ to $s$ with species-given rhombus defect (D.58), subject to $S \le \frac{1}{4}s^2 \sin\\theta$.

  3. Product & Affine Ratio of Squares (D.86): $x y \sin\\theta = S$ and $y^2 - C = k x^2$. In 1703 David Gregory (Praefatio) suspected the Greek text of D.86 might be corrupt; Robert Simson (pp. 467–468) brilliantly vindicated the integrity of Euclid’s biquadratic proof (which completes the square via $y^2 - C = y(y - d)$ and $(2y - d)^2 = 4y(y - d) + d^2$, [II.8]) and added Simson Prop. LXXXVIII covering the sum-of-squares system $x y = S,\; x^2 + y^2 = C$.

2. Chord Trigonometry (D.87–D.89) & Power of a Point (D.90–D.92)¶

Propositions D.87–D.89 establish the constructive equivalence between inscribed angles and chord lengths in circles given in magnitude or position (see data_06_circle_angle_locus.svg). In Book I of the Syntaxis (Almagest), Claudius Ptolemy builds his entire table of chords (Πραγματεία τῶν ἐν κύκλῳ εὐθειῶν) on this foundation alongside D.93.

Propositions D.90–D.92 reformulate [Elements III.17], [III.35], and [III.36] as the invariance of the power of a given point $|OP^2 - R^2|$ with respect to a position-given circle: every secant from an external given point (D.91) or chord through an internal given point (D.92) cuts off segments whose rectangle $PA \cdot PB$ is given in magnitude—the invariant engine behind Apollonius's Tangencies and Plane Loci as well as modern radical-axis geometry (Altshiller-Court 1952).

3. The Crown Jewels (D.93–D.94) & Earl Stanhope’s and Matthew Stewart’s Theorems¶

The treatise culminates in two virtuoso cyclic-similarity theorems (D.93–D.94, with D.93 rendered interactively in data_07_prop93_bisector.svg):

  • Euclid’s D.93 & Earl Stanhope’s Exterior-Bisector Theorem (`Simson Prop. XCVIII [F]`): When a given chord $BC$ cuts off a segment admitting given $\angle BAC$ in a magnitude-given circle, and the interior bisector $AD$ of $\angle BAC$ meets $BC$ at $E$ and the opposite arc at $D$, Euclid proves both that $(BA + AC) : AD = BC : BD$ is given in ratio and that $(BA + AC) \cdot ED = BD^2$ is given in magnitude! In his 1762/1806 edition (Prop. XCVIII & p. 469), Simson paired D.93 with its dual communicated to him by Philip Stanhope, 2nd Earl Stanhope (Earl Stanhope): if instead the exterior angle at $A$ is bisected by chord $AD'$ meeting $BC$ produced at $E'$ and the circle at $D'$, then the difference $|BA - AC|$ has a given ratio to $AD'$, and $|BA - AC| \cdot E'D' = BD'^2$ is given in magnitude!

  • Euclid’s D.94 & Matthew Stewart’s Generalization (`Simson Prop. C`): Immediately following D.94, Simson (Prop. C & pp. 469–470) published a remarkable generalization discovered by his pupil and colleague Matthew Stewart (author of Some General Theorems of Considerable Use in the Higher Parts of Mathematics, 1746), showing how fixed lines and chords inflected in a given circle preserve given combinations of products and squares—a direct bridge from Alexandrian Data to eighteenth-century Scottish synthetic geometry.

English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 46 [μϛ΄ · Prop. Prop. XLIX]

If a triangle have one angle given, and the sides together about another angle have a given ratio to the remaining side, the triangle is given in species.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 47 [μζ΄ · Prop. Prop. LI]

Rectilineal figures given in species are divided into triangles given in species.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 48 [μη΄ · Prop. Prop. LII]

If on the same straight line two triangles given in species be described, they will have a given ratio to one another.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 49 [μθ΄ · Prop. Prop. LIII]

If on the same straight line any two rectilineal figures given in species be described, they will have a given ratio to one another.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 50 [ν΄ · Prop. Prop. LIV]

If two straight lines have a given ratio to one another, the rectilineal figures similar and similarly described upon them will also have a given ratio to one another.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 51 [να΄ · Prop. Prop. LV]

If two straight lines have a given ratio to one another, and upon them be described any rectilineal figures given in species, those figures will have a given ratio to one another.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 52 [νβ΄ · Prop. Prop. LVI]

If on a straight line given in magnitude a rectilineal figure given in species be described, it is given in magnitude.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 53 [νγ΄ · Prop. Prop. LVII]

If two figures be given in species, and one side of one have a given ratio to one side of the other, the remaining sides will also have given ratios to the remaining sides.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 54 [νδ΄ · Prop. Prop. LIX]

If two figures given in species have a given ratio to one another, their sides will also have a given ratio to one another.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 55 [νε΄ · Prop. Prop. LX]

If an area be given in species and in magnitude, its sides will also be given in magnitude.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 56 [νϛ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXIII]

If two equiangular parallelograms have a given ratio to one another, as a side of the first is to a side of the second, so is the remaining side of the second to that straight line to which the remaining side of the first has the given ratio that the parallelogram has to the parallelogram.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 57 [νζ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXI]

If a given area be applied to a given straight line in a given angle, the breadth of the application is given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 58 [νη΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXXIII]

If a given area be applied to a given straight line, falling short by a figure given in species, the breadths of the defect are given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 59 [νθ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXXIV]

If a given area be applied to a given straight line, exceeding by a figure given in species, the breadths of the excess are given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 60 [ξ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXXII]

If a parallelogram given in species and in magnitude be increased or diminished by a gnomon given in magnitude, the breadths of the gnomon are given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 61 [ξα΄ · Prop. Prop. LXIX]

If along one of the sides of a figure given in species a parallelogrammic area be applied in a given angle, and the figure have a given ratio to the parallelogram, the parallelogram is given in species.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 62 [ξβ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXX]

If two straight lines have a given ratio to one another, and on one of them be described a figure given in species, and on the other a parallelogrammic area in a given angle, and the figure have a given ratio to the parallelogram, the parallelogram is given in species.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 63 [ξγ΄ · Prop. Omitted (case of Prop. LIII)]

If a triangle be given in species, the square on each of its sides will have a given ratio to the triangle.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 64 [ξδ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXIV]

If an obtuse-angled triangle have the obtuse angle given, then that area by which the square on the side subtending the obtuse angle exceeds the squares on the sides containing the obtuse angle will have a given ratio to the triangle.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 65 [ξε΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXV]

If an acute-angled triangle have one acute angle given, then that area by which the square on the side subtending the acute angle is less than the squares on the sides containing the acute angle will have a given ratio to the triangle.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 66 [ξϛ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXII Cor.]

If a triangle have a given angle, the rectangle contained by the straight lines containing the given angle has a given ratio to the triangle.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 67 [ξζ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXVI]

If a triangle have a given angle, then that area by which the square on both the sides containing the given angle taken together as one exceeds the square on the remaining side will have a given ratio to the triangle.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 68 [ξη΄ · Prop. Prop. LXV]

If two equiangular parallelograms have a given ratio to one another, and one side have to one side a given ratio, the remaining side will also have to the remaining side a given ratio.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 69 [ξθ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXVI]

If two parallelograms have given angles, and have a given ratio to one another, and one side have to one side a given ratio, the remaining side will also have a given ratio to the remaining side.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 70 [ο΄ · Prop. Props. LXVII–LXVIII]

If of two parallelograms about equal angles, or about unequal yet given angles, the sides have given ratios to one another, the parallelograms themselves will also have a given ratio to one another.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 71 [οα΄ · Prop. Prop. LXVIII Cor. 1]

If of two triangles the sides about equal angles, or about unequal yet given angles, have given ratios to one another, the triangles themselves also have a given ratio to one another.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 72 [οβ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXVIII Cor. 2]

If of two triangles both the bases be in a given ratio, and the straight lines drawn onto them from the vertices making either equal angles or unequal yet given angles with the bases be in a given ratio, the triangles themselves will also have a given ratio to one another.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 73 [ογ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXIV (pt. 2)]

If of two parallelograms about equal angles, or about unequal yet given angles, the sides stand so that as a side of the first is to a side of the second, so is the remaining side of the second to some other straight line, and the remaining side of the first have a given ratio to that straight line, the parallelograms themselves will also have a given ratio to one another.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 74 [οδ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXIV (pt. 1; cf. LXIII)]

If two parallelograms have a given ratio, either in equal angles or in unequal yet given angles, as a side of the first is to a side of the second, so will the other side of the second be to that straight line to which the remaining side of the first has a given ratio.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 75 [οε΄ · Prop. Prop. LXIV Cor.]

If two triangles have a given ratio to one another, either in equal angles or in unequal yet given angles, as a side of the first is to a side of the second, so will the other side of the second be to that straight line to which the remaining side of the first has a given ratio.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 76 [οϚ΄ · Prop. Prop. L]

If in a triangle given in species a perpendicular be drawn from the vertex onto the base, the drawn perpendicular has a given ratio to the base.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 77 [οζ΄ · Prop. Omitted (dup. of Prop. LIX / D.54)]

If two figures given in species have a given ratio to one another, any side of one of the figures will also have a given ratio to any side of the other.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 78 [οη΄ · Prop. Omitted (case of Prop. LXX / D.62)]

If a given figure have a given ratio to some parallelogram, and one side have a given ratio to one side of the figure, while the parallelogram is in a given angle, the parallelogram will be given in species.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 79 [οθ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXVII Cor. 1]

If there be two triangles having one angle equal to one angle, and from the vertices perpendiculars be drawn to the bases, and as the base of the first triangle is to its perpendicular, so be the base of the second triangle to its perpendicular, the triangles are equiangular.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 80 [π΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXVIII]

If a triangle have one angle given, and the rectangle contained by the sides about the given angle have a given ratio to the square on the remaining side, the triangle is given in species.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 81 [πα΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXI]

If of three proportional straight lines the extremes have given ratios to the extremes of three proportional straight lines, the means will also have a given ratio to one another; and if an extreme have a given ratio to an extreme and the mean to the mean, the remaining extreme will also have a given ratio to the remaining extreme.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 82 [πβ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXII]

If four straight lines be proportional, as the first is to that straight line to which the second has a given ratio, so will the third be to that straight line to which the fourth has a given ratio.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 83 [πγ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXIII]

If four straight lines be so related to one another that, when three of them are taken and a fourth is assumed to them to which the remaining one of the original four has a given ratio, the four straight lines are proportional, then as the fourth is to the third, so is the second to that straight line to which the first has a given ratio.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 84 [πδ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXXV]

If two straight lines contain a given area in a given angle, and one exceed the other by a given straight line, each of them will also be given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 85 [πε΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXXVI]

If two straight lines contain a given area in a given angle, and both taken together be given, each of them will also be given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 86 [πϛ΄ · Prop. Prop. LXXXIX (cf. LXXXVII–LXXXVIII)]

If two straight lines contain a given area in a given angle, and the square on one be greater than the square on the other by a given area than in ratio, each of them will also be given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 87 [πζ΄ · Prop. Prop. XCI]

If in a circle given in magnitude a straight line be drawn cutting off a segment that accepts a given angle, the straight line drawn is given in magnitude.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 88 [πη΄ · Prop. Prop. XCII]

If in a circle given in magnitude a straight line given in magnitude be drawn, it will cut off a segment accepting a given angle.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 89 [πθ΄ · Prop. Prop. XCIII]

If on the circumference of a circle given in position a given point be taken, and from it a straight line be inflected to the circumference of the circle making a given angle, the other extremity of the inflected line is given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 90 [ϙ΄ · Prop. Prop. XCIV]

If from a given point a straight line be drawn touching a circle given in position, the straight line drawn is given in position and in magnitude.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 91 [ϙα΄ · Prop. Prop. XCV]

If a point be taken outside a circle given in position, and from the point some straight line be drawn across the circle, the rectangle contained by the whole drawn straight line and the segment intercepted outside between the point and the convex circumference is given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 92 [ϙβ΄ · Prop. Prop. XCVI]

If a point be taken inside a circle given in position, and through the point some straight line be drawn across the circle, the rectangle contained by the segments of the drawn straight line is given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 93 [ϙγ΄ · Prop. Prop. XCVII (cf. Prop. XCVIII [Stanhope])]

If into a circle given in magnitude a straight line be drawn cutting off a segment accepting a given angle, and the angle in the segment be bisected, both the straight lines containing the given angle taken together will have a given ratio to the bisecting chord, and the rectangle contained by both the straight lines containing the given angle taken together and the lower segment cut off from the bisector toward the circumference will be given.
English · Greek (Menge 1896) · Summary · Notes

Proposition 94 [ϙδ΄ · Prop. Prop. XCIX (cf. Prop. C [Stewart])]

If in the diameter of a circle given in position a given point be taken, and from that point a straight line be drawn to the circle, and from the section a perpendicular be drawn to the drawn line, and through the point where the perpendicular meets the circumference again a parallel be drawn to the first drawn line, both the point at which the parallel meets the diameter is given, and the rectangle contained by the parallel chords is given.
