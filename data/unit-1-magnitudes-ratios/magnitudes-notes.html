Unit 1: Magnitudes & Ratios (Propositions 1–24) — Philological & Critical Notes¶

1. Inhomogeneous Magnitudes & Simson’s Diorismos on D.2, D.7, and D.8¶

Unit 1 (D.1–D.24) operates at the universal level of Elements Book V magnitudes (μεγέθη): lines, plane areas, solids, and angles. In his critical notes on D.2 (pp. 456–457), Robert Simson pointed out a subtle foundational boundary that Euclid leaves tacit: in the Elements, constructing a fourth proportional $C : D = A : B$ is proved constructively only for straight lines ([VI.12]), not directly for arbitrary solids or inhomogeneous magnitudes.

Simson observed that whenever a given ratio between two homogeneous magnitudes can be exhibited as the ratio of two given straight lines $L_1 : L_2$ (as is always the case for constructible plane and solid problems in geometry), [VI.12] supplies the required fourth proportional rigorously. Similarly, in D.8 (magnitudes having given ratios to the same magnitude have a given ratio to one another), $A$ and $C$ must be homogeneous with one another (both lines, or both areas, etc.) in order to stand in a Eudoxian ratio ([V.def.3]).

2. The Affine-Linear Calculus (Defs. 11–12 & D.10–D.21) and Simson’s Additions¶

Propositions D.10–D.21 constitute an extraordinary piece of Greek symbolic compression. By packaging the two-parameter affine relation $y = k x + c$ ($c > 0$ given in magnitude, $k > 0$ given in ratio) into the single predicate δοθέντι μεῖζον ἢ ἐν λόγῳ ([Data Def. 11]) and $y = k x - c$ into [Data Def. 12], Euclid proves that affine functions of an unknown magnitude form a closed algebraic groupoid:

  • Additive & Subtractive Shifts (D.10–D.11): Notice that in Heinrich Menge's non-Theonine text (1896, pp. 16–22), both D.10 and D.11 contain two parts—a direct theorem and its converse! In the converse of D.10, subtracting the given offset $c$ from $x + y = c + k y$ splits into two cases according as $c < x$ (leaving $x = c + (k-1)y$) or $c > x$ (producing $x + (c - x) = c$ where $y : (c - x)$ is given, i.e., $x = c - k' y$). Because Euclid in Vat. gr. 190 avoided phrasing the second branch via Def. 12 directly, Simson (Props. XIV–XXIV) split and expanded the entire suite symmetrically across all sign combinations (Props. XIV–XV and Props. XXI–XXII).

  • Composition & Elimination (D.13, D.17–D.19): D.19 proves closure under composition ($A = k_1 B + c_1$ and $B = k_2 C + c_2 \implies A = (k_1 k_2) C + (c_1 + k_1 c_2)$), while D.17 and D.18 eliminate a shared variable between two affine equations.

  • The Doublet D.21: As Simson noted (p. 458), D.21 (adding magnitudes in a given ratio to two given magnitudes) is mathematically identical to the first part of D.14 (adding two given magnitudes to magnitudes in a given ratio) with the roles of the summands swapped; Simson therefore omitted D.21, whereas Menge retained it on the manuscript authority of Vat. gr. 190.

3. Non-Singularity in D.23 (det != 0) & Simson’s Critique of Newton on D.24¶

Two philological gems crown the close of Unit 1:

  • Determinant Non-Singularity in D.23: In D.23, if whole $AB = AE + EB$ has a given ratio $r$ to whole $CD = CF + FD$, while $AE : CF = r_1$ and $EB : FD = r_2$ are given, the crucial Greek proviso μὴ τοὺς αὐτοὺς δέ ("yet not the same ratios", $r_1 \neq r_2$) is the exact classical statement of matrix non-singularity! Algebraically, $(r_1 - r) CF + (r_2 - r) FD = 0$; if $r_1 = r_2 = r$, the two equations collapse into one ($0 = 0$) and $CF : FD$ is indeterminate (ἄτακτον), whereas $r_1 \neq r_2$ forces $CF : FD = (r - r_2) : (r_1 - r)$ uniquely. Bartolomeo Zamberti's 1505 Latin translation omitted the negation non, rendering the theorem false until Claude Hardy (1625) and David Gregory (1703) restored the Greek condition.

  • Continued Proportion & Quadratic Root Extraction in D.24: D.24 proves that if $A : B = B : C$ and $A : C$ is given, then $A : B$ is given ($A/B = \sqrt{A/C}$). Notably, Unit 1 here steps from general magnitudes to straight lines (εὐθεῖαι) so that [VI.13] can construct the geometric mean $E = \sqrt{D \cdot F}$. Late Byzantine manuscripts appended an alternative proof (Ἄλλως, relegated by Menge to the Appendix) that tried to dispense with squares, and Isaac Newton cited a similar shortcut in Principia Book I, Lemma XVII; Simson (pp. 459–460) demonstrated that the scholiast’s Ἄλλως tacitly begged the question, vindicating Euclid’s authentic proof.
