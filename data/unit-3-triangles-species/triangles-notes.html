Unit 3: Triangles & Polygons Given in Species (Propositions 39–55) — Philological & Critical Notes¶

1. Triangle Rigidity modulo Similarity & Petersen’s Method of Similarity (D.39–D.42)¶

Unit 3 shifts from position in the plane to intrinsic shape—figures given in species ([Data Def. 3]), i.e., determined up to similarity (angles given and pairwise side-ratios given). Propositions D.39–D.42 lift the congruence and similarity theorems of Books I and VI into constructive shape-determination operators:

  • D.39 (SSS $\to$ species, via [I.22] & [I.8]);

  • D.40 (AAA / AA $\to$ species, via [I.32] & [VI.4]);

  • D.41 (SAS-ratio $\to$ species, via [VI.6]);

  • D.42 (SSS-ratios $\to$ species, via [VI.5]).

Notice Euclid’s constructive technique throughout: he sets out an arbitrary reference segment $DE$ given in position and magnitude, builds a concrete prototype $\triangle DEF$ whose vertices are determined in position by Unit 2, reads off its given angles and ratios, and transfers species to $\triangle ABC$ by Book VI similarity. This is the exact archetype of Petersen's Method of Similarity (1879, Chapter II).

2. Unambiguous Right-Triangle HL (D.43) vs. General SSA Bifurcation (D.44)¶

The contrast between D.43 and D.44 (illustrated interactively in data_03_triangle_species.svg) showcases Euclid’s command of diorismos:

  • In D.43, $\angle BAC = 90^\circ$ is opposite the hypotenuse $BC$, while the ratio $BC : BA$ ($> 1$ by [I.19]) is given. Since the remaining angle $\angle ACB$ is necessarily acute ([I.17]), inscribing $DG$ in the Thales semicircle $DGE$ ([III.31]) yields a single, unambiguous species ([VI.7]).

  • In D.44, $\angle ABC$ is given while the ratio $AB : AC$ of the sides about another angle $\angle BAC$ is given (SSA). When $AC < AB$ ($AC \ge AB \sin B$), the circle $(A, AC)$ cuts ray $BC$ in two distinct points $C_1, C_2$ symmetric across the altitude foot $P$, with $\angle AC_1 B + \angle AC_2 B = 180^\circ$! Euclid’s proof drops the altitude $AD \perp BC$, reduces $\triangle ADC$ to D.43, and handles the acute and obtuse branches separately (χωρὶς δὲ τοῦ συμπτώματος), matching [VI.7].

3. Angle-Bisector Harmonic Reduction (D.45–D.46) vs. Simson’s Construction¶

Propositions D.45 and D.46 determine the species of $\triangle ABC$ from one angle and the ratio $(BA + AC) : BC$ of the sum of two sides to the third side:

  • Euclid’s Proof via Elements VI.3: Bisecting $\angle BAC$ by $AD$ gives $BA : AC = BD : DC$ ([VI.3]), whence alternando and componendo $(BA + AC) : BC = BA : BD$! In D.46 (where $\angle ABC$ is given), $\angle ABD$ lies between $BA$ and $BD$, so D.41 applies immediately without ambiguity. In D.45 (where $\angle BAC$ is given), $\angle BAD = \frac{1}{2}\angle BAC$ is opposite $BD$; since $BD < BC$ and $\angle ADB = \angle DAC + \angle C > \angle BAD$, $\angle ABD$ can actually take two supplementary-like configurations only up to swapping $AB$ and $AC$!

  • Simson’s Isosceles-Extension Proof & Added Prop. L: Simson (Props. XLVIII–L, pp. 394–397, 463) noted that extending $CA$ to $E$ with $AE = AB$ makes $EC = BA + AC$ directly and $\angle AEB = \frac{1}{2}\angle BAC$, reducing D.45 (Simson XLVIII) cleanly to D.44 on $\triangle EBC$ and inspiring Simson’s companion Prop. L for the difference of the sides $(BA - AC) : BC$ (via cutting off $AE' = AB$ internally).

4. Polygon Triangulation & Quadratic Area-to-Side Extraction (D.47–D.55)¶

The second half of Unit 3 (D.47–D.55) links 1D linear segments to 2D rectilinear areas:

  • D.47 proves that every species-given $n$-gon triangulates into $(n-2)$ species-given triangles by ear-clipping diagonals (D.41).

  • D.48–D.51 establish that any two species-given figures on the same or ratio-given segments have a given area ratio, culminating in D.52 (species-given figure on a magnitude-given segment is given in area, calibrated against the unit square $AB^2$).

  • D.54 and D.55 invert the quadratic scaling map $S \propto x^2$! Using D.24 (which extracted the square root of a ratio via the geometric mean [VI.13]), D.54 deduces linear side-ratios from area-ratios, and D.55 proves the grand converse of D.52: any figure given both in species and in magnitude has all its sides given in magnitude—the exact analytical counterpart of [Elements VI.25] (constructing a figure similar to one given figure and equal in area to another).
