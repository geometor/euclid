Unit 6: Hyperbolic/Elliptic Systems, Circles, Chords & Angle Bisectors (Propositions 84–94) — Philological & Critical Notes¶

1. Canonical Vieta Systems (D.84–D.85) & the Biquadratic Theorem (D.86)¶

Unit 6 opens (D.84–D.86) with the three most famous algebraic propositions in the Data—solving simultaneous two-variable quadratic systems:

  1. Product & Difference (D.84): $x y \sin\\theta = S$ and $y - x = d \implies$ apply $S$ to $d$ with species-given rhombus excess (D.59). When $\\theta = 90^\circ$ and $S = d^2$, $x(x + d) = d^2$ yields the golden ratio $y/x = \phi$!

  2. Product & Sum (D.85): $x y \sin\\theta = S$ and $x + y = s \implies$ apply $S$ to $s$ with species-given rhombus defect (D.58), subject to $S \le \frac{1}{4}s^2 \sin\\theta$.

  3. Product & Affine Ratio of Squares (D.86): $x y \sin\\theta = S$ and $y^2 - C = k x^2$. In 1703 David Gregory (Praefatio) suspected the Greek text of D.86 might be corrupt; Robert Simson (pp. 467–468) brilliantly vindicated the integrity of Euclid’s biquadratic proof (which completes the square via $y^2 - C = y(y - d)$ and $(2y - d)^2 = 4y(y - d) + d^2$, [II.8]) and added Simson Prop. LXXXVIII covering the sum-of-squares system $x y = S,\; x^2 + y^2 = C$.

2. Chord Trigonometry (D.87–D.89) & Power of a Point (D.90–D.92)¶

Propositions D.87–D.89 establish the constructive equivalence between inscribed angles and chord lengths in circles given in magnitude or position (see data_06_circle_angle_locus.svg). In Book I of the Syntaxis (Almagest), Claudius Ptolemy builds his entire table of chords (Πραγματεία τῶν ἐν κύκλῳ εὐθειῶν) on this foundation alongside D.93.

Propositions D.90–D.92 reformulate [Elements III.17], [III.35], and [III.36] as the invariance of the power of a given point $|OP^2 - R^2|$ with respect to a position-given circle: every secant from an external given point (D.91) or chord through an internal given point (D.92) cuts off segments whose rectangle $PA \cdot PB$ is given in magnitude—the invariant engine behind Apollonius's Tangencies and Plane Loci as well as modern radical-axis geometry (Altshiller-Court 1952).

3. The Crown Jewels (D.93–D.94) & Earl Stanhope’s and Matthew Stewart’s Theorems¶

The treatise culminates in two virtuoso cyclic-similarity theorems (D.93–D.94, with D.93 rendered interactively in data_07_prop93_bisector.svg):

  • Euclid’s D.93 & Earl Stanhope’s Exterior-Bisector Theorem (`Simson Prop. XCVIII [F]`): When a given chord $BC$ cuts off a segment admitting given $\angle BAC$ in a magnitude-given circle, and the interior bisector $AD$ of $\angle BAC$ meets $BC$ at $E$ and the opposite arc at $D$, Euclid proves both that $(BA + AC) : AD = BC : BD$ is given in ratio and that $(BA + AC) \cdot ED = BD^2$ is given in magnitude! In his 1762/1806 edition (Prop. XCVIII & p. 469), Simson paired D.93 with its dual communicated to him by Philip Stanhope, 2nd Earl Stanhope (Earl Stanhope): if instead the exterior angle at $A$ is bisected by chord $AD'$ meeting $BC$ produced at $E'$ and the circle at $D'$, then the difference $|BA - AC|$ has a given ratio to $AD'$, and $|BA - AC| \cdot E'D' = BD'^2$ is given in magnitude!

  • Euclid’s D.94 & Matthew Stewart’s Generalization (`Simson Prop. C`): Immediately following D.94, Simson (Prop. C & pp. 469–470) published a remarkable generalization discovered by his pupil and colleague Matthew Stewart (author of Some General Theorems of Considerable Use in the Higher Parts of Mathematics, 1746), showing how fixed lines and chords inflected in a given circle preserve given combinations of products and squares—a direct bridge from Alexandrian Data to eighteenth-century Scottish synthetic geometry.
