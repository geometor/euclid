Commentary on Book VII Definition 9
===================================

Back to :ref:`Book VII Definitions <VII.def>`.

᾿Αρτιάκις δὲ :term:`περισσός <odd-number>` ἐστιν ὁ ὑπὸ ἀρτίου :term:`ἀριθμοῦ <number>` μετρούμενος κατὰ περισσὸν :term:`ἀριθμόν <number>`.

Euclid uses the term :term:`*even-times odd* <even-times-odd-number>` (:term:`ἀρτιάκις περισσός <even-times-odd-number>`), whereas :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` and the others make it one word, :term:`*even-odd* <even-times-odd-number>` (ἀρτιοπέριττος). According to the stricter definition given by the latter (I. 9, 1), the :term:`*even-odd* <even-times-odd-number>` number is related to the :term:`*even-times even* <even-times-even-number>` as the other extreme. It is such a number as, when once halved, leaves as quotient an odd number; that is, it is of the form :math:`2(2m+1)`. :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` sets the even-odd numbers out as follows,

.. math::

   6,\ 10,\ 14,\ 18,\ 22,\ 26,\ 30,\text{ etc.}

In this case, as :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` observes, any part, or submultiple, is called by a name *not* corresponding in kind to its actual value (:term:`δύναμις <dynamis>`) in units. Thus,
in the case of 18, the :math:`\frac{1}{2}` part is called after the even number 2, but its value is the odd number 9, and the :math:`\frac{1}{3}\text{rd}` part is called after the odd number 3, while its *value* is the even number 6, and so on.

The third class of even numbers according to the strict subdivision is the :term:`*odd-even* <odd-times-even-number>` (:term:`περισσάρτιος <odd-times-even-number>`). Numbers are of this class when they can be halved twice or more times successively, but the quotient left when they can no longer be halved is an odd number and not unity. They are therefore of the form :math:`2^{n+1}(2m+1)`, where :math:`n`, :math:`m` are integers. They are, so to say, intermediate between, or a mixture of, the extreme classes :term:`*even-times even* <even-times-even-number>` and :term:`*even-odd* <even-times-odd-number>`, for the following reasons. (1) Their subdivision by 2 proceeds for some way like that of the even-times even, but ends in the way that the division of the even-odd by 2 ends. (2) The numbers after which submultiples are called and their *value* (:term:`δύναμις <dynamis>`) in units may be both of one kind, i.e. both odd or both even (as in the case of the even-times even), or again may be one odd and one even as in the case of the even-odd. For example 24 is an odd-even number; the :math:`\frac{1}{4}\text{th}`, :math:`\frac{1}{12}\text{th}`, :math:`\frac{1}{6}\text{th}` or :math:`\frac{1}{2}` parts of it are even, but the :math:`\frac{1}{3}\text{rd}` part of it, or 8, is even, and the :math:`\frac{1}{8}\text{th}` part of it, or 3, is odd. (3) :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` shows (I. 10, 6—9) how to form all the numbers of the odd-even class. Set out two lines (:math:`a`) of odd numbers beginning with 3, (:math:`b`) of even-times even numbers beginning with 4, thus:

.. math::

   \begin{array}{ll}
   (a) & 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 9,\ 11,\ 13,\ 15\text{ etc.} \\
   (b) & 4,\ 8,\ 16,\ 32,\ 64,\ 128,\ 256\text{ etc.}
   \end{array}

Now multiply each of the first numbers into each of the second row. Let the products of one of the first into all the second set make horizontal rows; we then get the rows

.. math::

   \begin{array}{l}
   12,\ 24,\ 48,\ 96,\ 192,\ 384,\ 768\text{ etc.} \\
   20,\ 40,\ 80,\ 160,\ 320,\ 640,\ 1280\text{ etc.} \\
   28,\ 56,\ 112,\ 224,\ 448,\ 896,\ 1792\text{ etc.} \\
   36,\ 72,\ 144,\ 288,\ 576,\ 1152,\ 2304\text{ etc.}
   \end{array}

and so on.

Now, says :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>`, you will be surprised to see (φανήσεταί σοι θαυμαστῶς) that (:math:`a`) the *vertical* rows have the property of the :term:`*even-odd* <even-times-odd-number>` series, 6, 10, 14, 18, 22 etc., viz. that, if an odd number of successive numbers be taken, the middle number is half the sum of the extremes, and if an even number, the two middle numbers together are equal to the sum of the extremes, (:math:`b`) the *horizontal* rows have the property of the :term:`*even-times even* <even-times-even-number>` series 4, 8, 16 etc., viz. that the product of the extremes of any number of successive terms is equal, if their number be odd, to the square of the middle term, or, if their number be even, to the product of the two middle terms.

Let us now return to Euclid. His 9th definition states that an :term:`*even-times odd* <even-times-odd-number>` number is a number which, when divided by an even number, gives an odd number as quotient. Following this definition in our text comes a 10th definition which defines an :term:`*odd-times even* <odd-times-even-number>` number; this is stated to be a number which, when divided by an odd number, gives an even number as quotient. According to these definitions any :term:`*even-times odd* <even-times-odd-number>` number would also be :term:`*odd-times even* <odd-times-even-number>`, and, from the fact that :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` notes this, we may fairly conclude that he found Def. 10 as well as Def. 9 in the text of Euclid which he used. But, if both definitions are genuine, the enunciations of IX. 33 and IX. 34 as we have them present difficulties. IX. 33 says that “If a number have its half odd, it is even-times odd *only*”; but, on the assumption that
both definitions are genuine, this would not be true, for the number would be :term:`*odd-times even* <odd-times-even-number>` as well. IX. 34 says that "If a number neither be one of those which are continually doubled from 2, nor have its half odd, it is both even-times even and even-times odd." The term :term:`*odd-times even* <odd-times-even-number>` (:term:`περισσάκις ἄρτιος <odd-times-even-number>`) not occurring in these propositions, nor anywhere else after the definition, that definition becomes superfluous. :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` however (p. 24, 7—14) quotes these enunciations differently. In the first he has instead of "even-times odd only" the words "*both even-times odd and odd-times even*"; and, in the second, for "both even-times even and even-times odd" he has "is both even-times even and at the same time even-times odd and even-times even." In both cases therefore "odd-times even" is added to the enunciation as :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` had it; the words cannot have been added by :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` himself because he himself does not use the term :term:`*odd-times even* <odd-times-even-number>`, but the one word :term:`*odd-even* <odd-times-even-number>` (:term:`περισσάρτιος <odd-times-even-number>`). In order to get over the difficulties involved by Def. 10 and these differences of reading we have practically to choose between (1) accepting :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>`' reading in all three places and (2) adhering to the reading of our MSS. in IX. 33, 34 and rejecting Def. 10 altogether as an interpolation. Now the readings of our text of IX. 33, 34 are those of the Vatican MS. and the Theonine MSS. as well; hence they must go back to a time before Theon, and must therefore be almost as old as those of :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>`. :person:`Heiberg <heiberg>` considers it improbable that Euclid would wish to maintain a pointless distinction between :term:`*even-times odd* <even-times-odd-number>` and :term:`*odd-times even* <odd-times-even-number>`, and on the whole concludes that Def. 10 was first interpolated by some ignorant person who did not notice the difference between the Euclidean and Pythagorean classification, but merely noticed the absence of a definition of :term:`*odd-times even* <odd-times-even-number>` and fabricated one as a companion to the other. When this was done, it would be easy to see that the statement in IX. 33 that the number referred to is "*even-times odd only*" was not strictly true, and that the addition of the words "and odd-times even" was necessary in IX. 33 and IX. 34 as well.
