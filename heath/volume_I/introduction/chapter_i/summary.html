Chapter I Summary and Outline: Euclid and the Traditions About Him¶

Executive Summary¶

Sir Thomas L. Heath opens his monumental introduction to Euclid’s Elements by investigating the scarce historical data and persistent legends surrounding the life, personality, and chronology of Euclid of Alexandria (c. 300 B.C.).

Drawing on the crucial summary preserved in Proclus’ Commentary on Euclid Book I, Heath establishes:

  1. Chronological Anchor: Euclid flourished under the reign of Ptolemy I Soter (306–283 B.C.), standing intermediate between the direct pupils of Plato (Plato d. 347/6 B.C.) and Archimedes (287–212 B.C.).

  2. Athenian Training: Euclid almost certainly received his mathematical education in Athens among the Platonists, where the earlier element-writers (Hippocrates of Chios, Leon, Theudius) had taught.

  3. Platonism vs. Mathematical Purpose: Heath refutes Proclus’ Neoplatonic claim that the entire purpose of the Elements was teleologically directed toward the construction of the five Platonic “cosmic” solids, arguing that the Elements were written as a universal foundation for elementary geometry.

  4. Alexandrian School: Euclid established the preeminent mathematical academy at Alexandria, where Apollonius of Perga later studied with Euclid’s successors.

  5. Debunking Medieval and Arabic Fables:

    • Euclid of Megara Confusion: Medieval authors (from Valerius Maximus and Theodorus Metochita to Campanus, Tartaglia, and Candalla) universally confused Euclid the geometer with Euclid of Megara, the Socratic philosopher (c. 400 B.C.). This error was finally refuted by Constantinus Lascaris (d. c. 1493) and Federico Commandino (1572).

    • Arabic Biographies and Fables: Accounts in Ibn an-Nadīm’s Kitāb al-Fihrist, al-Qifṭī’s Ta’rīkh al-Ḥukamā, and Naṣīraddīn aṭ-Ṭūsī alleging that Euclid was born at Tyre or Damascus, was the grandson of Zenarchus, or was merely an editor/plagiarist of a 15-book treatise by “Apollonius the carpenter,” are dismantled by Heath as philological misunderstandings of Hypsicles’ Greek preface to Book XIV.

Detailed Outline¶

§ 1. Proclus’ Summary and Chronology (pp. 1–2)¶

  • Analysis of Proclus (ed. Friedlein, p. 68, 6–20).

  • Euclid’s synthesis: collecting Eudoxus’ theorems, perfecting Theaetetus’, and supplying rigorous demonstrations.

  • The famous anecdote: Ptolemy asking if there is a shorter path; Euclid’s reply that “there is no royal road to geometry” (compared with Stobaeus’ version attributed to Alexander and Menaechmus).

  • Historical synchronization: Plato (d. 347/6) $\rightarrow$ Euclid (fl. c. 300) $\rightarrow$ Archimedes (287–212) $\rightarrow$ Eratosthenes (c. 284–204).

§ 2. School and Philosophical Affiliation (pp. 2–3)¶

  • Probable Athenian education among the Academy’s geometric circle.

  • Proclus’ Neoplatonist bias asserting Euclid’s goal was the five Platonic figures.

  • Foundation of the mathematical school at Alexandria (attested by Pappus VII, p. 678).

  • Pappus’ defense of Euclid’s character against Apollonius’ preface to the Conics (the four-line locus and Aristaeus).

  • The anecdote in Stobaeus: Euclid giving threepence (ὀβολοί) to the student who asked what profit was to be gained from studying geometry.

§ 3. The Medieval Confusion: Euclid of Megara (pp. 3–4)¶

  • Ancient confusion initiated in Valerius Maximus VIII. 12.

  • 14th-century entrenchment: Theodorus Metochita and Paris MS. Euclidis philosophi Socratici liber elementorum.

  • Early printed editions propagating the error: Campanus (1482), Tartaglia (1565), Candalla (1566).

  • Philological recovery: Constantinus Lascaris (d. 1493) and Federico Commandino (1572).

  • The false Sicilian birthplace (Gela) traced to Diogenes Laertius’ note on Alexander Polyhistor.

§ 4. Arabic Traditions and Philological Deconstruction (pp. 4–6)¶

  • Arabic biographical accounts in al-Qifṭī (Ta’rīkh al-Ḥukamā), Casiri, and Ḥājjī Khalfa.

  • Origin of the Syrian/Tyrian attribution and the “Academy door” inscription legend.

  • The fable of “Apollonius the carpenter” in the Fihrist, al-Kindī, Qāḍīzāde ar-Rūmī, and Naṣīraddīn aṭ-Ṭūsī.

  • Heath’s forensic philological resolution:

    • Hypsicles’ Preface to Book XIV quoted in full (Basilides of Tyre, Protarchus, comparison of dodecahedron and icosahedron).

    • How Arabian translators confused Greek proper names (Basilides mistaken for βασιλεύς / king; Tyrian origin of Basilides transferred to Euclid).

    • Greek etymological corruptions: the Organon read as instrumentum musicum, and Uclides etymologized as Ucli (“key”) + Dis (“measure/geometry”).
