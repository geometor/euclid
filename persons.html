People¶

Biographical Register of Ancient Geometers, Commentators & Modern Editors

This unified register presents the principal historical authors, commentators, translators, and critical editors who created and transmitted Euclid’s Elements and Data, with cross-references to the dedicated apparatus indices in each Book Codex.

Authors, Translators & Editors¶

Euclid of Alexandria (fl. c. 300 BCE)¶

Geometer · Author of Elements, Data, Phaenomena & Optics

Alexandrian mathematician called ὁ Στοιχειωτής (“the Writer of Elements”) by Proclus and Marinus of Neapolis. Active during the reign of Ptolemy I Soter (323–285 BCE), Euclid founded the mathematical school of Alexandria. His monumental Elements (Στοιχεῖα) unified centuries of Hellenistic mathematical discovery—from the Pythagorean theory of numbers and Eudoxus’s theory of proportion to Theaetetus’s classification of incommensurable lines and regular polyhedra—into an axiomatic-deductive system that became the paradigm of rigorous demonstration for two millennia. Alongside the synthetic Elements, Euclid composed the Data (Δεδομένα) as the foundational manual of geometric analysis and functional determinacy, establishing what magnitudes, ratios, and figures are uniquely determined when others are given.

  • Primary Codices: Elements (Books I–XIII) · Data (Units 0–6)

  • Apparatus Details: Elements Person Index · Data Person Index

Sir Thomas Little Heath (1861 – 1940)¶

British Classical Scholar, Mathematician & Historian of Greek Mathematics

Fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge, and Permanent Secretary to HM Treasury. Heath was the preeminent English historian of Greek mathematics, producing the definitive English critical translations and mathematical commentaries on Euclid (The Thirteen Books of Euclid’s Elements, 1908; 2nd ed. rev., 1926), Archimedes (The Works of Archimedes, 1897; The Method of Archimedes, 1912), Apollonius of Perga (Treatise on Conic Sections, 1896), and Aristarchus of Samos (1913), culminating in his masterwork, A History of Greek Mathematics (Clarendon Press, 1921). His 1926 Cambridge edition of the Elements forms the textual foundation of the GEOMETOR diplomatic reconstruction.

  • Primary Contribution: Elements English Translation & Commentary

  • Apparatus Details: Elements Person Index · Data Person Index

Johan Ludvig Heiberg (1854 – 1928)¶

Danish Classical Philologist & Critical Editor of Greek Mathematical Texts

Professor of Classical Philology at the University of Copenhagen. Heiberg established the modern critical Greek text of the ancient mathematicians, discovering the famous Archimedes Palimpsest in Constantinople in 1906. Between 1883 and 1888, he edited the definitive five-volume Greek critical edition of Euclid’s Elements (Euclidis Elementa, Teubner), identifying Codex Vaticanus Graecus 190 (P) as the superior pre-Theonine archetype and purging the accretions introduced by Theon of Alexandria in the fourth century CE. He co-directed the monumental Euclidis Opera Omnia series with Heinrich Menge.

  • Primary Contribution: Critical Greek text of the Elements (Vols. I–V of Euclidis Opera Omnia)

  • Apparatus Details: Elements Person Index · Data Person Index

Heinrich Menge (1838 – 1904)¶

German Classical Philologist & Critical Editor of Euclid’s Data

Co-director with J. L. Heiberg of the Teubner Euclidis Opera Omnia. In 1896, Menge published Volume VI, Euclidis Data cum commentario Marini et scholiis antiquis, establishing the authoritative 94-proposition non-Theonine Greek text of Euclid’s Data directly from Vat. gr. 190, accompanied by a definitive Latin translation, critical apparatus, ancient scholia, and the complete text of Marinus’s fifth-century Protheoria. His Teubner pagination serves as the primary folio milestone track throughout the GEOMETOR Data edition.

  • Primary Contribution: Critical Greek text and apparatus of Data

  • Apparatus Details: Data Person Index

Robert Simson (1687 – 1768)¶

Scottish Mathematician & Professor of Mathematics at the University of Glasgow

Pioneering restorationist of ancient Greek geometry who dedicated nearly five decades to recovering the original purity of Euclid and Apollonius. His landmark edition, The Elements of Euclid… Also the Book of Euclid’s Data (Glasgow: Robert & Andrew Foulis, 1756 / 1762), rescued the Data from corrupted medieval Latin versions, providing brilliant critical corrections, restored propositions, and annotations that guided British geometry education for over a century and directly informed Heath’s and Menge’s recensions.

  • Primary Contribution: Critical reconstruction of Data and Latin/English commentaries on Elements

  • Apparatus Details: Elements Person Index · Data Person Index

Marinus of Neapolis (c. 440 – c. 495 CE)¶

Neoplatonic Philosopher, Mathematician & Scholarch of the Academy

Samaritan-born philosopher from Flavia Neapolis (modern Nablus, Palestine) who studied in Athens under Proclus and succeeded him in 485 CE as Scholarch (Diadochos) of the Platonic Academy. Marinus authored the surviving biography of his master, Life of Proclus (Vita Procli), and the essential Protheoria (philosophical introduction) prefixed to Euclid’s Data. In the Protheoria, Marinus investigates the ontological and epistemological definition of the “given” (δεδομένον), synthesizes definitions from Apollonius and Ptolemy, and formulates his celebrated canon: being given requires being both knowable (gnōrimon) and procurable (porimon).

  • Primary Contribution: Philosophical Protheoria (§§ 1–18) in Data Unit 0

  • Apparatus Details: Elements Person Index · Data Person Index

Book Codex Registers¶

Detailed biographical entries, work citations, and locus occurrences for all historical commentators and scholars cited across both treatises are maintained in the respective codex apparatus indices:

  • Elements: Index of Persons — Comprehensive register of over 100 ancient, medieval, and modern mathematicians and historians cited across Volumes I–III of Heath’s edition.

  • Data: Index of Persons & Commentators — Dedicated index of Hellenistic geometers, Neoplatonic philosophers, Renaissance humanists, and modern locus systematizers cited in the Data.
