#### 4. The cissoid.

This curve is assumed to be the same as that by means of which, according to Eutocius (*Comm. on Archimedes*, III. p. 66 sqq.), Diocles in his book περὶ πυρείων (*On burning-glasses*) solved the problem of doubling the cube. It is the locus of points which he found by the following construction. Let <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span> be diameters at right angles in a circle with centre <span data-ref="0178.g1:O">$O$</span>.

Let <span data-ref="0178.g1:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0178.g1:F">$F$</span> be points on the quadrants <span data-ref="0178.g1:< B O C )">$BC$</span>, <span data-ref="0178.g1:< B O A )">$BA$</span> respectively such that the arcs <span data-ref="0178.g1:< B O E )">$BE$</span>, <span data-ref="0178.g1:< B O F )">$BF$</span> are equal.

Draw <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span>, <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ F H /">$FH$</span> perpendicular to <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ C A /">$CA$</span>. Join <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ A E /">$AE$</span>, and let <span data-ref="0178.g1:P">$P$</span> be its intersection with <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ F H /">$FH$</span>.

The cissoid is the locus of all the points <span data-ref="0178.g1:P">$P$</span> corresponding to different positions of <span data-ref="0178.g1:E">$E$</span> on the quadrant <span data-ref="0178.g1:< B O C )">$BC$</span> and of <span data-ref="0178.g1:F">$F$</span> at an equal distance from <span data-ref="0178.g1:B">$B$</span> along the arc <span data-ref="0178.g1:< B O A )">$BA$</span>.

<span data-ref="0178.g1:A">$A$</span> is the point on the curve corresponding to the position <span data-ref="0178.g1:C">$C$</span> for the point <span data-ref="0178.g1:E">$E$</span>, and <span data-ref="0178.g1:B">$B$</span> the point on the curve corresponding to the position of <span data-ref="0178.g1:E">$E$</span> in which it coincides with <span data-ref="0178.g1:B">$B$</span>.

It is easy to see that the curve extends in the direction <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> beyond <span data-ref="0178.g1:B">$B$</span>, and that <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ C K /">$CK$</span> drawn perpendicular to <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ C A /">$CA$</span> is an asymptote. It may be regarded also as having a branch <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span> symmetrical with <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, and, beyond <span data-ref="0178.g1:D">$D$</span>, approaching <span data-ref="0178.g1:[ K C ]">$KC$</span> produced as asymptote.

![Cissoid of Diocles](0178.g1.svg)

If <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ O A /">$OA$</span>, <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ O D /">$OD$</span> are coordinate axes, the equation of the curve is obviously
$$y^2 (a + x) = (a - x)^3,$$
where <span data-ref="0178.g1:/ O A /">$a$</span> is the radius of the circle.

There is a cusp at <span data-ref="0178.g1:A">$A$</span>, and it agrees with this that Proclus should say (p. 126, 24) that “cissoidal lines converging to one point like the leaves of ivy—for this is the origin of their name—form an angle.” He makes the slight correction (p. 128, 5) that it is not two *parts* of a curve, but *one* curve, which in this case makes an angle.

But what is surprising is that Proclus seems to have no idea of the curve passing outside the circle and having an asymptote, for he several times speaks of it as a *closed* curve (forming a figure and including an area) : cf. p. 152, 7, “the plane (area) cut off by the cissoidal line has one bounding (line), but it has not in it a centre such that all (straight lines drawn to the curve) from it are equal.” It would appear as if Proclus regarded the cissoid as formed by the *four* symmetrical cissoidal arcs shown in the figure.
