DEFINITIONS 10, 11, 12.
-----------------------

10. Ὅταν δὲ :term:`εὐθεῖα <straight-line>` ἐπ’ εὐθεῖαν σταθεῖσα τὰς ἐφεξῆς :term:`γωνίας <angle>` ἴσας ἀλλήλαις ποιῇ, ὀρθὴ ἑκατέρα τῶν ἴσων γωνιῶν ἐστί, καὶ ἡ ἐφεστηκυῖα :term:`εὐθεῖα <straight-line>` :term:`κάθετος <kathetos>` καλεῖται, ἐφ’ ἣν ἐφέστηκεν.

11. Ἀμβλεῖα :term:`γωνία <angle>` ἐστὶν ἡ μείζων ὀρθῆς.

12. Ὀξεῖα δὲ ἡ ἐλάσσων ὀρθῆς.

10. *When a straight line set up on a straight line makes the adjacent angles equal to one another, each of the equal angles is right, and the straight line standing on the other is called a perpendicular to that on which it stands.*

11. *An obtuse angle is an angle greater than a right angle.*

12. *An acute angle is an angle less than a right angle.*

----

ἐφεξῆς is the regular term for *adjacent* angles, meaning literally “(next) in order.” I do not find the term used in :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` of *angles*, but he explains its meaning in such passages as :cite:`*Physics* VI. 1, 231 b 8 <aristotle:phys:VI.1.231b8>`: “those things are (next) in order which have nothing of the same kind (συγγενές) between them.”

:term:`κάθετος <kathetos>`, *perpendicular*, means literally *let fall*: the full expression is *perpendicular straight line*, as we see from the enunciation of Eucl. I. 11, and the notion is that of a straight line let fall *upon the surface of the earth*, a *plumb-line*. :person:`Proclus <proclus>` (:cite:`p. 283, 9 <proclus:in-euclid:283.9>`) tells us that in ancient times the perpendicular was called *gnomon-wise* (κατὰ γνώμονα), because the gnomon (an upright stick) was set up at right angles to the horizon.

The three kinds of angles are among the things which according to the Platonic Socrates (:cite:`*Republic* VI. 510 C <plato:rep:VI.510C>`) the geometer assumes and argues from, declining to give any account of them because they are obvious. :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` discusses the *priority* of the right angle in comparison with the acute (:cite:`*Metaph.* 1084 b 7 <aristotle:met:1084b7>`): in one way the right angle is prior, i.e. *in being defined* (ὅτι ὥρισται) and by its *notion* (τῷ λόγῳ), in another way the acute is prior, i.e. as being a *part*, and because the right angle is divided into acute angles; the acute angle is prior as *matter*, the right angle in respect of *form*; cf. also :cite:`*Metaph.* 1035 b 6 <aristotle:met:1035b6>`, “the notion of the right angle is not divided into
