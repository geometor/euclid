:title: Postulate 3 and Commentary on Postulates 2, 3

Suppose that two straight lines :g:`$AC$ <0213.g1:/ A C />`, :g:`$AD$ <0213.g1:/ A D />` have a common segment :g:`$AB$ <0213.g1:/ A B />`, and let :g:`$BE$ <0213.g1:/ B E />` be drawn at right angles to :g:`$AC$ <0213.g1:/ A C />`.
Then the angle :g:`$EBC$ <0213.g1:< E B C )>` is right.
If then the angle :g:`$EBD$ <0213.g1:< E B D )>` is also right, the two angles will be equal: which is impossible.
If the angle :g:`$EBD$ <0213.g1:< E B D )>` is not right, draw :g:`$BF$ <0213.g1:/ B F />` at right angles to :g:`$AD$ <0213.g1:/ A D />`; therefore the angle :g:`$FBA$ <0213.g1:< F B A )>` is right.
But the angle :g:`$EBA$ <0213.g1:< E B A )>` is right.
Therefore the angles :g:`$EBA$ <0213.g1:< E B A )>`, :g:`$FBA$ <0213.g1:< F B A )>` are equal: which is impossible.

.. picture:: 0213.g1.svg
   :alt: Zeno and Posidonius on straight lines with a common segment

:person:`Zeno <zeno>` objected to this, says :person:`Proclus <proclus>`, because it assumed the later proposition I. 11 for its proof. :person:`Posidonius <posidonius>` said that there was no trace of such a proof to be found in the text-books of Elements, and that it was only invented by :person:`Zeno <zeno>` for the purpose of slandering contemporary geometers. :person:`Posidonius <posidonius>` maintains further that even this proof has something to be said for it. There must be some straight line at right angles to each of the two straight lines :g:`$AC$ <0213.g1:/ A C />`, :g:`$AD$ <0213.g1:/ A D />` (the very definition of right angles assumes this): "*suppose then it happens to be the straight line we have set up.*" Here then we have an ancient instance of a defence of *hypothetical construction*, but in such apologetic terms ("it is possible to say *something* even for this proof") that we may conclude that in general it would not have been accepted by geometers of that time as a legitimate means of proving a proposition.
:person:`Todhunter <todhunter>` proposed to deduce that *two straight lines cannot have a common segment* from I. 13. But this will not serve either, since, as before mentioned, the assumption is really required for I. 4.
It is best to make it a postulate.

----

POSTULATE 3.
------------

**Καὶ παντὶ κέντρῳ καὶ διαστήματι κύκλον γράφεσθαι.**

*To describe a circle with any centre and distance.*

----

In this case Euclid's text has the passive of the verb: "a circle can be drawn"; :person:`Proclus <proclus>` however has the active (γράψαι) as :person:`Euclid <euclid>` has in the first two Postulates.
*Distance*, διαστήματι. This word, meaning "distance" quite generally (cf. Arist. :cite:`*Metaph.* 1055 a 9 <aristotle:met:1055a9>` "it is between extremities that distance is greatest," *ibid.* 1056 a 36 "things which have something between them, that is, a certain distance"), and also "distance" in the sense of "dimension" (as in "space has three dimensions, length, breadth and depth," Arist. :cite:`*Physics* IV. 1, 209 a 4 <aristotle:phys:IV.1.209a4>`), was the regular word used for describing a circle with a certain *radius*, the idea being that each point of the circumference was at that *distance* from the centre (cf. Arist. *Meteorologica* III. 5, 376 b 8: "if a circle be drawn...with distance ΜΠ"). The Greeks had no word corresponding to *radius*: if they had to express it, they said "(straight lines) drawn from the centre" (αἱ ἐκ τοῦ :term:`κέντρου <centre>`, Eucl. III. Def. 1 and Prop. 26; *Meteorologica* II. 5, 362 b 1 has the full phrase αἱ ἐκ τοῦ :term:`κέντρου <centre>` ἀγόμεναι :term:`γραμμαί <line>`).
Mr Frankland observes that it would be remarkable if, unlike Postulates 1 and 2, this Postulate implied *merely* what it says, that a circle can be drawn with any centre and distance. We may regard it, if we please, as helping to the complete delineation of the Space which Euclid's geometry is to investigate formally. The Postulate has the effect of removing any restriction upon the size of the circle.
