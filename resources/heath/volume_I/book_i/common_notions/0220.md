IV. Ptolemy lastly enforces his conclusion that the straight lines will meet *on the side on which are the angles less than two right angles* by recurring to the *a fortiori* step in the foregoing proof.

Let the angles <span data-ref="0220.g1:< A F G )">$AFG$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:< C G F )">$CGF$</span> in the accompanying figure be together less than two right angles.

Therefore the angles <span data-ref="0220.g1:< B F G )">$BFG$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:< D G F )">$DGF$</span> are greater than two right angles.

We have proved that the straight lines are not non-secant.

If they meet, they must meet either towards <span data-ref="0220.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:C">$C$</span>, or towards <span data-ref="0220.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:D">$D$</span>.

(1) Suppose they meet towards <span data-ref="0220.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:D">$D$</span>, at <span data-ref="0220.g1:K">$K$</span>.

Then, since the angles <span data-ref="0220.g1:< A F G )">$AFG$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:< C G F )">$CGF$</span> are less than two right angles, and the angles <span data-ref="0220.g1:< A F G )">$AFG$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:< B F G )">$GFB$</span> are equal to two right angles, take away the common angle <span data-ref="0220.g1:< A F G )">$AFG$</span>, and

![Ptolemy's diagram showing lines AB, CD meeting towards B, D at K](0220.g1.svg)

the angle <span data-ref="0220.g1:< C G F )">$CGF$</span> is less than the angle <span data-ref="0220.g1:< B F G )">$BFG$</span>;

that is, the exterior angle of the triangle <span data-ref="0220.g1:< K F G >">$KFG$</span> is less than the interior and opposite angle <span data-ref="0220.g1:< B F G )">$BFG$</span>:

which is impossible.

Therefore <span data-ref="0220.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> do not meet towards <span data-ref="0220.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:D">$D$</span>.

(2) But they do meet, and therefore they must meet in one direction or the other:

therefore they meet towards <span data-ref="0220.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:C">$B$</span>, that is, on the side where are the angles less than two right angles.

The flaw in Ptolemy's argument is of course in the part of his proof of I. 29 which I have italicised. As Proclus says, he is not entitled to assume that, if <span data-ref="0220.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> are parallel, whatever is true of the interior angles on one side of <span data-ref="0220.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span> (i.e. that they are together equal to, greater than, or less than, two right angles) is necessarily true at the same time of the interior angles on the other side. Ptolemy justifies this by saying that <span data-ref="0220.g1:/ F A /">$FA$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:/ G C /">$GC$</span> are no more parallel in one direction than <span data-ref="0220.g1:/ F B /">$FB$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g1:/ G D /">$GD$</span> are in the other: which is equivalent to the assumption that *through any point only one parallel can be drawn to a given straight line*. That is, he assumes an equivalent of the very Postulate he is endeavouring to prove.

### Proclus.

Before passing to his own attempt at a proof, Proclus (p. 368, 26 sqq.) examines an ingenious argument (recalling somewhat the famous one about Achilles and the tortoise) which appeared to show that it was *impossible* for the lines described in the Postulate to meet.

Let <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> make with <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ A C /">$AC$</span> the angles <span data-ref="0220.g2:< B A C )">$BAC$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g2:< A C D )">$ACD$</span> together less than two right angles.

Bisect <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ A C /">$AC$</span> at <span data-ref="0220.g2:E">$E$</span> and along <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> respectively measure <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ C G /">$CG$</span> so that each is equal to <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span>.

Bisect <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ F G /">$FG$</span> at <span data-ref="0220.g2:H">$H$</span> and mark off <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ F K /">$FK$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ G L /">$GL$</span> each equal to <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ F H /">$FH$</span>; and so on.

![Diagram of Zeno-like bisection argument examined by Proclus](0220.g2.svg)

Then <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span>, <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ C G /">$CG$</span> will not meet at any point on <span data-ref="0220.g2:/ F G /">$FG$</span>; for, if that were the case, two sides of a triangle would be together equal to the third: which is impossible.
