(2) *Proof substituted later.*

If possible, let $2R + \alpha$ be the sum of the three angles of the triangle <span data-ref="0231.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span>, of which <span data-ref="0231.g1:< C A B )">$A$</span> is not greater than either of the others.

Bisect <span data-ref="0231.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> at <span data-ref="0231.g1:H">$H$</span>, and produce <span data-ref="0231.g1:/ A H /">$AH$</span> to <span data-ref="0231.g1:D">$D$</span>, making <span data-ref="0231.g1:/ H D /">$HD$</span> equal to <span data-ref="0231.g1:/ A H /">$AH$</span>; join <span data-ref="0231.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>.

![Diagram for Legendre's second proof of Theorem I](0231.g1.svg)

Then the triangles <span data-ref="0231.g1:< A H C >">$AHC$</span>, <span data-ref="0231.g1:< D H B >">$DHB$</span> are equal in all respects (I. 4); and the angles <span data-ref="0231.g1:< C A H )">$CAH$</span>, <span data-ref="0231.g1:< A C H )">$ACH$</span> are respectively equal to the angles <span data-ref="0231.g1:< B D H )">$BDH$</span>, <span data-ref="0231.g1:< D B H )">$DBH$</span>.

It follows that the sum of the angles of the triangle <span data-ref="0231.g1:< A B D >">$ABD$</span> is equal to the sum of the angles of the original triangle, i.e. to $2R + \alpha$.

And one of the angles <span data-ref="0231.g1:< D A B )">$DAB$</span>, <span data-ref="0231.g1:< B D H )">$ADB$</span> is either equal to or less than half the angle <span data-ref="0231.g1:< C A B )">$CAB$</span>.

Continuing the same construction with the triangle <span data-ref="0231.g1:< A B D >">$ADB$</span>, we find a third triangle in which the sum of the angles is still $2R + \alpha$, while one of them is equal to or less than <span data-ref="0231.g1:< C A B )">$(\angle CAB)/4$</span>.

Proceeding in this way, we arrive at a triangle in which the sum of the angles is $2R + \alpha$, and one of them is not greater than <span data-ref="0231.g1:< C A B )">$(\angle CAB)/2^n$</span>.

And, if $n$ is sufficiently large, this will be less than $\alpha$; in which case we should have a triangle in which two angles are together greater than two right angles: which is absurd.

Therefore $\alpha$ is equal to or less than zero.

(It will be noted that in both these proofs, as in Eucl. I. 16, it is taken for granted that *a straight line is infinite in length* and does not return into itself, which is not true under the Riemann hypothesis.)

II. On the assumption that the sum of the angles of a triangle is *less* than two right angles, *if a triangle is made up of two others, the "deficit" of the former is equal to the sum of the "deficits" of the others*.

In fact, if the sums of the angles of the component triangles are $2R - \alpha$, $2R - \beta$ respectively, the sum of the angles of the whole triangle is
$$(2R - \alpha) + (2R - \beta) - 2R = 2R - (\alpha + \beta).$$

III. *If the sum of the three angles of a triangle is equal to two right angles, the same is true of all triangles obtained by subdividing it by straight lines drawn from a vertex to meet the opposite side.*

Since the sum of the angles of the triangle <span data-ref="0231.g2:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> is equal to $2R$, if the sum of the angles of the triangle <span data-ref="0231.g2:< A B D >">$ABD$</span> were $2R - \alpha$, it would follow that the sum of the angles of the triangle <span data-ref="0231.g2:< A D C >">$ADC$</span> must be $2R + \alpha$, which is absurd (by I. above).
