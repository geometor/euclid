AXIOMS OR *COMMON NOTIONS*.
---------------------------

In a paper *Sur l'authenticité des axiomes d'Euclide* in the *Bulletin des sciences math. et astron.* 1884, p. 162 sq. (*Mémoires scientifiques*, II., pp. 48—63), Paul :person:`Tannery <tannery>` maintained that the *Common Notions* (including the first three) were not in Euclid's work but were interpolated later. The following are his main arguments. (1) If :person:`Euclid <euclid>` had set about distinguishing between indemonstrable principles (*a*) common to all demonstrative sciences and (*b*) peculiar to geometry, he would, says :person:`Tannery <tannery>`, certainly not have placed the common principles second and the special principles (the Postulates) first. (2) If the *Common Notions* are Euclid's, this designation of them must be his too; for he must have used *some* name to distinguish them from the Postulates and, if he had used another name, such as *Axioms*, it is impossible to imagine why that name was changed afterwards for a less suitable one. The word ἔννοια (*notion*), says :person:`Tannery <tannery>`, never signified a notion in the sense of a *proposition*, but a notion of some *object*; nor is it found in any technical sense in :person:`Plato <plato>` and :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>`. (3) Tannery's own view was that the formulation of the *Common Notions* dates from the time of :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>`, and that it was inspired by his work relating to the Elements (we know from :person:`Proclus <proclus>` that :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>` tried to prove the *Common Notions*). This idea, :person:`Tannery <tannery>` thought, was confirmed by a "fortunate coincidence" furnished by the occurrence of the word ἔννοια (*notion*) in a quotation by :person:`Proclus <proclus>` (:cite:`p. 100, 6 <proclus:in-euclid:100.6>`): "we shall agree with :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>` when he says that we have a *notion* (ἔννοιαν) of a line when we order the lengths, only, of roads or walls to be measured."

In reply to argument (1) that it is an unnatural order to place the purely geometrical Postulates first, and the *Common Notions*, which are not peculiar to geometry, last, it may be pointed out that it would surely have been a still more awkward arrangement to give the Definitions first and then to separate from them, by the interposition of the *Common Notions*, the Postulates, which are so closely connected with the Definitions in that they proceed to postulate the *existence* of certain of the things defined, namely straight lines and circles.

(2) Though it is true that ἔννοια in :person:`Plato <plato>` and :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` is generally a notion of an *object*, not of a *fact* or proposition, there are instances in :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` where it does mean a notion of a fact: thus in the :cite:`*Eth. Nic.* IX. 11, 1171 a 32 <aristotle:eth-nic:IX.11.1171a32>` he speaks of "the notion (or consciousness) *that friends sympathise*" (ἡ ἔννοια τοῦ συναλγεῖν τοὺς φίλους) and again, b 14, of "the *notion* (or consciousness) *that they are pleased* at his good fortune." It is true that :person:`Plato <plato>` and :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` do not use the word in a technical sense; but neither was there apparently in Aristotle's time any fixed technical term for what we call "axioms," since he speaks of them variously as "the so-called axioms in mathematics," "the so-called common axioms," "the common (things)" (τὰ κοινά), and even "the common *opinions*" (:term:`κοιναὶ δόξαι <koinai-doxai>`). I see therefore no reason why :person:`Euclid <euclid>` should not himself have given a technical sense to "Common Notions," which is at least a distinct improvement upon "common opinions."

(3) The use of ἔννοια in Proclus' quotation from :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>` seems to me to be an unfortunate, rather than a fortunate, coincidence from Tannery's point of view, for it is there used precisely in the old sense of the notion of an *object* (in that case a line).
