Again, :g:`$AB$ <0261.g1:/ A B />` coinciding with :g:`$DE$ <0261.g1:/ D E />`, the straight line :g:`$AC$ <0261.g1:/ A C />` will also coincide with :g:`$DF$ <0261.g1:/ D F />`, because the angle :g:`$BAC$ <0261.g1:< B A C )>` is equal to the angle :g:`$EDF$ <0261.g1:< E D F )>`; hence the point :g:`$C$ <0261.g1:C>` will also coincide with the point :g:`$F$ <0261.g1:F>`, because :g:`$AC$ <0261.g1:/ A C />` is again equal to :g:`$DF$ <0261.g1:/ D F />`.

But :g:`$B$ <0261.g1:B>` also coincided with :g:`$E$ <0261.g1:E>`; hence the base :g:`$BC$ <0261.g1:/ B C />` will coincide with the base :g:`$EF$ <0261.g1:/ E F />` [For if, when :g:`$B$ <0261.g1:B>` coincides with :g:`$E$ <0261.g1:E>` and :g:`$C$ <0261.g1:C>` with :g:`$F$ <0261.g1:F>`, the base :g:`$BC$ <0261.g1:/ B C />` does not coincide with the base :g:`$EF$ <0261.g1:/ E F />`, two straight lines will enclose a space: which is impossible. Therefore the base :g:`$BC$ <0261.g1:/ B C />` will coincide with :g:`$EF$ <0261.g1:/ E F />`]\ [#f7]_ and will be equal to it. :just:`[C.N. 4] <I.cn.4>`

Thus the whole triangle :g:`$ABC$ <0261.g1:< A B C >>` will coincide with the whole triangle :g:`$DEF$ <0261.g1:< D E F >>`,

.. assertion::

   and will be equal to it.

And the remaining angles will also coincide with the remaining angles and will be equal to them,

.. subclause::

   the angle :g:`$ABC$ <0261.g1:< A B C )>` to the angle :g:`$DEF$ <0261.g1:< D E F )>`,

.. subclause::

   and the angle :g:`$ACB$ <0261.g1:< A C B )>` to the angle :g:`$DFE$ <0261.g1:< D F E )>`.

Therefore etc.\ [#f8]_

.. qed::

   (Being) what it was required to prove.

.. [#f1] It is a fact that Euclid's enunciations not infrequently leave something to be desired in point of clearness and precision. Here he speaks of the triangles having “the angle equal to the angle, namely the angle contained by the equal straight lines” (τὴν :term:`γωνίαν <angle>` τῇ γωνίᾳ ἴσην ἔχῃ τὴν ὑπὸ τῶν ἴσων :term:`εὐθειῶν <straight-line>` περιεχομένην), only one of the two angles being described in the latter expression (in the accusative), and a similar expression in the dative being left to be understood of the other angle. It is curious too that, after mentioning two “*sides*,” he speaks of the angles contained by the equal “*straight lines*,” not “*sides*.” It may be that he wished to adhere scrupulously, at the outset, to the phraseology of the definitions, where the angle is the inclination to one another of two *lines* or *straight lines*. Similarly in the enunciation of I. 5 he speaks of producing the equal “*straight lines*” as if to keep strictly to the wording of Postulate 2.

.. [#f2] **respectively.** I agree with Mr H. M. Taylor (*Euclid*, p. ix) that it is best to abandon the traditional translation of “each to each,” which would naturally seem to imply that all the four magnitudes are equal rather than (as the Greek ἑκατέρα ἑκατέρᾳ does) that one is equal to one and the other to the other.

.. [#f3] **the base.** Here we have the word *base* used for the first time in the *Elements*. :person:`Proclus <proclus>` explains it (p. 236, 12—15) as meaning (1), when no side of a triangle has been mentioned before, the side “which is on a level with the sight” (τὴν πρὸς τῇ ὄψει κειμένην), and (2), when two sides have already been mentioned, the third side. :person:`Proclus <proclus>` thus avoids the mistake made by some modern editors who explain the term exclusively with reference to the case where two sides have been mentioned before. That this is an error is proved (1) by the occurrence of the term in the enunciations of I. 37 etc. about triangles on the same base and equal bases, (2) by the application of the same term to the bases of parallelograms in I. 35 etc. The truth is that the use of the term must have been suggested by the practice of drawing the particular side horizontally, as it were, and the rest of the figure above it.
