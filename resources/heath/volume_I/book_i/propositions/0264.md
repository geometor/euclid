(2) On either side of the ray <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> there is a ray <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D F /">$DF$</span> such that the angle <span data-ref="0261.g1:< E D F )">$EDF$</span> is equal to the angle <span data-ref="0261.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span>.

It now follows that on <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D F /">$DF$</span> there is a point <span data-ref="0261.g1:F">$F$</span> such that <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D F /">$DF$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>.

And lastly (3), we require an axiom from which to infer that the two remaining angles of the triangles are respectively equal and that the bases are equal.

I have shown above (pp. 229—230) that Hilbert has an axiom stating the equality of the remaining angles simply, but proves the equality of the bases.

Another alternative is that of Pasch (*Vorlesungen über neuere Geometrie*, p. 109) who has the following “Grundsatz”:

If two figures <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> and <span data-ref="0261.g1:< F G H >">$FGH$</span> are given (<span data-ref="0261.g1:< F G H >">$FGH$</span> not being contained in a straight length), and <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span> are congruent, and if a plane surface be laid through <span data-ref="0261.g1:A">$A$</span> and <span data-ref="0261.g1:B">$B$</span>, we can specify in this plane surface, produced if necessary, two points <span data-ref="0261.g1:C">$C$</span>, <span data-ref="0261.g1:D">$D$</span>, neither more nor less, such that the figures <span data-ref="0261.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> and <span data-ref="0261.g1:< A B D >">$ABD$</span> are congruent with the figure <span data-ref="0261.g1:< F G H >">$FGH$</span>, and the straight line <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> has with the straight line <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> or with <span data-ref="0261.g1:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> produced one point common.

I pass to two points of detail in Euclid's proof:

(1) The inference that, since <span data-ref="0261.g1:B">$B$</span> coincides with <span data-ref="0261.g1:E">$E$</span>, and <span data-ref="0261.g1:C">$C$</span> with <span data-ref="0261.g1:F">$F$</span>, the bases of the triangles are wholly coincident rests, as expressly stated, on the impossibility of two straight lines enclosing a space, and therefore presents no difficulty.

But (2) most editors seem to have failed to observe that at the very beginning of the proof a much more serious assumption is made without any explanation whatever, namely that, if <span data-ref="0261.g1:A">$A$</span> be placed on <span data-ref="0261.g1:D">$D$</span>, and <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> on <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>, the point <span data-ref="0261.g1:B">$B$</span> will coincide with <span data-ref="0261.g1:E">$E$</span>, because <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>. That is, the *converse* of *Common Notion 4* is assumed for straight lines. Proclus merely observes, with regard to the converse of this Common Notion, that it is only true in the case of things “of the same form” (ὁμοειδῆ), which he explains as meaning straight lines, arcs of one and the same circle, and angles “contained by lines similar and similarly situated” (p. 241, 3—8).

Savile however saw the difficulty and grappled with it in his note on the Common Notion. After stating that all straight lines with two points common are congruent between them (for otherwise two straight lines would enclose a space), he argues thus. Let there be two straight lines <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>, and let <span data-ref="0261.g1:A">$A$</span> be placed on <span data-ref="0261.g1:D">$D$</span>, and <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> on <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>. Then <span data-ref="0261.g1:B">$B$</span> will coincide with <span data-ref="0261.g1:E">$E$</span>. For, if not, let <span data-ref="0261.g1:B">$B$</span> fall somewhere short of <span data-ref="0261.g1:E">$E$</span> or beyond <span data-ref="0261.g1:E">$E$</span>; and in either case it will follow that the less is equal to the greater, which is impossible.

Savile seems to assume (and so apparently does Lardner who gives the same proof) that, if the straight lines be “applied,” <span data-ref="0261.g1:B">$B$</span> will fall somewhere on <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> or <span data-ref="0261.g1:[ D E ]">$DE$</span> produced. But the ground for this assumption should surely be stated; and it seems to me that it is necessary to use, not Postulate 1 alone, nor Postulate 2 alone, but both, for this purpose (in other words to assume, not only that *two straight lines cannot enclose a space*, but also that *two straight lines cannot have a common segment*). For the only safe course is to place <span data-ref="0261.g1:A">$A$</span> upon <span data-ref="0261.g1:D">$D$</span> and then turn <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> about <span data-ref="0261.g1:D">$D$</span> until *some* point on <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> intermediate between <span data-ref="0261.g1:A">$A$</span> and <span data-ref="0261.g1:B">$B$</span> coincides with *some* point on <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>. In this position <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> and <span data-ref="0261.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> have two points common. Then Postulate 1 enables us to infer that the straight lines coincide *between* the two common points, and Postulate 2 that they coincide beyond the second common point towards <span data-ref="0261.g1:B">$B$</span> and <span data-ref="0261.g1:E">$E$</span>. Thus the straight lines coincide throughout so far as *both* extend; and Savile's argument then proves that <span data-ref="0261.g1:B">$B$</span> coincides with <span data-ref="0261.g1:E">$E$</span>.
