Here, and in numberless later passages, I have inserted the word “sides” for the reason given in the note on I. 1, line 20. It would have been permissible to supply either “straight lines” or “sides”; but on the whole “sides” seems to be more in accordance with the phraseology of I. 4.

.. [#f4] **the base BC is common to them,** i.e., apparently, common to the angles, as the αὐτῶν in :term:`βάσις <basis>` αὐτῶν κοινὴ can only refer to γωνίᾳ and γωνίᾳ preceding. :person:`Simson <simson>` wrote “and the base :g:`$BC$ <0265.g1:/ B C />` is common to the two triangles :g:`$BFC$ <0265.g1:< B F C >>`, :g:`$CGB$ <0265.g1:< C G B >>`”; :person:`Todhunter <todhunter>` left out these words as being of no use and tending to perplex a beginner. But :person:`Euclid <euclid>` evidently chose to quote the conclusion of I. 4 exactly; the first phrase of that conclusion is that the bases (of the two triangles) are equal, and, as the equal bases are here the *same* base, :person:`Euclid <euclid>` naturally substitutes the word “common” for “equal.”

.. [#f5] As “(Being) what it was required to prove” (or “do”) is somewhat long, I shall henceforth write the time-honoured “Q. E. D.” and “Q. E. F.” for :term:`ὅπερ ἔδει δεῖξαι <hoper-edei-deixai>` and :term:`ὅπερ ἔδει ποιῆσαι <hoper-edei-poiesai>`.

According to :person:`Proclus <proclus>` (:cite:`p. 250, 20 <proclus:in-euclid:250.20>`) the discoverer of the fact that in any isosceles triangle the angles at the base are equal was :person:`Thales <thales>`, who however is said to have spoken of the angles as being *similar*, and not as being *equal*. (Cf. Arist. :cite:`*De caelo* IV. 4, 311 b 34 <aristotle:de-caelo:IV.4.311b34>` πρὸς ὁμοίας :term:`γωνίας <angle>` φαίνεται φερόμενον where *equal angles* are meant.)

A pre-Euclidean proof of I. 5.
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

One of the most interesting of the passages in :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` indicating differences between Euclid's proofs and those with which :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` was familiar, in other words, those of the text-books immediately preceding Euclid's, has reference to the theorem of I. 5. The passage (:cite:`*Anal. Prior.* I. 24, 41 b 13—22 <aristotle:anal-prior:I.24.41b13-22>`) is so important that I must quote it in full. :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` is illustrating the fact that in any syllogism one of the propositions must be affirmative and *universal* (καθόλου). “This,” he says, “is better shown in the case of geometrical propositions” (ἐν τοῖς διαγράμμασιν), e.g. the proposition that *the angles at the base of an isosceles triangle are equal.*

“For let :g:`$A$ <0267.g1:A; 0267.g1:/ O A />`, :g:`$B$ <0267.g1:B; 0267.g1:/ O B />` be drawn [i.e. joined] to the centre.
