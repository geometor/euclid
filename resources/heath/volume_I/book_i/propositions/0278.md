III. Let <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ D F /">$DF$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span> form an angle *exterior* to the figure.  
Let <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ D G /">$DG$</span> be joined.  
The proof proceeds as in the last case, except that subtraction takes the place of addition, and  
the remaining angle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< E D F )">$EDF$</span> is equal to the remaining angle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< E G F )">$EGF$</span>.

Therefore in all three cases the angle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< E D F )">$EDF$</span> is equal to the angle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< E G F )">$EGF$</span>, that is, to the angle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span>.

It will be observed that, in accordance with the practice of the Greek geometers in not recognising as an “angle” any angle not less than two right angles, the re-entrant angle of the quadrilateral <span data-ref="0278.g1:< D E G F >">$DEGF$</span> is ignored and the angle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< D F G )">$DFG$</span> is said to be *outside* the figure.

### PROPOSITION 9.

<p class="enunciation">To bisect a given rectilineal angle.</p>

![Proposition 9 figure](0278.g2.svg)

Let the angle <span data-ref="0278.g2:< B A C )">$BAC$</span> be the given rectilineal angle.  
Thus it is required to bisect it.  
Let a point <span data-ref="0278.g2:D">$D$</span> be taken at random on <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>;  
let <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span> be cut off from <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A C /">$AC$</span> equal to <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span>; <span class="justification">[I. 3]</span>  
<sup>5</sup> let <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ D E /">$DE$</span> be joined, and on <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ D E /">$DE$</span> let the equilateral triangle <span data-ref="0278.g2:< D E F >">$DEF$</span> be constructed;  
let <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span> be joined.

I say that the angle <span data-ref="0278.g2:< B A C )">$BAC$</span> has been bisected by the straight line <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span>.

<sup>10</sup> For, since <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span>,  
and <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span> is common,  
<p class="assertion">the two sides <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ D A /">$DA$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span> are equal to the two sides <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ E A /">$EA$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span> respectively.</p>

And the base <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ D F /">$DF$</span> is equal to the base <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ E F /">$EF$</span>;  
<p class="assertion"><sup>15</sup> therefore the angle <span data-ref="0278.g2:< D A F )">$DAF$</span> is equal to the angle <span data-ref="0278.g2:< E A F )">$EAF$</span>. <span class="justification">[I. 8]</span></p>

Therefore the given rectilineal angle <span data-ref="0278.g2:< B A C )">$BAC$</span> has been bisected by the straight line <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span>.

<p class="qed">Q. E. F.</p>

It will be observed from the translation of this proposition that Euclid does not say, in his description of the construction, that the equilateral triangle should be constructed on the side of <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ D E /">$DE$</span> opposite to <span data-ref="0278.g2:A">$A$</span>; he leaves this to be inferred from his figure. There is no particular value in Proclus’ explanation as to how we should proceed in case any one should assert that he could not recognise the existence of any space below <span data-ref="0278.g2:/ D E /">$DE$</span>.
