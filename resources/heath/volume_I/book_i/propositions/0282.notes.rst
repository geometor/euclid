Content Analysis
----------------

1. **Apollonius' Alternative Proof of I. 10**:

   - **Source**: Proclus, pp. 279, 16—280, 4.
   - **Construction**: Circles described with centres $A, B$ and radii $AB, BA$ intersecting in $C, D$. Lines $CD, AC, CB, AD, DB$ joined.
   - **Proof in two steps**:

     1. Triangles $ACD, BCD$: sides $AC = BC$ and $CD$ common, bases $AD = BD$, so $\angle ACD = \angle BCD$ [I. 8].
     2. Triangles $ACE, BCE$: $\angle ACD = \angle BCD$, $AC = CB$, $CE$ common, so $AE = EB$ follows by I. 4.

   - **Theoretical critique**: Heath notes the objection that Apollonius unnecessarily redid what had already been achieved in I. 9 (angle bisection), rather than relying upon it.
   - Illustrated by Graphic ``0282.g1.svg``.

2. **Philosophical Commentary on Indivisibility**:

   - **Proclus on the Indivisible Hypothesis (pp. 277, 25—279, 4)**:

     - Geometers did not assume as a preliminary hypothesis that a line is not composed of indivisible parts (ἐξ ἀμερῶν).
     - Argument against indivisibles: a finite line would consist of an odd or even number of parts; an odd number would require bisecting an indivisible component, which is impossible.

   - **Geminus' Analysis**:

     - Geometers assumed by common notion that a continuous magnitude—one composed of connected parts (συνημμένων)—is divisible (διαιρετόν).
     - However, *infinite* divisibility (ἐπ᾽ ἄπειρον διαιρεῖται) was not assumed as an axiom; it was *proved* through principles demonstrating the existence of incommensurable magnitudes (since incommensurability precludes any indivisible minimum or least common measure).

   - **Refutation of Xenocrates and Aristotle's Physics**:

     - Proposition 10 serves to refute Xenocrates' doctrine of indivisible lines (ἄτομοι γραμμαί).
     - Proclus' arguments align closely with Aristotle's treatment of magnitude and continuity in *Physics* VI. 1 (231 a 21 sqq.) and VI. 2 (233 b 15—32).
