Proclus says that two kinds of perpendicular were distinguished, the “plane” (ἐπίπεδος) and the “solid” (στερεά), the former being the perpendicular dropped on a line in a plane and the latter the perpendicular dropped on a plane. The term “solid perpendicular” is sufficiently curious, but it may perhaps be compared with the Greek term “solid locus” applied to a conic section, apparently on the ground that it has its origin in the section of a solid, namely a cone.

Attention is called by most editors to the assumption in this proposition that, if only <span data-ref="0285.g1:D">$D$</span> be taken on the side of <span data-ref="0285.g1:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> remote from <span data-ref="0285.g1:C">$C$</span>, the circle described with <span data-ref="0285.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> as radius must necessarily cut <span data-ref="0285.g1:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> in two points. To satisfy us of this we need, as in I. 1, some postulate of continuity, e.g. something like that suggested by Killing (see note on the Principle of Continuity above, p. 235):
“If a point [here the point describing the circle] moves in a figure which is divided into two parts [by the straight line], and if it belongs at the beginning of the motion to one part and at another stage of the motion to the other part, it must during the motion cut the boundary between the two parts,” and this of course applies to the motion in *two* directions from <span data-ref="0285.g1:D">$D$</span>.

But the editors have not, as a rule, noticed a possible objection to the Euclidean statement of this problem which is much more difficult to dispose of at this stage, i.e. without employing any proposition later than this in Euclid’s order. How do we know, says the supposed critic, that the circle does not cut <span data-ref="0285.g1:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> in *three* or more points, in which case there would be not one perpendicular but *three* or more? Proclus (pp. 286, 12—289, 6) tries to refute this objection, and it is interesting to follow his argument, though it will easily be seen to be inconclusive. He takes in order three possible suppositions.

1. May not the circle meet <span data-ref="0286.g1:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> in a third point <span data-ref="0286.g1:K">$K$</span> between the middle point of <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ G E /">$GE$</span> and either extremity of it, taking the form drawn in the figure appended?

Suppose this possible. Bisect <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ G E /">$GE$</span> in <span data-ref="0286.g1:H">$H$</span>. Join <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C H /">$CH$</span>, and produce it to meet the circle in <span data-ref="0286.g1:L">$L$</span>. Join <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C G /">$CG$</span>, <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C K /">$CK$</span>, <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>.

Then, since <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C G /">$CG$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>, and <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C H /">$CH$</span> is common, while the base <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ G H /">$GH$</span> is equal to the base <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ H E /">$HE$</span>,  
the angles <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C H G )">$CHG$</span>, <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C H E )">$CHE$</span> are equal and, since they are adjacent, they are both right.

Again, since <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C G /">$CG$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>,  
the angles at <span data-ref="0286.g1:G">$G$</span> and <span data-ref="0286.g1:E">$E$</span> are equal.

Lastly, since <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C K /">$CK$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C G /">$CG$</span> and also to <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>, the angles <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C G K )">$CGK$</span>, <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C K G )">$CKG$</span> are equal, as also are the angles <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C K E )">$CKE$</span>, <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C E K )">$CEK$</span>.

Since the angles <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C G K )">$CGK$</span>, <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C E K )">$CEK$</span> are equal, it follows that  
the angles <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C K G )">$CKG$</span>, <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C K E )">$CKE$</span> are equal and therefore both right.

Therefore the angle <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C K H )">$CKH$</span> is equal to the angle <span data-ref="0286.g1:< C H K )">$CHK$</span>,  
and <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C H /">$CH$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C K /">$CK$</span>.
