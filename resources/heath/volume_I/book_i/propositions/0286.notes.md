## Content Analysis
1. **Gnomon and Oenopides (Sentence Completion)**:
   - Sentence completed from Folio 0285: `gnomon-wise (κατὰ γνώμονα), because the *gnomon* is also at right angles to the horizon.”`
   - Historical sense of *gnomon*: vertical staff for casting shadows to measure time.
   - Citation: Moritz Cantor, *Vorlesungen über Geschichte der Mathematik*, Band I, 3. Auflage, p. 161 (abbreviated $\text{I}_3$ / $\text{I₃}$).
   - Forward reference to Euclid Book II, Definition 2 for geometrical meanings of gnomon.

2. **Plane vs. Solid Perpendicular**:
   - Proclus notes two classifications:
     - "plane" (ἐπίπεδος): perpendicular dropped to a line in a plane.
     - "solid" (στερεά): perpendicular dropped to a plane in 3D space.
   - Heath compares "solid perpendicular" to "solid locus" (τόπος στερεός) as applied to conic sections originating from solid cones.

3. **Assumption of Continuity**:
   - Euclid assumes without explicit postulate that circle described with radius $CD$ cuts $AB$ in two points when $D$ is on the opposite side from $C$.
   - Modern foundation requires a continuity principle, such as Wilhelm Killing's motion postulate (*Einführung in die Grundlagen der Geometrie*, cited in Heath's earlier note on Continuity, p. 235).

4. **Objection: Multiple Intersections (Proclus)**:
   - A critical objection at this stage of the *Elements* (before I. 16 or III. 10): How do we prove that a circle cannot intersect a straight line in *three* or more points, creating multiple perpendiculars?
   - Proclus (*In primum Euclidis Elementorum librum commentarii*, pp. 286, 12—289, 6) examines three cases:
     - **Case 1** (Folio 0286): Circle meets $AB$ in a third point $K$ between midpoint $H$ and extremity $E$.
     - **Construction & Derivation**:
       - Bisect $GE$ at $H$, draw $CH$ and produce to circle at $L$; join $CG, CK, CE$.
       - From $\triangle CGH \cong \triangle CEH$ [I. 8], $\angle CHG = \angle CHE = \text{right angle}$ and $\angle CGK = \angle CEK$.
       - Since $C$ is circle centre, $CG = CK = CE$. Thus isosceles triangles $\triangle CGK$ and $\triangle CKE$ give $\angle CGK = \angle CKG$ and $\angle CEK = \angle CKE$.
       - Since $\angle CGK = \angle CEK$, adjacent angles $\angle CKG = \angle CKE = \text{right angle}$.
       - Thus in $\triangle CHK$, $\angle CKH = \angle CHK = \text{right angle}$, which leads to $CH = CK$.
   - Page ends at `and CH is equal to CK.`, continuing onto Folio 0287 where the contradiction ($CK = CL > CH$, hence $CH = CL$) is stated.
