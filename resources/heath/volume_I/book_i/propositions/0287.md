But <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C K /">$CK$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C L /">$CL$</span>, by the definition of the circle; therefore <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C H /">$CH$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0286.g1:/ C L /">$CL$</span>: which is impossible.

Thus Proclus; but why should not the circle meet <span data-ref="0286.g1:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> in <span data-ref="0286.g1:H">$H$</span> as well as <span data-ref="0286.g1:K">$K$</span>?

2. May not the circle meet <span data-ref="0287.g1:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> in <span data-ref="0287.g1:H">$H$</span> the middle point of <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ G E /">$GE$</span> and take the form shown in the second figure?

![Proclus Refutation of Objections to I. 12 Case 2](0287.g1.svg)

In that case, says Proclus, join <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C G /">$CG$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C H /">$CH$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span> as before. Then bisect <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ H E /">$HE$</span> at <span data-ref="0287.g1:K">$K$</span>, join <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C K /">$CK$</span> and produce it to meet the circumference at <span data-ref="0287.g1:L">$L$</span>.

Now, since <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ H K /">$HK$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ K E /">$KE$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C K /">$CK$</span> is common, and the base <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C H /">$CH$</span> is equal to the base <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>,  
the angles at <span data-ref="0287.g1:K">$K$</span> are equal and therefore both right angles.

Therefore the angle <span data-ref="0287.g1:< C H K )">$CHK$</span> is equal to the angle <span data-ref="0287.g1:< C K H )">$CKH$</span>, whence <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C K /">$CK$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C H /">$CH$</span> and therefore to <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C L /">$CL$</span>: which is impossible.

So Proclus; but why should not the circle meet <span data-ref="0287.g1:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> in <span data-ref="0287.g1:K">$K$</span> as well as <span data-ref="0287.g1:H">$H$</span>?

3. May not the circle meet <span data-ref="0287.g2:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> in two points besides <span data-ref="0287.g2:G">$G$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g2:E">$E$</span> and pass, between those two points, to the side of <span data-ref="0287.g2:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> towards <span data-ref="0287.g2:C">$C$</span>, as in the next figure?

![Proclus Refutation of Objections to I. 12 Case 3](0287.g2.svg)

Here again, by the same method, Proclus proves that, <span data-ref="0287.g2:K">$K$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g2:L">$L$</span> being the other two points in which the circle cuts <span data-ref="0287.g2:[ A B ]">$AB$</span>,  
<span data-ref="0287.g2:/ C K /">$CK$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0287.g2:/ C H /">$CH$</span>,  
and, since the circle cuts <span data-ref="0287.g2:/ C H /">$CH$</span> in <span data-ref="0287.g2:M">$M$</span>,  
<span data-ref="0287.g2:/ C M /">$CM$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0287.g2:/ C K /">$CK$</span> and therefore to <span data-ref="0287.g2:/ C H /">$CH$</span>: which is impossible.

But, again, why should the circle not cut <span data-ref="0287.g2:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> in the point <span data-ref="0287.g2:H">$H$</span> as well?

In fact, Proclus’ cases are not mutually exclusive, and his method of proof only enables us to show that, if the circle meets <span data-ref="0287.g2:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> in one more point besides <span data-ref="0287.g2:G">$G$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g2:E">$E$</span>, it must meet it in more points still. We can always find a new point of intersection by bisecting the distance separating any two points of intersection, and so, applying the method *ad infinitum*, we should have to conclude ultimately that the circle with radius <span data-ref="0287.g2:/ C H /">$CH$</span> (or <span data-ref="0287.g2:/ C G /">$CG$</span>) coincides with <span data-ref="0287.g2:[ A B ]">$AB$</span>. It would follow that a circle with centre <span data-ref="0287.g2:C">$C$</span> and radius greater than <span data-ref="0287.g2:/ C H /">$CH$</span> would not meet <span data-ref="0287.g2:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> at all. Also, since all straight lines from <span data-ref="0287.g2:C">$C$</span> to points on <span data-ref="0287.g2:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> would be equal in length, there would be an infinite number of perpendiculars from <span data-ref="0287.g2:C">$C$</span> on <span data-ref="0287.g2:[ A B ]">$AB$</span>.

Is this under any circumstances possible? It is not possible in Euclidean space, but it is possible, under the Riemann hypothesis (where a straight line is a “closed series” and returns on itself), in the case where <span data-ref="0287.g2:C">$C$</span> is the pole of the straight line <span data-ref="0287.g2:[ A B ]">$AB$</span>.

It is natural therefore that, for a proof that in Euclidean space there is only one perpendicular from a point to a straight line, we have to wait until I. 16, the precise proposition which under the Riemann hypothesis is only valid with a certain restriction and not universally. There is no difficulty involved by waiting until I. 16, since I. 12 is not used before that proposition is reached; and we are only in the same position as when, in order to satisfy ourselves of the number of possible solutions of I. 1, we have to wait till I. 7.

But if we wish, after all, to prove the truth of the assumption *without recourse to any later proposition than* I. 12, we can do so by means of this same invaluable I. 7.
