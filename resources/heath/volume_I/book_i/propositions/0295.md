For, if possible, let <span data-ref="0295.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span> be all equal, <span data-ref="0295.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g1:C">$C$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g1:D">$D$</span> being in a straight line.  
Then, since <span data-ref="0295.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> are equal, the angles <span data-ref="0295.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g1:< A C B )">$ACB$</span> are equal.  
Similarly, since <span data-ref="0295.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span> are equal, the angles <span data-ref="0295.g1:< A B D )">$ABD$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g1:< A D B )">$ADB$</span> are equal.  
Therefore the angle <span data-ref="0295.g1:< A C B )">$ACB$</span> is equal to the angle <span data-ref="0295.g1:< A D C )">$ADC$</span>, i.e. the exterior angle to the interior and opposite angle: which is impossible.

![Proclus Proof of Impossibility of Three Equal Lines to Same Straight Line](0295.g1.svg)

Proclus next (p. 308, 14 sqq.) undertakes to prove by means of I. 16 that, *if a straight line falling on two straight lines make the exterior angle equal to the interior and opposite angle, the two straight lines will not form a triangle or meet*, for in that case the same angle would be both greater and equal.

The proof is really equivalent to that of Eucl. I. 27. If <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ B E /">$BE$</span> falls on the two straight lines <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> in such a way that the angle <span data-ref="0295.g2:< C D E )">$CDE$</span> is equal to the interior and opposite angle <span data-ref="0295.g2:< A B D )">$ABD$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span> and <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> cannot form a triangle or meet. For, if they did, then (by I. 16) the angle <span data-ref="0295.g2:< C D E )">$CDE$</span> would be greater than the angle <span data-ref="0295.g2:< A B D )">$ABD$</span>, while by the hypothesis it is at the same time equal to it.

![Proclus Equivalent of I. 27 Figure](0295.g2.svg)

Hence, says Proclus, in order that <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ B A /">$BA$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ D C /">$DC$</span> may form a triangle it is necessary for them to *approach* one another in the sense of being turned round one pair of corresponding extremities, e.g. <span data-ref="0295.g2:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g2:D">$D$</span>, so that the other extremities <span data-ref="0295.g2:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g2:C">$C$</span> come nearer. This may be brought about in one of three ways: (1) <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span> may remain fixed and <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> be turned about <span data-ref="0295.g2:D">$D$</span> so that the angle <span data-ref="0295.g2:< C D E )">$CDE$</span> increases; (2) <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> may remain fixed and <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span> be turned about <span data-ref="0295.g2:B">$B$</span> so that the angle <span data-ref="0295.g2:< A B D )">$ABD$</span> becomes smaller; (3) both <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span> and <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> may move so as to make the angle <span data-ref="0295.g2:< A B D )">$ABD$</span> smaller and the angle <span data-ref="0295.g2:< C D E )">$CDE$</span> larger at the same time. The reason, then, of the straight lines <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> coming to form a triangle or to meet is (says Proclus) *the movement of the straight lines*.

Though he does not mention it here, Proclus does in another passage (p. 371, 2—10, quoted on p. 207 above) hint at the possibility that, while I. 16 may remain universally true, either of the straight lines <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ B A /">$BA$</span>, <span data-ref="0295.g2:/ D C /">$DC$</span> (or both together) may be turned through any angle not greater than a certain finite angle and yet may not meet (the Bolyai-Lobachewsky hypothesis).

### PROPOSITION 17.

<p class="enunciation">In any triangle two angles taken together in any manner are less than two right angles.</p>

Let <span data-ref="0296.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> be a triangle;  
I say that two angles of the triangle <span data-ref="0296.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> taken together in any manner are less than two right angles.

For let <span data-ref="0296.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> be produced to <span data-ref="0296.g1:D">$D$</span>. <span class="justification">[Post. 2]</span>

Then, since the angle <span data-ref="0296.g1:< A C D )">$ACD$</span> is an exterior angle of the triangle <span data-ref="0296.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span>,  
<p class="assertion">it is greater than the interior and opposite angle <span data-ref="0296.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span>.</p>
