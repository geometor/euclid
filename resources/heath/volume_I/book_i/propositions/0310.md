“He takes an angle <span data-ref="0310.g1:< C D E )">$CDE$</span> at random,” says Proclus (p. 335, 19 sqq.), “and a straight line <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, and with centre <span data-ref="0310.g1:D">$D$</span> and distance <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> describes the circumference <span data-ref="0310.g1:( C E )">$CE$</span>, and in the same way with centre <span data-ref="0310.g1:A">$A$</span> and distance <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> the circumference <span data-ref="0310.g1:( F B )">$FB$</span>. Then, cutting off <span data-ref="0310.g1:( F B )">$FB$</span> equal to <span data-ref="0310.g1:( C E )">$CE$</span>, he joins <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ A F /">$AF$</span>. And he declares that the angles <span data-ref="0310.g1:< F A B )">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0310.g1:< C D E )">$D$</span> standing on equal circumferences are equal.”

![Folio 0310 Figure 1 - Apollonius Construction of Equal Angle by Circular Arcs](0310.g1.svg)

In the first place, as Proclus remarks, it should be premised that <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> in order that the circles may be equal; and the use of Book **III.** for such an elementary construction is objectionable. The omission to state that <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> must be taken equal to <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> was no doubt a slip, if it occurred. And, as regards the equal angles “standing on equal circumferences,” it would seem possible that Apollonius said this *in explanation*, for the sake of brevity, rather than by way of proof. It seems to me probable that his construction was only given from the point of view of *practical*, not theoretical, geometry. It really comes to the same thing as Euclid’s except that <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span> is taken equal to <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>. For cutting off the arc <span data-ref="0310.g1:( B F )">$BF$</span> equal to the arc <span data-ref="0310.g1:( C E )">$CE$</span> can only be meant in the sense of measuring the chord <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>, say, with a pair of compasses, and then drawing a circle with centre <span data-ref="0310.g1:B">$B$</span> and radius equal to the chord <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>. Apollonius’ direction was therefore probably intended as a practical short cut, avoiding the actual drawing of the chords <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>, <span data-ref="0310.g1:/ B F /">$BF$</span>, which, as well as a proof of the equality in all respects of the triangles <span data-ref="0310.g1:< C D E >">$CDE$</span>, <span data-ref="0310.g1:< B A F >">$BAF$</span>, would be required to establish *theoretically* the correctness of the construction.

### PROPOSITION 24.

<p class="enunciation">
If two triangles have the two sides equal to two sides respectively, but have the one of the angles contained by the equal straight lines greater than the other, they will also have the base greater than the base.
</p>

<sup>5</sup> Let <span data-ref="0310.g2:< A B C >">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0310.g2:< D E F >">$DEF$</span> be two triangles having the two sides <span data-ref="0310.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0310.g2:/ A C /">$AC$</span> equal to the two sides <span data-ref="0310.g2:/ D E /">$DE$</span>, <span data-ref="0310.g2:/ D F /">$DF$</span> respectively, namely <span data-ref="0310.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span> to <span data-ref="0310.g2:/ D E /">$DE$</span>, and <span data-ref="0310.g2:/ A C /">$AC$</span> to <span data-ref="0310.g2:/ D F /">$DF$</span>, and let the angle at <span data-ref="0310.g2:< B A C )">$A$</span> be greater than the angle at <span data-ref="0310.g2:< E D F )">$D$</span>;  
I say that the base <span data-ref="0310.g2:/ B C /">$BC$</span> is also greater than the base <span data-ref="0310.g2:/ E F /">$EF$</span>.
