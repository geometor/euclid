### Proof of Euclid's Postulate 5 from "Playfair's" axiom.

Let <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> make with the transversal <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> the angles <span data-ref="0327.g1:< A E F )">$AEF$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:< E F C )">$EFC$</span> together less than two right angles.

To prove that <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> meet towards <span data-ref="0327.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:C">$C$</span>.

![Folio 0327 Figure 1 - Proof of Postulate 5 from Playfair's Axiom](0327.g1.svg)

Through <span data-ref="0327.g1:E">$E$</span> draw <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ G H /">$GH$</span> making with <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> the angle <span data-ref="0327.g1:< G E F )">$GEF$</span> equal (and alternate) to the angle <span data-ref="0327.g1:< E F D )">$EFD$</span>.  
Thus <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ G H /">$GH$</span> is parallel to <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>. [**I.** 27]  
Then (1) <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> must meet <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> in one direction or the other.  
For, if it does not, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> must be parallel to <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>; hence we have two straight lines <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ G H /">$GH$</span> intersecting at <span data-ref="0327.g1:E">$E$</span> and both parallel to <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>:  
which is impossible.  
Therefore <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> must meet.

(2) Since <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> meet, they must form a triangle with <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span>.  
But in any triangle any two angles are together less than two right angles.  
Therefore the angles <span data-ref="0327.g1:< A E F )">$AEF$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:< E F C )">$EFC$</span> (which are less than two right angles), and not the angles <span data-ref="0327.g1:< B E F )">$BEF$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:< E F D )">$EFD$</span> (which are together greater than two right angles, by **I.** 13), are the angles of the triangle;  
that is, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ E A /">$EA$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ F C /">$FC$</span> meet in the direction of <span data-ref="0327.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:C">$C$</span>, or on the side of <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> on which are the angles together less than two right angles.

The usual course in modern text-books which use "Playfair's" axiom in lieu of Euclid's Postulate is apparently to prove **I.** 29 by means of the axiom, and then Euclid's Postulate by means of **I.** 29.

De Morgan would introduce the proof of Postulate 5 by means of "Playfair's" axiom before **I.** 29, and would therefore apparently prove **I.** 29 as Euclid does, without any change.

As between Euclid's Postulate 5 and "Playfair's" axiom, it would appear that the tendency in modern text-books is rather in favour of the latter. Thus, to take a few noteworthy foreign writers, we find that Rausenberger stands almost alone in using Euclid's Postulate, while Hilbert, Henrici and Treutlein, Rouché and De Comberousse, Enriques and Amaldi all use "Playfair's" axiom.

Yet the case for preferring Euclid's Postulate is argued with some force by Dodgson (*Euclid and his modern Rivals*, pp. 44–6). He maintains (1) that "Playfair's" axiom in fact involves Euclid's Postulate, but at the same time involves *more* than the latter, so that, to that extent, it is a needless strain on the faith of the learner. This is shown as follows.

Given <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> making with <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> the angles <span data-ref="0327.g1:< A E F )">$AEF$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:< E F C )">$EFC$</span> together less than two right angles, draw <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ G H /">$GH$</span> through <span data-ref="0327.g1:E">$E$</span> so that the angles <span data-ref="0327.g1:< G E F )">$GEF$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:< E F C )">$EFC$</span> are together equal to two right angles.  
Then, by **I.** 28, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ G H /">$GH$</span>, <span data-ref="0327.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> are "separational."

We see then that any lines which have the property ($\alpha$) that they make with a transversal angles less than two right angles have also the property ($\beta$) that one of them intersects a straight line which is "separational" from the other.

Now Playfair's axiom asserts that the lines which have property ($\beta$) meet if produced: for, if they did not, we should have two intersecting straight lines both "separational" from a third, which is impossible.
