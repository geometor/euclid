Let a point <span data-ref="0330.g1:D">$D$</span> be taken at random on <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>, and let <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span> be joined;  
on the straight line <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ D A /">$DA$</span>, and at the point <span data-ref="0330.g1:A">$A$</span> on it, let the angle <span data-ref="0330.g1:< D A E )">$DAE$</span> be constructed equal to the angle <span data-ref="0330.g1:< A D C )">$ADC$</span> <span class="justification">[I. 23]</span>;  
and let the straight line <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ A F /">$AF$</span> be produced in a straight line with <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ E A /">$EA$</span>.

Then, since the straight line <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span> falling on the two straight lines <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>, <span data-ref="0330.g1:[ E F ]">$EF$</span> has made the alternate angles <span data-ref="0330.g1:< E A D )">$EAD$</span>, <span data-ref="0330.g1:< A D C )">$ADC$</span> equal to one another,  
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ E A F /">$EAF$</span> is parallel to <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>. <span class="justification">[I. 27]</span></p>

Therefore through the given point <span data-ref="0330.g1:A">$A$</span> the straight line <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ E A F /">$EAF$</span> has been drawn parallel to the given straight line <span data-ref="0330.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>.

<p class="qed">Q. E. F.</p>

---

Proclus rightly remarks (p. 376, 14–20) that, as it is implied in I. 12 that only one perpendicular can be drawn to a straight line from an external point, so here it is implied that only one straight line can be drawn through a point parallel to a given straight line. The construction, be it observed, depends only upon I. 27, and might therefore have come directly after that proposition. Why then did Euclid postpone it until after I. 29 and I. 30? Presumably because he considered it necessary, before giving the construction, to place beyond all doubt the fact that only one such parallel can be drawn. Proclus infers this fact from I. 30; for, he says, if two straight lines could be drawn through one and the same point parallel to the same straight line, the two straight lines would be *parallel*, though intersecting at the given point: which is impossible. I think it is a fair inference that Euclid would have considered it necessary to justify the assumption that only one parallel can be drawn by some such argument, and that he deliberately determined that his own assumption was more appropriate to be made the subject of a Postulate than the assumption of the uniqueness of the parallel.

### PROPOSITION 32.

<p class="enunciation">
In any triangle, if one of the sides be produced, the exterior angle is equal to the two interior and opposite angles, and the three interior angles of the triangle are equal to two right angles.
</p>

Let <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> be a triangle, and let one side of it <span data-ref="0331.g1:[ B D ]">$BC$</span> be produced to <span data-ref="0331.g1:D">$D$</span>;  
I say that the exterior angle <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A C D )">$ACD$</span> is equal to the two interior and opposite angles <span data-ref="0331.g1:< C A B )">$CAB$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span>, and the three interior angles of the triangle <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< B C A )">$BCA$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< C A B )">$CAB$</span> are equal to two right angles.
