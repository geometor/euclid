For let <span data-ref="0331.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span> be drawn through the point <span data-ref="0331.g1:C">$C$</span> parallel to the straight line <span data-ref="0331.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>. <span class="justification">[I. 31]</span>

![Folio 0331 Figure 1 - Proposition 32](0331.g1.svg)

Then, since <span data-ref="0331.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is parallel to <span data-ref="0331.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>,  
and <span data-ref="0331.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> has fallen upon them,  
<p class="assertion">the alternate angles <span data-ref="0331.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A C E )">$ACE$</span> are equal to one another. <span class="justification">[I. 29]</span></p>

Again, since <span data-ref="0331.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is parallel to <span data-ref="0331.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>,  
and the straight line <span data-ref="0331.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span> has fallen upon them,  
<p class="assertion">the exterior angle <span data-ref="0331.g1:< E C D )">$ECD$</span> is equal to the interior and opposite angle <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span>. <span class="justification">[I. 29]</span></p>

But the angle <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A C E )">$ACE$</span> was also proved equal to the angle <span data-ref="0331.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span>;  
<p class="assertion">therefore the whole angle <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A C D )">$ACD$</span> is equal to the two interior and opposite angles <span data-ref="0331.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span>.</p>

Let the angle <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A C B )">$ACB$</span> be added to each;  
<p class="assertion">therefore the angles <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A C D )">$ACD$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A C B )">$ACB$</span> are equal to the three angles <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< B C A )">$BCA$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< C A B )">$CAB$</span>.</p>

But the angles <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A C D )">$ACD$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A C B )">$ACB$</span> are equal to two right angles; <span class="justification">[I. 13]</span>  
<p class="assertion">therefore the angles <span data-ref="0331.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< B C A )">$BCA$</span>, <span data-ref="0331.g1:< C A B )">$CAB$</span> are also equal to two right angles.</p>

<p class="qed">Therefore etc. <span class="justification">Q. E. D.</span></p>

This theorem was discovered in the very early stages of Greek geometry. What we know of the history of it is gathered from three allusions found in Eutocius, Proclus and Diogenes Laertius respectively.

**1.** Eutocius at the beginning of his commentary on the *Conics* of Apollonius (ed. Heiberg, Vol. II. p. 170) quotes Geminus as saying that “the ancients (οἱ ἀρχαῖοι) investigated the theorem of the two right angles in each individual species of triangle, first in the equilateral, again in the isosceles, and afterwards in the scalene triangle, and later geometers demonstrated the general theorem to the effect that in *any* triangle the three interior angles are equal to two right angles.”

**2.** Now, according to Proclus (p. 379, 2–5), Eudemus the Peripatetic refers the discovery of this theorem to the Pythagoreans and gives what he affirms to be their demonstration of it. This demonstration will be given below, but it should be remarked that it is general, and therefore that the “later geometers” spoken of by Geminus were presumably the Pythagoreans, whence it appears that the “ancients” contrasted with them must have belonged to the time of Thales, if they were not his Egyptian instructors.
