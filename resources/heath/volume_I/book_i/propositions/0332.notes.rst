Summary
-------

- **Content**:

  - **Completion of Pamphile / Thales semicircle discussion**:

    - Thales learning geometry from the Egyptians and sacrificing an ox on discovering that the angle in a semicircle is a right angle.
    - Mathematical analysis of how this discovery leads to the theorem that the angle sum of a *right-angled triangle* equals two right angles:

      - Considering two isosceles triangles with vertices at the centre and bases along the legs of the right angle, using Euclid I. 5 (known to Thales).
      - Cantor's contention that Thales must have proved the semicircle property via III. 31, using I. 32 as applied to right-angled triangles.

  - **Reconstruction of the Pre-Euclidean Proof (Hankel & Cantor)**:

    - Based on Geminus's report that the theorem was established sequentially for equilateral, isosceles, and scalene triangles.
    - **Stage 1 (Equilateral Triangle)**:

      - Space filling around a vertex by six equilateral triangles: known practically to the Egyptians through triangular/hexagonal floor tiles (citing G. J. Allman, p. 12) or six-spoked chariot wheels from the time of Ramses II (19th Dynasty, ~1300 B.C., citing Cantor I₃, p. 109).
      - Since six angles of an equilateral triangle equal 4 right angles, three angles equal 2 right angles.
      - Analogous observation for isosceles right-angled triangle by dividing a square along its diagonal (angle sum = 2 right angles).

    - **Stage 2 (Right-Angled and Isosceles Triangles)**:

      - Drawing perpendicular $AD$ from vertex $A$ to base $BC$ of equilateral triangle $ABC$, and completing rectangle $ADCE$.
      - Rectangle (sum = 4 right angles) bisected by diagonal $AC$ into two equal right triangles, each with angle sum = 2 right angles.
      - Generalization to *any* rectangle and therefore *any* right-angled triangle; joining two congruent right-angled triangles demonstrates the theorem for *any* isosceles triangle.

    - **Stage 3 (General Triangle)**:

      - Introduction of resolving *any* triangle into the half of a rectangle or two right-angled triangles (ends mid-sentence with ``or``).
