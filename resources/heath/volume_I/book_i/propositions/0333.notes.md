# Transcription Notes: Folio 0333 (Page 319)

## Summary
- **Content**:
  - **Reconstruction of Pre-Euclidean Angle-Sum Proof (Conclusion)**:
    - Continuation of Hankel and Cantor's reconstruction of the older proof of Euclid I. 32.
    - Demonstrates that any triangle can be divided into two right-angled triangles inscribed in a rectangle, leading to the inference that the angle sum of any triangle equals two right angles.
    - Embedded graphic `0333.g1.svg` illustrating this decomposition.
  - **Critical Evaluation of Traditions (Pamphile and Geminus)**:
    1. **Pamphile's Story of Thales' Sacrifice**:
       - The story recorded by Pamphile that Thales sacrificed an ox upon discovering that an angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle suspiciously parallels the legend of Pythagoras celebrating his discovery of Euclid I. 47 (Proclus, p. 426, 6—9).
       - Diogenes Laertius adds that others, including Apollodorus the calculator (ὁ λογιστικός), attribute the inscription of a right-angled triangle in a circle to Pythagoras.
       - Chronological distance: Pamphile lived under Nero (A.D. 54—68), roughly 700 years after Thales (c. 640 B.C.).
       - Critique of Max Schmidt (*Kulturhistorische Beiträge zur Kenntnis des griechischen und römischen Altertums*, 1906, p. 31): Schmidt claims older sources name Pythagoras, but Heath points out nothing beyond Diogenes Laertius' mention is known of Apollodorus.
       - Heath concludes that the attribution to Thales is more than doubtful.
    2. **Geminus' Three Stages of I. 32**:
       - Eudemus explicitly attributes the discovery of the theorem for *any* triangle to the Pythagoreans without mentioning gradual stages (equilateral -> isosceles -> scalene).
       - Hankel's intermediate isosceles stage is mathematically redundant: decomposing a scalene triangle into two right triangles is just as direct as decomposing an isosceles triangle.
       - J. L. Heiberg's insight (*Mathematisches zu Aristoteles*, p. 20): Geminus' account likely originated in a misapprehension of Aristotle (*Anal. Post.* I. 5, 74 a 25), where Aristotle distinguishes between proving a property of individual species versus proving it universally and primarily (πρῶτον καὶ καθόλου) of the entire genus.
       - Aristotle's three illustrative examples:
         - Parallels cut by a transversal summing to two right angles (Euclid I. 27 / I. 28, discussed pp. 308—9).
         - Alternate proportion (*alternando*) in magnitude theory.
         - Hypothetical separate proofs of the angle sum for equilateral, scalene, and isosceles triangles (`οὐδ᾽ ἄν τις δείξῃ`).

## Editorial & Typographical Findings
- **Greek Text & Diacritics**:
  - `ὁ λογιστικός`: rough breathing on `ὁ`; acute accent on final syllable of `λογιστικός`.
  - `πρῶτον`: circumflex over omega.
  - `καθόλου`: acute accent over omicron.
  - `οὐδ᾽ ἄν τις δείξῃ`: smooth breathing with apostrophe on `οὐδ᾽`; smooth breathing with acute on `ἄν`; enclitic `τις` without accent; acute accent and subscript iota on `δείξῃ`.
