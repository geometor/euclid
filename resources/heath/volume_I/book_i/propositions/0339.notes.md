# Transcription Notes: Folio 0339 (Page 325)

## Summary
- **Content**:
  - **Commentary on Proposition 34 (continued)**:
    1. **Note 1 on "Parallelogrammic Areas"**:
       - Discussion of Euclid's first introduction of the expression "parallelogrammic areas" (παραλληλόγραμμα χωρία) or "parallelogram" areas.
       - The term strictly denotes areas bounded by parallel straight lines, with Euclid's implicit restriction to four-sided figures (quadrilaterals), excluding regular polygons with even numbers of sides.
       - Proclus (p. 393) records that the word "parallelogram" (παραλληλόγραμμος) was introduced by Euclid, suggested by I. 33, formed analogously to "rectilineal" (εὐθύγραμμος).
    2. **Textual Note on Lines 17, 18, 40 and 36**:
       - Heath notes that while he writes "$DCB$" and "$DC, CB$", the Greek text in these places reads "$BCD$" and "$CD, BC$" respectively (referencing his earlier note on I. 33, lines 15, 18).
    3. **Proclus' Philosophical Analysis via Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics***:
       - Proclus (pp. 390 sqq.) classifies parallelograms by diagonal bisection of angles:
         - Diagonals bisect both angles and areas: squares and rhombi.
         - Diagonals bisect areas but not angles: rectangles and rhomboids.
       - Relates this to Aristotle's epistemological distinction (*Anal. Post.* I. 4, 5, 73 a 21–74 b 4) between:
         - Attributes predicable of every individual instance of a class (κατὰ παντός).
         - Attributes true of it primarily (τούτου πρώτου) and universally/generally (καθόλου).
       - Aristotle warns that mathematicians are prone to mistake a proof *kata pantos* for one *toutou protou katholou* when a higher unifying genus lacks a recognized name.
       - Proclus adapts this to geometrical theorems:
         - Theorem part 1 (opposite sides and angles equal) is *general*, because it holds only for parallelograms.
         - Theorem part 2 (diameter bisects area) is *not general*, because diameter bisection applies to circles and ellipses as well. Early investigations were partial/particular (μερικώτεραι) before reaching generality.
    4. **Heath's Critique of Proclus**:
       - Heath questions Proclus' distinction: Euclid tacitly assumes a quadrilateral throughout. Since other figures (e.g. regular even-sided polygons) have opposite sides and angles equal, part 1 is no more general than part 2 once restricted to quadrilaterals.
    5. **Apollonius and the Concept of Diameter**:
       - Proclus' discussion reflects Greek inquiries into the general concept of diameters.
       - Apollonius (*Conics*, Book I, definitions) defines the diameter of "any bent line" (καμπύλη γραμμή) as any straight line drawn from the bent line which bisects all straight lines (chords) drawn in the line parallel to a given straight line.
       - In Archimedean and Hellenistic terminology, "bent line" (καμπύλη γραμμή) denotes curves as well as composite lines of straight and curved segments.
