## Textual Observations & Editorial Decisions

1. **Conclusion of Plutarch Citation**:
   - The folio opens with the completion of the quotation from Plutarch (*Symp.* VIII. 2, 4) begun at the bottom of folio 0357:
     *"demonstrated that the square on the hypotenuse is equal to the squares on the sides about the right angle” (\*Symp.\* VIII. 2, 4)."*

2. **Pythagorean Ritual & Sacrifice Critique**:
   - Heath notes that Carl Anton Bretschneider and Hermann Hankel argued that the tradition of Pythagoras sacrificing an ox must be rejected as incompatible with Pythagorean ritual rules forbidding animal slaughter.
   - Despite this, the historical attribution of the method of "application of areas" to the early Pythagoreans is well founded.

3. **Generalization of Application of Areas (Geometric Algebra)**:
   - Heath outlines the three categories of the problem:
     - Simple application (*parabole*): Euclid I. 44 and I. 45.
     - Application with exceeding (*hyperbole*): Euclid VI. 29.
     - Application with falling short (*ellipsis*): Euclid VI. 28.
   - Polytonic Greek: `διορισμός` (*diorismos* — condition of possibility / specification), required in the case of falling short so that the applied area does not exceed the maximum possible area (the condition established in Euclid VI. 27).

4. **Mathematical Formulation (Display Equations)**:
   - Heath connects the geometric constructions to mixed quadratic equations:
     - Solving via Euclid II. 5 and 6:
       $$
       \begin{aligned}
       ax \pm x^2 &= b^2, \\
       x^2 - ax &= b^2;
       \end{aligned}
       $$
     - General equations solved in Euclid VI. 28 and 29:
       $$ax \pm \frac{b}{c}x^2 = \frac{C}{m}.$$
       Here $C$ is capitalized, denoting the area of the given rectilineal figure.

5. **Connection to Apollonius and Conic Sections**:
   - Heath explains Apollonius of Perga's adoption of the terms *parabola*, *hyperbola*, and *ellipse* based on their geometric symptomata (the geometric equivalents of Cartesian equations referred to a diameter and the tangent at its vertex as axes).
   - Parameter $p$ (latus rectum) is given for central conics as equal to $\frac{{d'}^2}{d}$, where $d$ is the diameter and $d'$ its conjugate diameter.
   - **Page Boundary Note**: The folio ends with the introductory sentence: *"Apollonius gives the properties of the three conics in the following form."*
   - The specific coordinate equations ($y^2 = px$, etc.) appear at the top of the next page (folio 0359 / page 345). They are therefore not transcribed on this folio, maintaining strict page-by-page fidelity to the print volume.
