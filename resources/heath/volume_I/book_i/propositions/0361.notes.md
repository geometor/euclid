1. **Top Text: Continuation of Commentary on Transformation of Areas (Proposition 45)**:
   - Folio 0361 completes the historical and mathematical note on the *Transformation of areas* commenced at the foot of folio 0360 (page 346).
   - The opening clause continues the sentence broken across the page boundary:
     - Page 346: *"We can now take stock of how far the propositions I. 43–45 bring us in the matter of \*transformation of areas\*, which constitutes so important a part of"*
     - Page 347: *"what has been fitly called the \*geometrical algebra\* of the Greeks."*
   - Key mathematical themes in Heath's exposition:
     - Representation of any rectilineal area (via triangular decomposition) as a parallelogram with a prescribed side and angle.
     - The primacy of the rectangle as representing the product of two magnitudes.
     - Geometrical *application* (παραβολή) as the equivalent of algebraic *division* ($A / b = h$).
     - Geometrical addition and subtraction of areas into a single rectangle with a given base, equivalent to finding a common factor.
     - Missing step in Book I: finding a *square* equal to a given rectangle (reserved for II. 14).
     - Overview of Book II: transformations of combinations of rectangles and squares, anticipating the Pythagorean theorem in I. 47 (sum of squares).
     - The converse transformation into an area *similar* to a given rectilineal figure requires proportions (VI. 25).
   - Proclus Citation (pp. 422, 24—423, 6):
     - Connects the problem of transformation of areas to the origin of the ancient quest to square the circle (*tetragonismos*).
     - Notes Archimedes' determination of the circle (*Dimension of the Circle*, Prop. 1): equal to a right-angled triangle with perpendicular equal to radius and base equal to perimeter.
     - Heath preserves the editorial clarification `[the perpendicular]` in brackets.
