**1, 3, 30.** Proclus (p. 423, 18 sqq.) notes the difference between the word *construct* (συστήσασθαι) applied by Euclid to the construction of a triangle (and, he might have added, of an angle) and the words *describe on* (ἀναγράφειν ἀπό) used of drawing a square on a given straight line as one side. The triangle (or angle) is, so to say, pieced together, while the describing of a square on a given straight line is the making of a figure “from” one side, and corresponds to the multiplication of the number representing the side by itself.

Proclus (pp. 424—5) proves that, *if squares are described on equal straight lines, the squares are equal; and, conversely, that, if two squares are equal, the straight lines are equal on which they are described*. The first proposition is immediately obvious if we divide the squares into two triangles by drawing a diagonal in each. The converse is proved as follows.

![Folio 0362 Figure 1 - Proclus Equal Squares on Equal Straight Lines](0362.g1.svg)

Place the two equal squares <span data-ref="0362.g1:< A D F B >">$AF$</span>, <span data-ref="0362.g1:< B C E G >">$CG$</span> so that <span data-ref="0362.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0362.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> are in a straight line. Then, since the angles are right, <span data-ref="0362.g1:/ F B /">$FB$</span>, <span data-ref="0362.g1:/ B G /">$BG$</span> will also be in a straight line. Join <span data-ref="0362.g1:/ A F /">$AF$</span>, <span data-ref="0362.g1:/ F C /">$FC$</span>, <span data-ref="0362.g1:/ C G /">$CG$</span>, <span data-ref="0362.g1:/ G A /">$GA$</span>.

Now, since the squares are equal, the triangles <span data-ref="0362.g1:< A B F >">$ABF$</span>, <span data-ref="0362.g1:< C B G >">$CBG$</span> are equal.

Add to each the triangle <span data-ref="0362.g1:< F B C >">$FBC$</span>; therefore the triangles <span data-ref="0362.g1:< A F C >">$AFC$</span>, <span data-ref="0362.g1:< G F C >">$GFC$</span> are equal, and hence they must be in the same parallels.
