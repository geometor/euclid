2. **Egyptian Arithmetical Evidence vs. Geometric Theorem**:
   - **Hypothesis of Egyptian Origin**: Vitruvius (IX. 2) reports that Pythagoras taught how to make a right angle using lengths 3, 4, 5. Moritz Cantor inferred that Pythagoras derived this directly from Egyptian practice.
   - **Kahun Papyrus Fragment (12th Dynasty, c. 2000 B.C.)**:
     - Contains extractions of square roots: $\sqrt{16} = 4$, $\sqrt{1\frac{7}{9}} = 1\frac{1}{3}$, $\sqrt{6\frac{1}{4}} = 2\frac{1}{2}$.
     - Equations recorded:
       $$
       \begin{aligned}
       1^2 + \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2 &= \left(1\frac{1}{4}\right)^2 \\
       8^2 + 6^2 &= 10^2 \\
       2^2 + \left(1\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 &= \left(2\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 \\
       16^2 + 12^2 &= 20^2.
       \end{aligned}
       $$
     - Each equation scales by a constant factor to $4^2 + 3^2 = 5^2$.
   - **Heath's & Peet's Critique**: Heath emphasizes that while the Egyptians possessed the arithmetical relation $3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$, there is no evidence that they recognized the triangle with sides 3, 4, 5 as right-angled. Heath cites the authoritative work of T. Eric Peet (*The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus*, 1923), confirming that nothing in Egyptian mathematics demonstrates acquaintance with the Pythagorean theorem or any special geometric cases.

3. **Discovery of the General Theorem & Demonstration Graphic**:
   - Heath reconstructs Pythagoras' conceptual progress: from the numerical relation of (3, 4, 5) to investigating the simplest geometric case, namely the *isosceles* right-angled triangle.
   - **Demonstration Graphic (`0366.g1.svg`)**:

     - Right diagram: A composite square divided into four squares and subdivided by diagonals to form an inscribed tilted square, as analyzed by Albert Bürk (*Das Āpastamba-Śulba-Sūtra*, 1901) to demonstrate the Indian/Vedic geometric origin of the theorem.
