1. **Page Opening & Heath's Defense of Euclid against Schopenhauer**:

   - The folio opens mid-sentence continuing from Folio 0367 (page 353):
     ``the method of proportions into one based on Book I. only, effected by a construction and proof so extraordinarily ingenious, is a veritable tour de force which compels admiration...``
   - Heath delivers a sharp rebuke to Arthur Schopenhauer for his "ignorant strictures" against Euclid's proof.
   - **Schopenhauer's Citations**:

     - Heath cites Arthur Schopenhauer's *Sämmtliche Werke* (using the 19th-century German orthography *Sämmtliche* with umlaut and double *m*), specifically Volume III, § 39 and Volume I, § 15 (from *Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung*).
     - Schopenhauer famously complained that Euclid's demonstration produces conviction without insight, calling it:

       - “a mouse-trap proof” (*Mausefallenbeweis*)
       - “a proof walking on stilts, nay, a mean, underhand, proof” (“*Des Eukleides stelzbeiniger, ja, hinterlistiger Beweis*”).

     - Heath points out that Schopenhauer failed to understand why Euclid could not use an intuitively obvious visual proof: Euclid was rigorously constrained by the logical architecture of the *Elements*, needing the theorem in Book II without relying on proportions (which could not be established rigorously until Books V and VI).

2. **Proportion Proof Method** (*b***) vs. Euclid VI. 31**:

   - **Method** (*b*):

     - The altitude from the right angle partitions the original right triangle into two smaller triangles, each similar to the original triangle and to each other.
     - In these three similar triangles, the sides containing the right angle and the hypotenuse are corresponding sides.
     - The sum of the areas of the two smaller triangles equals the area of the whole triangle on the hypotenuse.
     - By the theorem that similar figures are in the duplicate ratio of corresponding sides, the same relation must hold for squares described on these sides.

   - **Heath's Evaluation**:

     - Heath observes that method (*b*) applies universally to *any* similar rectilineal figures, which would establish the generalized theorem of Euclid VI. 31.
     - However, Proclus (*In primum Euclidis Elementorum librum commentarii*) expressly regards VI. 31 as entirely Euclid's own discovery.
     - Heath therefore concludes that method (*a*) (transcribed on Folio 0367) is much more historically probable as Pythagoras' original approach than method (*b*).

3. **Geometric Dissection Hypothesis (Bretschneider & Hankel)**:

   - In section ``(2)``, Heath examines the alternative conjecture that Pythagoras' proof avoided proportions altogether and rested purely on planar decomposition (geometrical algebra):
   - Citations:

     - Carl Anton Bretschneider, *Die Geometrie und die Geometer vor Euklides*, Leipzig, 1870, p. 82.
     - Hermann Hankel, *Zur Geschichte der Mathematik in Alterthum und Mittelalter*, Leipzig, 1874, p. 98.

   - **Reconstruction of the Dissection**:

     - Consider a square of side $a + b$ subdivided into two squares of sides $a$ and $b$ and two rectangular complements of dimensions $a \times b$ (as in Eucl. II. 4).
     - Each rectangular complement is divided by its diagonal $c$ into two congruent right-angled triangles with sides $a, b, c$, yielding four congruent triangles.
     - In the second figure, the same four right triangles are arranged cyclically around the perimeter of an identical $(a + b) \times (a + b)$ square.
     - The unfilled central area forms an tilted square of side $c$ and area $c^2$.
     - Since subtracting the four congruent triangles from the whole square leaves $a^2 + b^2$ in the first figure and $c^2$ in the second figure, it follows immediately that:

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$$
