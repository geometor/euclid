2. **Bhāskara's Visual Proof ("See!")**:
   - Heath observes that the dissection proof of Bretschneider/Hankel lacks specifically Greek geometric style and instead recalls Indian mathematical tradition.
   - **Bhāskara II** (*Bhāskarācārya*, b. 1114 A.D., author of the *Siddhānta Śiromaṇi*, *Līlāvatī*, and *Bījagaṇita*):
     - Heath cites Moritz Cantor, *Vorlesungen über Geschichte der Mathematik*, Band I, 3. Auflage, p. 656 (`Cantor, I₃, p. 656`).
     - Bhāskara describes four congruent right triangles of sides $a, b, c$ placed inwards on the four sides of the square on the hypotenuse $c$.
     - The central hole is a square of side $a - b$.
     - In Indian tradition, the figure is accompanied simply by the word *paśya* (“see!”), leaving the observer to see:
       $$c^2 = 4\frac{ab}{2} + (a - b)^2 = 2ab + a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = a^2 + b^2.$$

3. **Aristotle's *Physics* III. 4 & Pythagorean Gnomons**:
   - Heath notes that while Pythagoras may not have possessed a general proof of this algebraic dissection type valid for incommensurable sides, the property for rational right-angled triangles (*e.g.* 3, 4, 5) could very naturally have been demonstrated by dividing squares into unit squares.
   - Heath cites Aristotle's allusion in *Physics* III. 4 (203a 13–15) to odd numbers as *gnomons* placed around unity to form successive squares ($1 + 3 = 4, 1 + 3 + 5 = 9, \dots$).
   - This suggests that early Pythagorean mathematics operated with square and gnomonic arrays of dots/pebbles (*psephoi*), or squares partitioned into unit squares.

4. **Zeuthen's Reconstruction & the Chinese *Zhoubi Suanjing* (*Chóu-peï*)**:
   - **Zeuthen's Paper**:
     - Heath cites Hieronymus Georg Zeuthen: “*Théorème de Pythagore*,” *Origine de la Géométrie scientifique*, in *Comptes rendus du II*<sup>*me*</sup> *Congrès international de Philosophie*, Genève, 1904.
   - **The $7 \times 7$ Grid Diagram**:
     - Take a square on a line of length $7 = 4 + 3$, subdivided into 49 unit squares.
     - Four $4 \times 3$ rectangles are placed cyclically around the border, enclosing a central $1 \times 1$ unit square.
     - The square $EFGH$ formed on the diagonals has an area of:
       $$49 - 4 \times \left(\frac{4 \times 3}{2}\right) = 49 - 24 = 25 = 5^2.$$
     - Alternatively, $EFGH$ consists of the four half-rectangles plus the unit square ($4 \times 6 + 1 = 25$), while the squares $3^2$ and $4^2$ at adjacent corners also sum to $9 + 16 = 25$.
   - **Cantor & the Chinese *Chóu-peï***:
     - Heath notes Cantor's citation (`Cantor, I₃, p. 680`) indicating that this exact figure is given in the ancient Chinese astronomical/mathematical treatise *Zhoubi Suanjing* (Heath's transcription: *Chóu-peï*), known as the *xian-tu* (hypotenuse diagram).
