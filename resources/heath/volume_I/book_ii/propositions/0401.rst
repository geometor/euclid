:title: Proposition 6

*Aristot. Phys.* pp. 61—68, ed. Diels) from Eudemus' *History of Geometry*. In this fragment Hippocrates (5th cent. B.C.) assumes the following construction.

:g:`$AB$ <0401.g1:/ A B />` being the diameter and :g:`$O$ <0401.g1:O>` the centre of a semicircle, and :g:`$C$ <0401.g1:C>` being the middle point of :g:`$OB$ <0401.g1:/ O B />` and :g:`$CD$ <0401.g1:/ C D />` at right angles to :g:`$AB$ <0401.g1:/ A B />`, a straight line of length such that its square is $1\frac{1}{2}$ times the square on the radius (i.e. of length $a\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}$, where $a$ is the radius) is to be so placed, as :g:`$EF$ <0401.g1:/ E F />`, between :g:`$CD$ <0401.g1:/ C D />` and the circumference :g:`$AD$ <0401.g1:( O A )>` that it "verges towards :g:`$B$ <0401.g1:B>`," that is, :g:`$EF$ <0401.g1:/ E F />` when produced passes through :g:`$B$ <0401.g1:B>`.

.. picture:: 0401.g1.svg
   :alt: Diagram

Now the right-angled triangles :g:`$BFC$ <0401.g1:< B F C >>`, :g:`$BAE$ <0401.g1:< B A E >>` are similar, so that

$$BF : BC = BA : BE,$$

and therefore the rectangle $BE, BF = \text{rect. } BA, BC$

$$= \text{sq. on } BO.$$

In other words, :g:`$EF$ <0401.g1:/ E F />` ($= a\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}$) being given in length, :g:`$BF$ <0401.g1:/ B F />` ($= x$, say) has to be found such that

$$\left(\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}a + x\right)x = a^2;$$

or the quadratic equation

$$\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}ax + x^2 = a^2$$

has to be solved.

A straight line of length $a\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}$ would easily be constructed, for, in the figure, $CD^2 = AC \cdot CB = \frac{3}{4}a^2$, or $CD = \frac{1}{2}a\sqrt{3}$, and $a\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}$ is the diagonal of a square of which :g:`$CD$ <0401.g1:/ C D />` is the side.

There is no doubt that Hippocrates could have solved the equation by the geometrical construction given below, but he may have contemplated, on this occasion, the merely *mechanical* process of *placing* the straight line of the length required between :g:`$CD$ <0401.g1:/ C D />` and the circumference :g:`$AD$ <0401.g1:( O A )>` and *moving* it until :g:`$E$ <0401.g1:E>`, :g:`$F$ <0401.g1:F>`, :g:`$B$ <0401.g1:B>` were in a straight line. Zeuthen (*Die Lehre von den Kegelschnitten im Altertum*, pp. 270, 271) thinks this probable because, curiously enough, the fragment speaks immediately afterwards of "joining :g:`$B$ <0401.g1:B>` to :g:`$F$ <0401.g1:F>`."

To solve the equation

$$ax + x^2 = b^2$$

we have to find the rectangle :g:`$AH$ <0401.g2:< A H >>`, or the gnomon :g:`$NOP$ <0401.g2:< N O P >>`, which is equal in area to $b^2$ and has one of the sides containing the inner right angle equal to :g:`$CB$ <0401.g2:/ C B />` or $\frac{1}{2}a$. Thus we know $\left(\frac{1}{2}a\right)^2$ and $b^2$, and we have to find, by I. 47, a square equal to the sum of two given squares.

.. picture:: 0401.g2.svg
   :alt: Diagram

To do this Simson draws :g:`$BQ$ <0401.g2:/ B Q />` at right angles to :g:`$AB$ <0401.g2:/ A B />` and equal to $b$, joins :g:`$CQ$ <0401.g2:/ C Q />` and, with centre :g:`$C$ <0401.g2:C>` and radius :g:`$CQ$ <0401.g2:/ C Q />`, describes a circle cutting :g:`$AB$ <0401.g2:/ A B />` produced in :g:`$D$ <0401.g2:D>`. Thus :g:`$BD$ <0401.g2:/ B D />`, or $x$, is found.

Now the rectangle :g:`$AD$ <0401.g2:/ A D />`, :g:`$DB$ <0401.g2:/ D B />` together with the square on :g:`$CB$ <0401.g2:/ C B />`
is equal to the square on :g:`$CD$ <0401.g2:/ C D />`,
i.e. to the square on :g:`$CQ$ <0401.g2:/ C Q />`,
i.e. to the squares on :g:`$CB$ <0401.g2:/ C B />`, :g:`$BQ$ <0401.g2:/ B Q />`.
