An interesting variation of the form of this proposition may be obtained by regarding <span data-ref="0402.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0402.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> as two given straight lines of which <span data-ref="0402.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is the greater, and <span data-ref="0402.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> as the *difference* between the two straight lines. Thus the proposition shows that the squares on two straight lines are together equal to twice the rectangle contained by them and the square on their difference. That is, *the square on the difference of two straight lines is equal to the sum of the squares on the straight lines diminished by twice the rectangle contained by them*. In other words, just as II. 4 is the geometrical equivalent of the identity
\[
(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab,
\]
so II. 7 proves that
\[
(a - b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab.
\]
The addition and subtraction of these formulae give the algebraical equivalent of the propositions II. 9, 10 and II. 8 respectively; and we have accordingly a suggestion of alternative methods of proving those propositions.

### PROPOSITION 8.

<p class="enunciation">If a straight line be cut at random, four times the rectangle<br />
contained by the whole and one of the segments together with<br />
the square on the remaining segment is equal to the square<br />
described on the whole and the aforesaid segment as on one<br />
straight line.</p>

For let a straight line <span data-ref="0404.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> be cut at random at the point <span data-ref="0404.g1:C">$C$</span>;  
I say that four times the rectangle contained by <span data-ref="0404.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0404.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> together with the square on <span data-ref="0404.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> is equal to the square described on <span data-ref="0404.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0404.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> as on one straight line.
