### Important deductions from II. 9, 10.

I. Pappus (VII. pp. 856—8) uses II. 9, 10 for proving the well-known theorem that

*The sum of the squares on two sides of a triangle is equal to twice the square on half the base together with twice the square on the straight line joining the middle point of the base to the opposite vertex.*

Let <span data-ref="0415.g2:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> be the given triangle and <span data-ref="0415.g2:D">$D$</span> the middle point of the base <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ B C /">$BC$</span>. Join <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span>, and draw <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span> perpendicular to <span data-ref="0415.g3:/ B C /">$BC$</span> (produced if necessary).

Now, by II. 9, 10,
the squares on <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ B E /">$BE$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ E C /">$EC$</span> are equal to twice the squares on <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ B D /">$BD$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ D E /">$DE$</span>.

Add to each twice the square on <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span>.

Then, remembering that
the squares on <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ B E /">$BE$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ E A /">$EA$</span> are equal to the square on <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ B A /">$BA$</span>,
the squares on <span data-ref="0415.g3:/ A E /">$AE$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g3:/ E C /">$EC$</span> are equal to the square on <span data-ref="0415.g3:/ A C /">$AC$</span>,
and the squares on <span data-ref="0415.g3:/ A E /">$AE$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g3:/ E D /">$ED$</span> are equal to the square on <span data-ref="0415.g3:/ A D /">$AD$</span>,
we find that
the squares on <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ B A /">$BA$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ A C /">$AC$</span> are equal to twice the squares on <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g2:/ B D /">$BD$</span>.

The proposition is generally proved by means of II. 12, 13, but not, I think, so conveniently as by the method of Pappus.

II. The inference was early made by Gregory of St. Vincent (1584–1667) and Viviani (1622–1703) that *In any parallelogram the squares on the diagonals are together equal to the squares on the sides, or to twice the squares on adjacent sides.*

III.
