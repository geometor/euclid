INTRODUCTION.

CHAPTER I.
----------

EUCLID AND THE TRADITIONS ABOUT HIM.
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

As in the case of the other great mathematicians of Greece, so in Euclid's case, we have only the most meagre particulars of the life and personality of the man.

Most of what we have is contained in the passage of Proclus' summary relating to him, which is as follows\ [#f1]_:

   "Not much younger than these (sc. Hermotimus of Colophon and Philippus of Medma) is :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, who put together the Elements, collecting many of Eudoxus' theorems, perfecting many of Theaetetus', and also bringing to irrefragable demonstration the things which were only somewhat loosely proved by his predecessors. This man lived\ [#f2]_ in the time of the first :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>`. For :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>`, who came immediately after the first (:person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>`)\ [#f3]_, makes mention of :person:`Euclid <euclid>`: and, further, they say that :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` once asked him if there was in geometry any shorter way than that of the elements, and he answered that there was no royal road to geometry\ [#f4]_. He is then younger than the pupils of :person:`Plato <plato>` but older than :person:`Eratosthenes <eratosthenes>` and :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>`; for the latter were contemporary with one another, as :person:`Eratosthenes <eratosthenes>` somewhere says."

This passage shows that even :person:`Proclus <proclus>` had no direct knowledge of Euclid's birthplace or of the date of his birth or death. He proceeds by inference. Since :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` lived just after the first
