He is speaking about Apollonius' preface to the first book of his *Conics*, where he says that :person:`Euclid <euclid>` had not completely worked out the synthesis of the "three- and four-line locus," which in fact was not possible without some theorems first discovered by himself. :person:`Pappus <pappus>` says on this\ [#f1]_: "Now :person:`Euclid <euclid>`—regarding :person:`Aristaeus <aristaeus>` as deserving credit for the discoveries he had already made in conics, and without anticipating him or wishing to construct anew the same system (such was his scrupulous fairness and his exemplary kindliness towards all who could advance mathematical science to however small an extent), being moreover in no wise contentious and, though exact, yet no braggart like the other [:person:`Apollonius <apollonius>`]—wrote so much about the locus as was possible by means of the conics of :person:`Aristaeus <aristaeus>`, without claiming completeness for his demonstrations." It is however evident, when the passage is examined in its context, that :person:`Pappus <pappus>` is not following any tradition in giving this account of :person:`Euclid <euclid>`: he was offended by the terms of Apollonius' reference to :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, which seemed to him unjust, and he drew a fancy picture of :person:`Euclid <euclid>` in order to show :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>` in a relatively unfavourable light.

Another story is told of :person:`Euclid <euclid>` which one would like to believe true. According to :person:`Stobaeus <stobaeus>`\ [#f2]_, "some one who had begun to read geometry with :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, when he had learnt the first theorem, asked :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, 'But what shall I get by learning these things?' :person:`Euclid <euclid>` called his slave and said 'Give him threepence, since he must make gain out of what he learns.'"

In the middle ages most translators and editors spoke of :person:`Euclid <euclid>` as :person:`Euclid <euclid>` of Megara. This description arose out of a confusion between our :person:`Euclid <euclid>` and the philosopher :person:`Euclid <euclid>` of Megara who lived about 400 B.C. The first trace of this confusion appears in Valerius Maximus (in the time of Tiberius) who says\ [#f3]_ that :person:`Plato <plato>`, on being appealed to for a solution of the problem of doubling the cubical altar, sent the inquirers to ":person:`Euclid <euclid>` the geometer." There is no doubt about the reading, although an early commentator on Valerius Maximus wanted to correct "*Eucliden*" into "*Eudoxum*," and this correction is clearly right. But, if Valerius Maximus took :person:`Euclid <euclid>` the geometer for a contemporary of :person:`Plato <plato>`, it could only be through confusing him with :person:`Euclid <euclid>` of Megara. The first specific reference to :person:`Euclid <euclid>` as :person:`Euclid <euclid>` of Megara belongs to the 14th century, occurring in the ὑπομνηματισμοί of Theodorus Metochita (d. 1332) who speaks of ":person:`Euclid <euclid>` of Megara, the Socratic philosopher, contemporary of :person:`Plato <plato>`," as the author of treatises on plane and solid geometry, data, optics etc.: and a Paris MS. of the 14th century has "Euclidis philosophi Socratici liber elementorum." The misunderstanding was general in the period from Campanus' translation (Venice 1482) to those of :person:`Tartaglia <tartaglia>` (Venice 1565) and Candalla (Paris 1566).
