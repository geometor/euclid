4. The *Porisms*.
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

It is not possible to give in this place any account of the controversies about the contents and significance of the three lost books of *Porisms*, or of the important attempts by Robert :person:`Simson <simson>` and :person:`Chasles <chasles>` to restore the work. These may be said to form a whole literature, references to which will be found most abundantly given by :person:`Heiberg <heiberg>` and :person:`Loria <loria>`, the former of whom has treated the subject from the philological point of view, most exhaustively, while the latter, founding himself generally on :person:`Heiberg <heiberg>`, has added useful details, from the mathematical side, relating to the attempted restorations, etc.\ [#f2]_ It must suffice here to give an extract from the only original source of information about the nature and contents of the *Porisms*, namely :person:`Pappus <pappus>`\ [#f3]_. In his general preface about the books composing the *Treasury of Analysis* (:term:`τόπος ἀναλυόμενος <topos-analyomenos>`) he says:

   "After the *Tangencies* (of :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>`) come, in three books, the *Porisms* of :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, [in the view of many] a collection most ingeniously devised for the analysis of the more weighty problems, [and] although nature presents an unlimited number of such porisms\ [#f4]_, [they have added nothing to what was written originally by :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, except that some before my time have shown their want of taste by adding to a few (of the propositions) second proofs, each (proposition) admitting of a definite number of demonstrations, as we have shown, and :person:`Euclid <euclid>` having given one for each, namely that which is the most lucid. These porisms embody a theory subtle, natural, necessary, and of considerable generality, which is fascinating to those who can see and produce results]. "Now all the varieties of porisms belong, neither to theorems nor problems, but to a species occupying a sort of intermediate position [so that their enunciations can be formed like those of either theorems or problems], the result being that, of the great number of geometers, some regarded them as of the class of theorems, and others of problems, looking only to the form of the proposition. But that the ancients knew better the difference between these three things is clear from the definitions. For they said that a theorem is that which is proposed with a view to the demonstration of the very thing proposed, a problem that which is thrown out with a view to the construction of the very thing proposed, and a porism that which is proposed with a view to the producing of the very thing proposed.
