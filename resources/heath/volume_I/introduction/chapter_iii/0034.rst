I. :person:`Heron <heron>`.

:person:`Proclus <proclus>` alludes to :person:`Heron <heron>` twice as *Heron mechanicus*\ [#f1]_, in another place\ [#f2]_ he associates him with Ctesibius, and in the three other passages\ [#f3]_ where :person:`Heron <heron>` is mentioned there is no reason to doubt that the same person is meant, namely :person:`Heron <heron>` of Alexandria. The date of :person:`Heron <heron>` is still a vexed question. In the early stages of the controversy much was made of the supposed relation of :person:`Heron <heron>` to Ctesibius. The best MS. of Heron's *Belopoeica* has the heading Ἥρωνος Κτησιβίου βελοποιϊκά, and an anonymous Byzantine writer of the tenth century, evidently basing himself on this title, speaks of Ctesibius as Heron's καθηγητής, "master" or "teacher." We know of two men of the name of Ctesibius. One was a barber who lived in the time of :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` Euergetes II, i.e. :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` VII, called Physcon (died 117 B.C.), and who is said to have made an improved water-organ\ [#f4]_. The other was a mechanician mentioned by Athenaeus as having made an elegant drinking-horn in the time of :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` Philadelphus (285–247 B.C.)\ [#f5]_. Martin\ [#f6]_ took the Ctesibius in question to be the former and accordingly placed :person:`Heron <heron>` at the beginning of the first century B.C., say 126–50 B.C. But Philo of Byzantium\ [#f7]_, who repeatedly mentions Ctesibius by name, says that the first mechanicians had the advantage of being under kings who loved fame and supported the arts. Hence our Ctesibius is more likely to have been the earlier Ctesibius who was contemporary with :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` II Philadelphus.

But, whatever be the date of Ctesibius, we cannot safely conclude that :person:`Heron <heron>` was his immediate pupil. The title "Heron's (edition of) Ctesibius's *Belopoeica*" does not, in fact, justify any inference as to the interval of time between the two works.

We now have better evidence for a *terminus post quem*. The *Metrica* of :person:`Heron <heron>`, besides quoting :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` and :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>`, twice refers to "the books about straight lines (chords) in a circle" (ἐν τοῖς περὶ τῶν ἐν κύκλῳ :term:`εὐθειῶν <straight-line>`). Now we know of no work giving a Table of Chords earlier than that of Hipparchus. We get, therefore, at once, 150 B.C. or thereabouts as the *terminus post quem*. But, again, Heron's *Mechanica* quotes a definition of "centre of gravity" as given by ":person:`Posidonius <posidonius>`, a Stoic": and, even if this :person:`Posidonius <posidonius>` lived before :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>`, as the context seems to imply, it is certain that another work of Heron's, the *Definitions*, owes something to :person:`Posidonius <posidonius>` of Apamea or Rhodes, Cicero's teacher (135–51 B.C.). This brings Heron's date down to the end of the first century B.C., at least.
