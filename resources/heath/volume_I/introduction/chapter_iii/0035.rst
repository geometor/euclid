Nor is it possible to establish inter-dependence between :person:`Vitruvius <vitruvius>` and :person:`Heron <heron>`; the differences between them seem on the whole more numerous and important than the resemblances (e.g. :person:`Vitruvius <vitruvius>` uses 3 as the value of $\pi$, while :person:`Heron <heron>` always uses the Archimedean value $3\frac{1}{7}$). The inference is that :person:`Heron <heron>` can hardly have written earlier than the first century A.D.

The most recent theory of Heron's date makes him later than Claudius :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` the astronomer (100–178 A.D.). The arguments are mainly these. (1) :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` claims as a discovery of his own a method of measuring the distance between two places (as an arc of a great circle on the earth's surface) in the case where the places are neither on the same meridian nor on the same parallel circle. :person:`Heron <heron>`, in his *Dioptra*, speaks of this method as of a thing generally known to experts. (2) The dioptra described in Heron's work is a fine and accurate instrument, much better than anything :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` had at his disposal. (3) :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>`, in his work Περὶ ῥοπῶν, asserted that water with water round it has no weight and that the diver, however deep he dives, does not feel the weight of the water above him. :person:`Heron <heron>`, strangely enough, accepts as true what :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` says of the diver, but is dissatisfied with the explanation given by "some," namely that it is because water is uniformly heavy—this seems to be equivalent to Ptolemy's dictum that water in water has no weight—and he essays a different explanation based on :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>`. (4) It is suggested that the Dionysius to whom :person:`Heron <heron>` dedicated his *Definitions* is a certain Dionysius who was *praefectus urbi* in 301 A.D.

On the other hand :person:`Heron <heron>` was earlier than :person:`Pappus <pappus>`, who was writing under Diocletian (284–305 A.D.), for :person:`Pappus <pappus>` alludes to and draws upon the works of :person:`Heron <heron>`. The net result, then, of the most recent research is to place :person:`Heron <heron>` in the third century A.D. and perhaps little earlier than :person:`Pappus <pappus>`. :person:`Heiberg <heiberg>`\ [#f1]_ accepts this conclusion, which may therefore, perhaps, be said to hold the field for the present\ [#f2]_.

That :person:`Heron <heron>` wrote a systematic commentary on the *Elements* might be inferred from :person:`Proclus <proclus>`, but it is rendered quite certain by references to the commentary in Arabian writers, and particularly in an-Nairīzī's commentary on the first ten Books of the *Elements*. The :cite:`*Fihrist* <ibn-al-nadim:fihrist>` says, under :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, that ":person:`Heron <heron>` wrote a commentary on this book [the *Elements*], endeavouring to solve its difficulties\ [#f3]_"; and under :person:`Heron <heron>`, "He wrote: the book of explanation of the obscurities in :person:`Euclid <euclid>`\ [#f4]_..." An-Nairīzī's commentary quotes :person:`Heron <heron>` by name very frequently, and often in such a way as to leave no doubt that the author had Heron's work actually before him.
