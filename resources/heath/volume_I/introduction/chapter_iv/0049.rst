:person:`Eudemus <eudemus>`, the pupil of :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>`, who, among other works, wrote a history of arithmetic, a history of astronomy, and a history of geometry. The importance of the last mentioned work is attested by the frequent use made of it by ancient writers. That there was no other history of geometry written after the time of :person:`Eudemus <eudemus>` seems to be proved by the remark of :person:`Proclus <proclus>` in the course of his famous summary: "Those who compiled histories bring the development of this science up to this point. *Not much younger than these is Euclid\ [#f2]_*...." The loss of Eudemus' history is one of the gravest which fate has inflicted upon us, for it cannot be doubted that :person:`Eudemus <eudemus>` had before him a number of the actual works of earlier geometers, which, as before observed, seem to have vanished completely when they were superseded by the treatises of :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` and :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>`. As it is, we have to be thankful for the fragments from :person:`Eudemus <eudemus>` which such writers as :person:`Proclus <proclus>` have preserved to us.

I agree with van Pesch\ [#f3]_ that there is no sufficient reason for doubting that the work of :person:`Eudemus <eudemus>` was accessible to :person:`Proclus <proclus>` at first hand. For the later writers :person:`Simplicius <simplicius>` and :person:`Eutocius <eutocius>` refer to it in terms such as leave no room for doubt that *they* had it before them. I have already quoted a passage from Simplicius' account of the lunes of :person:`Hippocrates <hippocrates-of-chios>` to the effect that :person:`Eudemus <eudemus>` must be considered the best authority since he lived nearer the times\ [#f4]_. In the same place :person:`Simplicius <simplicius>` says\ [#f5]_, "I will set out what :person:`Eudemus <eudemus>` says word for word (κατὰ λέξιν λεγόμενα), adding only a little explanation in the shape of reference to Euclid's Elements *owing to the memorandum-like style of Eudemus* (διὰ τὸν ὑπομνηματικὸν τρόπον τοῦ Εὐδήμου) who sets out his explanations in the abbreviated form usual with ancient writers. Now in the second book of the history of geometry he writes as follows." It is not possible to suppose that :person:`Simplicius <simplicius>` would have written in this way about the style of :person:`Eudemus <eudemus>` if he had merely been copying certain passages second-hand out of some other author and had not the original work itself to refer to. In like manner, :person:`Eutocius <eutocius>` speaks of the paralogisms handed down in connexion with the attempts of :person:`Hippocrates <hippocrates-of-chios>` and Antiphon to square the circle\ [#f6]_, "with which I imagine that those are accurately acquainted who have examined (ἐπεσκεμμένους) the geometrical history of :person:`Eudemus <eudemus>` and know the Ceria Aristotelica." How could the contemporaries of :person:`Eutocius <eutocius>` have *examined* the work of :person:`Eudemus <eudemus>` unless it was still extant in his time?
