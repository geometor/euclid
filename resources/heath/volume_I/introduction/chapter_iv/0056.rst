Apollonius' *Conics*, and the curves invented by Nicomedes, Hippias and Perseus\ [#f1]_;

(16) a passage on the parallel-postulate regarded as the converse of I. 17\ [#f2]_.

Of the authors to whom Proclus was indebted in a less degree the most important is Apollonius of Perga. Two passages allude to his *Conics*\ [#f3]_, one to a work on irrationals\ [#f4]_, and two to a treatise *On the cochlias* (apparently the cylindrical helix) by Apollonius\ [#f5]_. But more important for our purpose are six references to Apollonius in connexion with elementary geometry.

(1) He appears as the author of an attempt to explain the idea of a line (possessing length but no breadth) by reference to daily experience, e.g. when we tell someone to measure, merely, the length of a road or of a wall\ [#f6]_; and doubtless the similar passage showing how we may in like manner get a notion of a surface (without depth) is his also\ [#f7]_.

(2) He gave a new general definition of an angle\ [#f8]_.

(3) He tried to prove certain axioms\ [#f9]_, and Proclus gives his attempt to prove Axiom 1, word for word\ [#f10]_.

Proclus further quotes:

(4) Apollonius' solution of the problem in Eucl. I. 10, avoiding Euclid's use of I. 9\ [#f11]_,

(5) his solution of the problem in I. 11, differing only slightly from Euclid's\ [#f12]_, and

(6) his solution of the problem in I. 23\ [#f13]_.

Heiberg\ [#f14]_ conjectures that Apollonius departed from Euclid's method in these propositions because he objected to solving problems of a more general, by means of problems of a more particular, character. Proclus however considers all three solutions inferior to Euclid's; and his remarks on Apollonius' handling of these elementary matters generally suggest that he was nettled by criticisms of Euclid in the work containing the things which he quotes from Apollonius, just as we conclude that Pappus was offended by the remarks of Apollonius about Euclid's incomplete treatment of the "three- and four-line locus\ [#f15]_." If this was the case, Proclus can hardly have got his information about these things at second-hand; and there seems to be no reason to doubt that he had the actual work of Apollonius before him. This work may have been the treatise mentioned by Marinus in the words "Apollonius in his general treatise" (Ἀπολλώνιος ἐν τῇ καθόλου πραγματείᾳ)\ [#f16]_.
