# Chapter IV Summary and Outline: Proclus and His Sources

## Executive Summary

Sir Thomas L. Heath undertakes a forensic source-critical analysis of **Proclus Diadochus** (410–485 A.D.) and his Commentary on Book I of Euclid's *Elements*. Along with the *Collection* of Pappus, Proclus' commentary constitutes the paramount surviving historical authority for pre-Euclidean Greek geometry.

Drawing primarily on the foundational source-study by **J. G. van Pesch** (*De Procli fontibus*, 1900), and evaluating earlier hypotheses by **Paul Tannery** (*La Géométrie grecque*, 1887), Heath delineates the intellectual environment, textual practices, primary library, and original contributions of Proclus:

1. **Character and Purpose of Proclus' Commentary**:
   - Educated in Alexandria (Aristotelian logic under Olympiodorus, mathematics under Heron) and Athens (Plutarch and Syrianus), Proclus was fundamentally a **Neoplatonist philosopher** rather than an inventive mathematician.
   - Mathematics served an anagogic function: a propaedeutic (*propaideia*) clearing the spiritual eye to attain intellectual apprehension of universal forms (νοερὰ γνῶσις) and elucidate Plato's *Timaeus*.
   - Origin: Transcripts of elementary lectures delivered to beginners at the Neoplatonic Academy, subsequently revised, enlarged, and deepened for a scholarly audience.
   - Textual limit: Despite expressed intentions to continue through Books II, III, and XII, Proclus never completed commentaries beyond **Book I**.

2. **Quotation Habits and Source Methodology**:
   - Proclus was a compiler of earlier authorities who systematically translated borrowed arguments into his own stylistic idiom; even when purporting to quote verbatim (*Plato says*, *Plotinus says*, *the geometer says*), he rarely transcribed word-for-word.
   - He seldom cited titles of treatises, omitting names when dealing with well-known commentaries. Consequently, an absence of citation does not imply Proclean originality.

3. **Catalogue of Directly Consulted Works**:
   - **Eudemus of Rhodes** (*History of Geometry*, *On the Angle*): The supreme 4th-century B.C. Peripatetic historical authority, cited directly for Thales, Oenopides, the Pythagoreans, and the geometric application of areas (παραβολή, ὑπερβολή, ἔλλειψις).
   - **Geminus of Rhodes** (*The Theory of the Mathematics*): 1st-century B.C. Stoic encyclopaedia of mathematical philosophy; source for the overarching classification of mathematical sciences, the theory of "uniform lines" (straight line, circle, cylindrical helix), asymptotes vs parallels, and the foundations of axioms and postulates.
   - **Earlier Commentators**: Heron of Alexandria, Porphyry, and Pappus of Alexandria.
   - **Apollonius of Perga**: A foundational work on elementary geometry (*General Treatise*, καθόλου πραγματεία) attempting to remodel definitions, prove Axiom 1, and supply alternate proofs for I.10, I.11, and I.23.
   - **Claudius Ptolemy**: Monograph on the Parallel Postulate (*On the meeting of straight lines produced from angles less than two right angles*).
   - **Posidonius**: Monograph refuting Zeno of Sidon, the Epicurean sceptic who attacked Euclid's unstated lemmas (e.g. that straight lines cannot share a common segment).
   - **Carpus mechanicus**: Astronomical treatise debating the ontological primacy of problems versus theorems.
   - **Classical and Neoplatonic Literature**: Plato, Aristotle, Archimedes, Plotinus, Syrianus, and Pythagorean mystical literature.

4. **The Eudemian Summary Controversy**:
   - The famous historical summary (pp. 64–70) divides into two sections at Euclid's appearance. Heath refutes Tannery's attribution of the whole to Geminus, concluding it represents a compilation from a Hellenistic compendium of Eudemus' history supplemented by Proclus.

5. **Proclus' Original Mathematical Contributions**:
   - Investigation into the ultimate geometric *causes* (τὴν αἰτίαν καὶ τὸ διὰ τί) of propositions (I.8, I.16, I.17, I.18, I.32, I.47).
   - Dialectical refutation of Ptolemy's parallel proof.
   - **Proclus' Own Demonstration of the Fifth Postulate**: Based on Aristotle's principle (*De Caelo* I.5) that straight lines diverging from a point at an angle separate by a distance exceeding any finite magnitude when extended indefinitely.

## Detailed Outline

### § 1. Proclus' Biography and Philosophical Outlook (pp. 29–31)
- Importance of Proclus and Pappus as surviving repositories of lost Greek mathematical history.
- Chronology: 410–485 A.D.
- Education in Alexandria (Olympiodorus, Heron) and Athens (Plutarch, Syrianus).
- Platonist teleology: mathematics subordinate to metaphysics; the "eye of the soul" and *Timaeus* cosmology.
- The elementary student audience (δυσθεωρήτους τοῖς εἰσαγομένοις) and subsequent revision for publication.

### § 2. Scope and Incompleteness of the Commentary (pp. 31–33)
- Internal evidence of unfulfilled intentions: projected discussions on Books II, III, and XII.
- Closing prayer and farewell to the reader (*ed. Friedlein*, p. 432).
- Rejection of the hypothesis of lost commentaries on later books; deconstruction of the Vatican Scholia (*Scholia Vaticana*).

### § 3. Principles of Source Criticism for Proclus (pp. 33–34)
- Nature of the commentary as a selective compilation.
- Distaste for verbatim citation; loose paraphrasing of Plato, Aristotle, Plotinus, and Euclid.
- Rare occurrence of explicit book titles.
- Criteria established by van Pesch for attributing anonymous notes to specific authorities.

### § 4. Eudemus of Rhodes as Primary Historical Source (pp. 35–38)
- Eudemus' Peripatetic historical trilogy: geometry, arithmetic, astronomy.
- Direct availability of Eudemus' *History of Geometry* to Proclus, Simplicius, and Eutocius.
- Stylistic traits: summary, memorandum style (ὑπομνηματικὸς τρόπος).
- Specific citations:
  - Thales: determination of ship distances (I.26), vertically opposite angles (I.15), bisection of circle by diameter.
  - Oenopides of Chios: perpendicular construction (I.12) as the "gnomonic line," angle copy (I.23).
  - Pythagoreans: triangle angle-sum (I.32), space-filling regular polygons, application of areas (παραβολή, ὑπερβολή, ἔλλειψις).
  - Plato and Leodamas: the analytical method.
  - The ox-sacrifice legend (I.47) traced to Apollodorus Logisticus and Plutarch rather than Eudemus.
- The Eudemian Historical Summary (pp. 37–38): Analysis of structure, non-Eudemian second half, and refutation of Tannery's Geminus hypothesis.

### § 5. Geminus of Rhodes and the Encyclopaedic Classification (pp. 38–42)
- Life, Stoic affiliation, and date (fl. *c.* 70 B.C.).
- The treatise *The Theory (or Doctrine) of the Mathematics* (τῶν μαθημάτων θεωρία).
- Geminus' systematic classification:
  - Division of mathematics into pure (arithmetic, geometry) and applied (mechanics, astronomy, optics, geodesy, harmonics, logistics).
  - Classification of curves: simple vs mixed, uniform (homoeomeric) vs non-uniform. The three uniform curves: straight line, circle, cylindrical helix.
  - Asymptotes and non-intersecting lines (ἀσύμπτωτοι): hyperbola and conchoid illustrating that convergence does not guarantee intersection.
  - Philosophical foundations: axioms vs postulates, motion of points, διορισμοί.
  - The Epicurean critique of I.20 ("obvious even to an ass").

### § 6. Apollonius of Perga's Foundational Work (pp. 42–43)
- The *General Treatise* (καθόλου πραγματεία) on the elements of geometry.
- Empirical definitions of line and surface.
- Verbatim proof of Axiom 1 (things equal to the same thing are equal to one another).
- Alternate solutions to I.10, I.11, and I.23 criticized by Proclus.

### § 7. Other Direct Sources Consulted by Proclus (pp. 43–44)
- **Claudius Ptolemy**: Monograph on the Parallel Postulate.
- **Posidonius**: Polemical work refuting Zeno of Sidon; defense of Euclidean principles against Epicurean scepticism.
- **Carpus mechanicus**: Classification of problems and theorems.
- **Syrianus**: Neoplatonic doctrine of the angle.
- Comprehensive bibliography synthesized by van Pesch (p. 44).

### § 8. Proclus' Original Contributions (pp. 44–45)
- Search for essential geometric causes (τὸ διὰ τί).
- Critical rejoinders to Pappus' axioms and proofs.
- Detailed critique of Ptolemy's attempt on the Parallel Postulate.
- **Proclus' Proof of Postulate 5**: Utilizing Aristotle's theorem on diverging straight lines.
