:person:`Heiberg <heiberg>` would reject VII. Def. 10, as to which see my note on that definition. Lastly the double definition of a solid angle (XI. Def. 11) constitutes a difficulty. The use of the word :term:`ἐπιφάνεια <surface>` suggests that the first definition may have been older than :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, and he may have quoted it from older *elements*, especially as his own definition which follows only includes solid angles contained by *planes*, whereas the other includes other sorts (cf. the words :term:`γραμμῶν <line>`, γραμμαῖς) which are also distinguished by :person:`Heron <heron>` (Def. 22). If the first definition had come last, it could have been rejected without hesitation: but it is not so easy to reject the first part up to and including "otherwise" (ἄλλως). No difficulty need be felt about the definitions of "oblong," "rhombus," and "rhomboid," which are not actually used in the *Elements*; they were no doubt taken from earlier elements and given for the sake of completeness.

As regards the axioms or, as they are called in the text, *common notions* (:term:`κοιναὶ ἔννοιαι <koinai-ennoiai>`), it is to be observed that :person:`Proclus <proclus>` says\ [#f62_1]_ that :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>` tried to prove "the axioms," and he gives Apollonius' attempt to prove Axiom 1. This shows at all events that :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>` had *some* of the axioms now appearing in the text. But how could :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>` have taken a controversial line against :person:`Euclid <euclid>` on the subject of axioms if these axioms had not been Euclid's to his knowledge? And, if they had been interpolated between Euclid's time and his own, how could :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>`, living so comparatively short a time after :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, have been ignorant of the fact? Therefore *some* of the axioms are Euclid's (whether he called them *common notions*, or *axioms*, as is perhaps more likely since :person:`Proclus <proclus>` calls them axioms): and we need not hesitate to accept as genuine the first three discussed by :person:`Proclus <proclus>`, viz. (1) things equal to the same equal to one another, (2) if equals be added to equals, wholes equal, (3) if equals be subtracted from equals, remainders equal. The other two mentioned by :person:`Proclus <proclus>` (whole greater than part, and congruent figures equal) are more doubtful, since they are omitted by :person:`Heron <heron>`, :person:`Martianus Capella <martianus-capella>`, and others. The axiom that "two lines cannot enclose a space" is however clearly an interpolation due to the fact that I. 4 appeared to require it. The others about equals added to unequals, doubles of the same thing, and halves of the same thing are also interpolated; they are connected with other interpolations, and :person:`Proclus <proclus>` clearly used some source which did not contain them.

:person:`Euclid <euclid>` evidently limited his formal axioms to those which seemed to him most essential and of the widest application; for he not unfrequently assumes other things as axiomatic, e.g. in VII. 28 that, if a number measures two numbers, it measures their difference.

The differences of reading appearing in :person:`Proclus <proclus>` suggest the question of the comparative purity of the sources used by :person:`Proclus <proclus>`, :person:`Heron <heron>` and others, and of our text.
