But the book was printed in Arabic, so that Kästner remarks that he will say as much about it as can be said about a book which one cannot read\ [#f78_2]_. The shorter form, which however, in most MSS., is in 15 Books, survives at Berlin, Munich, Oxford, British Museum (974, 1334\ [#f78_3]_, 1335), Paris (2465, 2466), India Office, and Constantinople; it was printed at Constantinople in 1801, and the first six Books at Calcutta in 1824\ [#f78_4]_.

Aṭ-Ṭūsī's work is however not a translation of Euclid's text, but a re-written :person:`Euclid <euclid>` based on the older Arabic translations. In this respect it seems to be like the Latin version of the *Elements* by :person:`Campanus <campanus>` (Campano), which was first published by Erhard Ratdolt at Venice in 1482 (the first printed edition of :person:`Euclid <euclid>`)\ [#f78_5]_. :person:`Campanus <campanus>` (13th c.) was a mathematician, and it is likely enough that he allowed himself the same liberty as aṭ-Ṭūsī in reproducing :person:`Euclid <euclid>`. Whatever may be the relation between Campanus' version and that of Athelhard of Bath (about 1120), and whether, as :person:`Curtze <curtze>` thinks\ [#f78_6]_, they both used one and the same Latin version of 10th—11th c., or whether :person:`Campanus <campanus>` used Athelhard's version in the same way as aṭ-Ṭūsī used those of his predecessors\ [#f78_7]_, it is certain that both versions came from an Arabian source, as is evident from the occurrence of Arabic words in them\ [#f78_8]_. Campanus' version is not of much service for the purpose of forming a judgment on the relative authenticity of the Greek and Arabian tradition; but it sometimes preserves traces of the purer source, as when it omits Theon's addition to VI. 33\ [#f78_9]_. A curious circumstance is that, while Campanus' version agrees with aṭ-Ṭūsī's in the number of the propositions in all the genuine Euclidean Books except V. and IX., it agrees with Athelhard's in having 34 propositions in Book V. (as against 25 in other versions), which confirms the view that the two are not independent, and also leads, as :person:`Klamroth <klamroth>` says, to this dilemma: either the additions to Book V. are Athelhard's own, or he used an Arabian :person:`Euclid <euclid>` which is not known to us\ [#f78_10]_. :person:`Heiberg <heiberg>` also notes that Campanus' Books XIV., XV. show a certain agreement with the preface to the Thābit-Isḥāq version, in which the author claims to have (1) given a method of inscribing spheres in the five regular solids, (2) carried further the solution of the problem how
