But, if the Greeks had used this construction for finding the centre of a circle, they would have considered it necessary to add a proof that no other point than that obtained by the construction can be the centre, as is clear both from the similar :term:`*reductio ad absurdum* <reductio-ad-absurdum>` in III. 1 and also from the fact that Euclid thinks it necessary to prove as a separate theorem (III. 9) that, if a point within a circle be such that three straight lines (at least) drawn from it to the circumference are equal, that point must be the centre. In fact, however, the proof amounts to no more than the remark that the two perpendicular bisectors can have no more than one point common.

And even in :person:`De Morgan <de-morgan>`'s method there is a yet unproved assumption. In order that :g:`$DO$ <0013.g1:/ D O />`, :g:`$EO$ <0013.g1:/ E O />` may meet, it is necessary that :g:`$AB$ <0013.g1:/ A B />`, :g:`$AC$ <0013.g1:/ A C />` should not be in one straight line or, in other words, that :g:`$BC$ <0013.g1:/ B C />` should not pass through :g:`$A$ <0013.g1:A>`. This results from III. 2, which therefore, strictly speaking, should precede.

To return to Euclid's own proposition III. 1, it will be observed that the demonstration only shows that the centre of the circle cannot lie on either side of :g:`$CD$ <0012.g1:/ C D />`, so that it must lie on :g:`$CD$ <0012.g1:/ C D />` or :g:`$CD$ <0012.g1:[ C D ]>` produced. It is however taken for granted rather than proved that the centre must be the middle point of :g:`$CE$ <0012.g1:/ C E />`. The proof of this by :term:`*reductio ad absurdum* <reductio-ad-absurdum>` is however so obvious as to be scarcely worth giving. The same consideration which would prove it may be used to show that *a circle cannot have more than one centre*, a proposition which, if thought necessary, may be added to III. 1 as a corollary.

:person:`Simson <simson>` observed that the proof of III. 1 could not but be by :term:`*reductio ad absurdum* <reductio-ad-absurdum>`. At the beginning of Book III. we have nothing more to base the proof upon than the definition of a circle, and this cannot be made use of unless we assume some point to be the centre. We cannot however assume that the point found by the construction is the centre, because that is the thing to be proved. Nothing is therefore left to us but to assume that some other point is the centre and then to prove that, whatever other point is taken, an absurdity results; whence we can infer that the point found is the centre.

The Porism to III. 1 is inserted, as usual, parenthetically before the words :term:`ὅπερ ἔδει ποιῆσαι <hoper-edei-poiesai>`, which of course refer to the problem itself.

PROPOSITION 2.
--------------

.. enunciation::

   If on the circumference of a circle two points be taken at random, the straight line joining the points will fall within the circle.

Let :g:`$ABC$ <0014.g1:( A B C )>` be a circle, and let two points :g:`$A$ <0014.g1:A>`, :g:`$B$ <0014.g1:B>` be taken at random on its circumference;

I say that the straight line joined from :g:`$A$ <0014.g1:A>` to :g:`$B$ <0014.g1:B>` will fall within the circle.

.. picture:: 0014.g1.svg
   :alt: Proposition 2

For suppose it does not, but, if possible, let it fall outside, as :g:`$AEB$ <0014.g1:/ A E B />`; let the centre of the circle :g:`$ABC$ <0014.g1:( A B C )>` be taken :just:`[III. 1] <III.1>`, and let it be :g:`$D$ <0014.g1:D>`; let :g:`$DA$ <0014.g1:/ D A />`, :g:`$DB$ <0014.g1:/ D B />` be joined, and let :g:`$DFE$ <0014.g1:/ D F E />` be drawn through.
