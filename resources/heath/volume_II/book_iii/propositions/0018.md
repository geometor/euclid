### PROPOSITION 5.

<p class="enunciation">If two circles cut one another, they will not have the same<br />
centre.</p>

For let the circles <span data-ref="0018.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0018.g1:( C D G )">$CDG$</span> cut one another at the points <span data-ref="0018.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0018.g1:C">$C$</span>;  
I say that they will not have the same centre.

![Proposition 5 Diagram](0018.g1.svg)

For, if possible, let it be <span data-ref="0018.g1:E">$E$</span>; let <span data-ref="0018.g1:/ E C /">$EC$</span> be joined, and let <span data-ref="0018.g1:/ E F G /">$EFG$</span> be drawn through at random.

Then, since the point <span data-ref="0018.g1:E">$E$</span> is the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0018.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>,  
<p class="assertion"><span data-ref="0018.g1:/ E C /">$EC$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0018.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span>. <span class="justification">[I. Def. 15]</span></p>

Again, since the point <span data-ref="0018.g1:E">$E$</span> is the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0018.g1:( C D G )">$CDG$</span>,  
<p class="assertion"><span data-ref="0018.g1:/ E C /">$EC$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0018.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span>.</p>

But <span data-ref="0018.g1:/ E C /">$EC$</span> was proved equal to <span data-ref="0018.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> also;  
therefore <span data-ref="0018.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> is also equal to <span data-ref="0018.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span>, the less to the greater: which is impossible.  
<p class="assertion">Therefore the point <span data-ref="0018.g1:E">$E$</span> is not the centre of the circles<br />
<span data-ref="0018.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0018.g1:( C D G )">$CDG$</span>.</p>

<p class="qed">Therefore etc. <span class="justification">Q. E. D.</span></p>

---

The propositions III. 5, 6 could be combined in one. It makes no difference whether the circles cut, or meet without cutting, so long as they do not coincide altogether; in either case they cannot have the same centre. The two cases are covered by the enunciation: *If the circumferences of two circles meet at a point they cannot have the same centre.* On the other hand, *If two circles have the same centre and one point in their circumferences common, they must coincide altogether.*
