### PROPOSITION 6.

<p class="enunciation">If two circles touch one another, they will not have the<br />
same centre.</p>

For let the two circles <span data-ref="0019.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0019.g1:( C D E )">$CDE$</span> touch one another at the point <span data-ref="0019.g1:C">$C$</span>;
I say that they will not have the same centre.

![Proposition 6 Diagram](0019.g1.svg)

For, if possible, let it be <span data-ref="0019.g1:F">$F$</span>; let <span data-ref="0019.g1:/ F C /">$FC$</span> be joined, and let <span data-ref="0019.g1:/ F E B /">$FEB$</span> be drawn through at random.

Then, since the point <span data-ref="0019.g1:F">$F$</span> is the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0019.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>,
<span data-ref="0019.g1:/ F C /">$FC$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0019.g1:/ F B /">$FB$</span>.

Again, since the point <span data-ref="0019.g1:F">$F$</span> is the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0019.g1:( C D E )">$CDE$</span>,
<span data-ref="0019.g1:/ F C /">$FC$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0019.g1:/ F E /">$FE$</span>.

But <span data-ref="0019.g1:/ F C /">$FC$</span> was proved equal to <span data-ref="0019.g1:/ F B /">$FB$</span>;
therefore <span data-ref="0019.g1:/ F E /">$FE$</span> is also equal to <span data-ref="0019.g1:/ F B /">$FB$</span>, the less to the greater: which is impossible.

Therefore <span data-ref="0019.g1:F">$F$</span> is not the centre of the circles <span data-ref="0019.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0019.g1:( C D E )">$CDE$</span>.

Therefore etc.

Q. E. D.

---

The English editions enunciate this proposition of circles touching *internally*, but the word (ἐντός) is a mere interpolation, which was no doubt made because Euclid's figure showed only the case of *internal* contact. The fact is that, in his usual manner, he chose for demonstration the more difficult case, and left the other case (that of *external* contact) to the intelligence of the reader. It is indeed sufficiently self-evident that circles touching externally cannot have the same centre; but Euclid's proof can really be used for this case too.

Camerer remarks that the proof of III. 6 seems to assume tacitly that the points <span data-ref="0019.g1:E">$E$</span> and <span data-ref="0019.g1:B">$B$</span> cannot coincide, or that circles which touch internally at <span data-ref="0019.g1:C">$C$</span> cannot meet in any other point, whereas this fact is not proved by Euclid till III. 13. But no such general assumption is necessary here; it is only necessary that *one* line drawn from the assumed common centre should meet the circles in different points; and the very notion of internal contact requires that, before one circle *meets* the other on its inner side, it must have passed through points *within* the latter circle.
