As De Morgan points out, there are here two assumptions similar to that tacitly made in the proof of III. 7, namely that <span data-ref="0026.g1:K">$K$</span> falls within the triangle <span data-ref="0026.g1:< D L M >">$DLM$</span> and <span data-ref="0026.g1:E">$E$</span> outside the triangle <span data-ref="0026.g1:< D F M >">$DFM$</span>. These facts can be proved in the same way as the assumption in III. 7. Let <span data-ref="0026.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> meet <span data-ref="0026.g1:/ F M /">$FM$</span> in <span data-ref="0026.g1:Y">$Y$</span> and <span data-ref="0026.g1:/ L M /">$LM$</span> in <span data-ref="0026.g1:Z">$Z$</span>. Then, as before, <span data-ref="0026.g1:/ M Z /">$MZ$</span> is less than <span data-ref="0026.g1:/ M L /">$ML$</span> and therefore than <span data-ref="0026.g1:/ M K /">$MK$</span>. Therefore <span data-ref="0026.g1:K">$K$</span> lies further than <span data-ref="0026.g1:Z">$Z$</span> from the foot of the perpendicular from <span data-ref="0026.g1:M">$M$</span> on <span data-ref="0026.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>. Similarly <span data-ref="0026.g1:E">$E$</span> lies further than <span data-ref="0026.g1:Y">$Y$</span> from the foot of the same perpendicular.

Heron deals with lines on *opposite* sides of the diameter through the external point in a manner similar to that adopted in his previous note.
For the case where <span data-ref="0026.g2:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:F">$F$</span> are the *second* points in which <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span> meet the circle the method answers well enough.
If <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span> is nearer the centre <span data-ref="0026.g2:D">$D$</span> than <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span> is,
<p class="assertion">sqs. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ D G /">$DG$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ G E /">$GE$</span> $=$ sqs. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ D H /">$DH$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ H F /">$HF$</span></p>
and
<p class="assertion">sqs. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ D G /">$DG$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ G A /">$GA$</span> $=$ sqs. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ D H /">$DH$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ H A /">$HA$</span>,</p>
whence, since <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ D G /">$DG$</span> $<$ <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ D H /">$DH$</span>,
it follows that <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ G E /">$GE$</span> $>$ <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ H F /">$HF$</span>,
and <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A G /">$AG$</span> $>$ <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A H /">$AH$</span>,
so that, by addition, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span> $>$ <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span>.

But, if <span data-ref="0026.g2:K">$K$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:L">$L$</span> be the points in which <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span> *first* meet the circle, the method fails, and Heron is reduced to proving, in the first instance, the property usually deduced from III. 36. He argues thus:
<span data-ref="0026.g2:< A K D )">$AKD$</span> being an obtuse angle,
<p class="assertion">sq. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span> $=$ sum of sqs. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A K /">$AK$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ K D /">$KD$</span> and twice rect. <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A K /">$AK$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ K G /">$KG$</span>. <span class="justification">[II. 12]</span></p>
<span data-ref="0026.g2:< A L D )">$ALD$</span> is also an obtuse angle, and it follows that
<p class="assertion">sum of sqs. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A K /">$AK$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ K D /">$KD$</span> and twice rect. <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A K /">$AK$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ K G /">$KG$</span> is equal to</p>
<p class="assertion">sum of sqs. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A L /">$AL$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ L D /">$LD$</span> and twice rect. <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A L /">$AL$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ L H /">$LH$</span>.</p>
Therefore, the squares on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ K D /">$KD$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ L D /">$LD$</span> being equal,
<p class="assertion">sq. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A K /">$AK$</span> and twice rect. <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A K /">$AK$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ K G /">$KG$</span> $=$ sq. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A L /">$AL$</span> and twice rect. <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A L /">$AL$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ L H /">$LH$</span>,</p>
or
<p class="assertion">sq. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A K /">$AK$</span> and rect. <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A K /">$AK$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ K E /">$KE$</span> $=$ sq. on <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A L /">$AL$</span> and rect. <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A L /">$AL$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ L F /">$LF$</span>,</p>
i.e.
<p class="assertion">rect. <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A K /">$AK$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span> $=$ rect. <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A L /">$AL$</span>, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span>.</p>
But, by the first part, <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span> $>$ <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span>.
Therefore <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A K /">$AK$</span> $<$ <span data-ref="0026.g2:/ A L /">$AL$</span>.

III. 7, 8 deal with the lengths of the several lines drawn to the circumference of a circle (1) from a point within it, (2) from a point outside it; but a similar proposition is true of straight lines drawn from a point on the circumference itself: *If any point be taken on the circumference of a circle, then, of all the straight lines which can be drawn from it to the circumference, the greatest is that in which the centre is; of any others that which is nearer to the straight line which passes through the centre is greater than one more remote; and from the same point there can be drawn to the circumference two straight lines, and only two, which are equal to one another, one on each side of the greatest line.*
