For the same reason  
<p class="assertion">the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0027.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> is also on <span data-ref="0027.g1:/ H L /">$HL$</span>.</p>

And the straight lines <span data-ref="0027.g1:/ G K /">$GK$</span>, <span data-ref="0027.g1:/ H L /">$HL$</span> have no other point common but the point <span data-ref="0027.g1:D">$D$</span>;  
<p class="assertion">therefore the point <span data-ref="0027.g1:D">$D$</span> is the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0027.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>.</p>

<p class="qed">Therefore etc. <span class="justification">Q. E. D.</span></p>

---

The result of this proposition is quoted by Aristotle, $Meteorologica$ III. 3, 373 a 13—16 (cf. note on I. 8).

III. 9 is, as De Morgan remarks, a *logical* equivalent of part of III. 7, where it is proved that every *non*-central point is *not* a point from which three equal straight lines can be drawn to the circle. Thus III. 7 says that every *not*-$A$ is *not*-$B$, and III. 9 states the equivalent fact that every $B$ is $A$. Mr H. M. Taylor does in effect make a *logical* inference of the theorem that, *If from a point three equal straight lines can be drawn to a circle, that point is the centre*, by making it a corollary to his proposition which includes the part of III. 7 referred to. Euclid does not allow himself these logical inferences, as we shall have occasion to observe elsewhere also.

Of the two proofs of this proposition given in earlier texts of Euclid, August and Heiberg regard that translated above as genuine, relegating the other, which Simson gave alone, to a place in an Appendix. Camerer remarks that the genuine proof should also have contemplated the case in which one or other of the straight lines <span data-ref="0027.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0027.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> passes through <span data-ref="0027.g1:D">$D$</span>. This would however have been a departure from Euclid's manner of taking the most obscure case for proof and leaving others to the reader.

The other proof, that selected by Simson, is as follows:  
"For let a point <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span> be taken within the circle <span data-ref="0028.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>, and from <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span> let more than two equal straight lines, namely <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span>, <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D B /">$DB$</span>, <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span>, fall on the circle <span data-ref="0028.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>;  
I say that the point <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span> so taken is the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0028.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>.

![Diagram for alternative proof of Proposition 9](0028.g1.svg)

For suppose it is not; but, if possible, let it be <span data-ref="0028.g1:E">$E$</span>, and let <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> be joined and carried through to the points <span data-ref="0028.g1:F">$F$</span>, <span data-ref="0028.g1:G">$G$</span>.  
Therefore <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span> is a diameter of the circle <span data-ref="0028.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>.  
Since, then, on the diameter <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span> of the circle <span data-ref="0028.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> a point has been taken which is not the centre of the circle, namely <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span>,  
<p class="assertion"><span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D G /">$DG$</span> is greatest, and <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span> is greater than <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D B /">$DB$</span>, and <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D B /">$DB$</span> than <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D A /">$DA$</span>.</p>
But the latter are also equal: which is impossible.  
<p class="assertion">Therefore <span data-ref="0028.g1:E">$E$</span> is not the centre of the circle.</p>
Similarly we can prove that neither is any other point except <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span>;  
<p class="assertion">therefore the point <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span> is the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0028.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>.</p>

<p class="qed"><span class="justification">Q. <span data-ref="0028.g1:E">$E$</span>. D."</span></p>

On this Todhunter correctly points out that the point $E$ might be supposed to fall *within* the angle <span data-ref="0028.g1:< A D C )">$ADC$</span>. It cannot then be shown that <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span> is greater than <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D B /">$DB$</span> and <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D B /">$DB$</span> than <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D A /">$DA$</span>, but only that either <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span> or <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D A /">$DA$</span> is less than <span data-ref="0028.g1:/ D B /">$DB$</span>; this however is sufficient for establishing the proposition.
