### PROPOSITION 10.

*A circle does not cut a circle at more points than two.*[^23_1]

For, if possible, let the circle <span data-ref="0029.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> cut the circle <span data-ref="0029.g1:( D E F )">$DEF$</span> at more points than two, namely <span data-ref="0029.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:G">$G$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:F">$F$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:H">$H$</span>;
let <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ B H /">$BH$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ B G /">$BG$</span> be joined and bisected at the points <span data-ref="0029.g1:K">$K$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:L">$L$</span>,
and from <span data-ref="0029.g1:K">$K$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:L">$L$</span> let <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ K C /">$KC$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ L M /">$LM$</span> be drawn at right angles to <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ B H /">$BH$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ B G /">$BG$</span> and carried through to the points <span data-ref="0029.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:E">$E$</span>.

![Diagram for Proposition 10](0029.g1.svg)

Then, since in the circle <span data-ref="0029.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> a straight line <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> cuts a straight line <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ B H /">$BH$</span> into two equal parts and at right angles,
the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0029.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> is on <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>. [III. 1, Por.]
Again, since in the same circle <span data-ref="0029.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> a straight line <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ N O /">$NO$</span> cuts a straight line <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ B G /">$BG$</span> into two equal parts and at right angles,
the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0029.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> is on <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ N O /">$NO$</span>.
But it was also proved to be on <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, and the straight lines <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:/ N O /">$NO$</span> meet at no point except at <span data-ref="0029.g1:P">$P$</span>;
therefore the point <span data-ref="0029.g1:P">$P$</span> is the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0029.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>.

Similarly we can prove that <span data-ref="0029.g1:P">$P$</span> is also the centre of the circle <span data-ref="0029.g1:( D E F )">$DEF$</span>;
therefore the two circles <span data-ref="0029.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0029.g1:( D E F )">$DEF$</span> which cut one another have the same centre <span data-ref="0029.g1:P">$P$</span>: which is impossible. [III. 5]
Therefore etc.

Q. E. D.

---

[^23_1]: 1. The word **circle** ($\kappa\acute{\upsilon}\kappa\lambda\omicron\varsigma$) is here employed in the unusual sense of the *circumference* ($\pi\epsilon\rho\iota\phi\acute{\epsilon}\rho\epsilon\iota\alpha$) of a circle. Cf. note on I. Def. 15.

There is nothing in the demonstration of this proposition which assumes that the circles *cut* one another; it proves that two circles cannot *meet* at more than two points, whether they cut or meet without cutting, i.e. *touch* one another.

Here again, of two demonstrations given in the earlier texts, Simson chose the second, which August and Heiberg relegate to an Appendix and which is as follows:
"For again let the circle <span data-ref="0030.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> cut the circle <span data-ref="0030.g1:( D E F )">$DEF$</span> at more points than two, namely <span data-ref="0030.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0030.g1:G">$G$</span>, <span data-ref="0030.g1:H">$H$</span>, <span data-ref="0030.g1:F">$F$</span>;
let the centre <span data-ref="0030.g1:K">$K$</span> of the circle <span data-ref="0030.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> be taken, and let <span data-ref="0030.g1:/ K B /">$KB$</span>, <span data-ref="0030.g1:/ K G /">$KG$</span>, <span data-ref="0030.g1:/ K F /">$KF$</span> be joined.
