Subject to the same preliminary investigation as that required by Euclid's proof, the proposition can also be proved directly from III. 7.

For, by III. 7, <span data-ref="0030.g2:/ G H /">$GH$</span> is the shortest straight line that can be drawn from <span data-ref="0030.g2:G">$G$</span> to the circle with centre <span data-ref="0030.g2:F">$F$</span>;
therefore <span data-ref="0030.g2:/ G H /">$GH$</span> is less than <span data-ref="0030.g2:/ G A /">$GA$</span>,
and therefore less than <span data-ref="0030.g2:/ G D /">$GD$</span>: which is absurd.

This proposition is the crucial one as regards circles which touch internally; and, when it is once established, the relative position of the circles can be completely elucidated by means of it and the propositions which have preceded it. Thus, in the annexed figure, if <span data-ref="0033.g1:F">$F$</span> be the centre of the outer circle and <span data-ref="0033.g1:G">$G$</span> the centre of the inner, and if any radius <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F Q /">$FQ$</span> of the outer circle meet the two circles in <span data-ref="0033.g1:Q">$Q$</span>, <span data-ref="0033.g1:P">$P$</span> respectively, it follows, from III. 7, III. 8, or the corresponding theorem with reference to a point *on* the circumference, that <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F A /">$FA$</span> is the maximum straight line from <span data-ref="0033.g1:F">$F$</span> to the circumference of the inner circle, <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F P /">$FP$</span> is less than <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F A /">$FA$</span>, and <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F P /">$FP$</span> diminishes in length as <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F Q /">$FQ$</span> moves round from <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F A /">$FA$</span> until <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F P /">$FP$</span> reaches its minimum length <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F B /">$FB$</span>. Hence the circles do not meet at any other point than <span data-ref="0033.g1:A">$A$</span>, and the distance <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ P Q /">$PQ$</span> cut off between them on any radius <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F Q /">$FQ$</span> of the outer circle becomes greater and greater as <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F Q /">$FQ$</span> moves round from <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F A /">$FA$</span> to <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F C /">$FC$</span> and is a maximum when <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F Q /">$FQ$</span> coincides with <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F C /">$FC$</span>, after which it diminishes again on the other side of <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F C /">$FC$</span>.

The same consideration gives the partial converse of III. 11 which forms the 6th lemma of Pappus to the first book of the *Tactiones* of Apollonius (Pappus, VII. p. 826). This is to the effect that, *if <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> are in one straight line, and on one side of <span data-ref="0033.g1:A">$A$</span>, the circles described on <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> as diameters touch (internally at the point <span data-ref="0033.g1:A">$A$</span>)*. Pappus concludes this from the fact that the circles have a common tangent at <span data-ref="0033.g1:A">$A$</span>; but the truth of it is clear from the fact that <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F P /">$FP$</span> diminishes as <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F Q /">$FQ$</span> moves away from <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ F A /">$FA$</span> on either side; whence the circles meet at <span data-ref="0033.g1:A">$A$</span> but do not cut one another.

Pappus' 5th lemma (VII. p. 824) is another partial converse, namely that, *given two circles touching internally at <span data-ref="0033.g1:A">$A$</span>, and a line <span data-ref="0033.g1:[ A B C ]">$ABC$</span> drawn from <span data-ref="0033.g1:A">$A$</span> cutting both, then, if the centre of the outer circle lies on <span data-ref="0033.g1:[ A B C ]">$ABC$</span>, so does the centre of the inner*. Pappus himself proves this, by means of the common tangent to the circles at <span data-ref="0033.g1:A">$A$</span>, in two ways. (1) The tangent is at right angles to <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> and therefore to <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>: therefore the centre of the inner circle lies on <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>. (2) By III. 32, the angles in the alternate segments of both circles are right angles, so that <span data-ref="0033.g1:/ A B C /">$ABC$</span> is a diameter of both.
