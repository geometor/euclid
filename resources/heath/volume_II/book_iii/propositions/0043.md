[^37_1]: **1. Of straight lines.** The Greek leaves these words to be understood.

[^37_5]: **5. Nearer to the diameter AD.** As <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>, <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span> are not in general parallel to <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span>, Euclid should have said "nearer to the centre."

It will be observed that Euclid's proof differs from that given in our text-books (which is Simson's) in that Euclid introduces another line <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ M N /">$MN$</span>, which is drawn so as to be equal to <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> but at right angles to <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ E K /">$EK$</span> and therefore parallel to <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span>. Simson dispenses with <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ M N /">$MN$</span> and bases his proof on a similar proof by Theodosius (*Sphaerica* I. 6). He proves that the sum of the squares on <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ E H /">$EH$</span>, <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ H B /">$HB$</span> is equal to the sum of the squares on <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ E K /">$EK$</span>, <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ K F /">$KF$</span>; whence he infers that, since the square on <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ E H /">$EH$</span> is less than the square on <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ E K /">$EK$</span>, the square on <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ B H /">$BH$</span> is greater than the square on <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ F K /">$FK$</span>. It may be that Euclid would have regarded this as too complicated an inference to make without explanation or without an increase in the number of his axioms. But, on the other hand, Euclid himself assumes that the angle subtended at the centre by <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ M N /">$MN$</span> is greater than the angle subtended by <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span>, or, in other words, that <span data-ref="0042.g1:M">$M$</span>, <span data-ref="0042.g1:N">$N$</span> both fall outside the triangle <span data-ref="0042.g1:< F E G >">$FEG$</span>. This is a similar assumption to that made in III. 7, 8, as already noticed; and its truth is obvious because <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ E M /">$EM$</span>, <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ E N /">$EN$</span>, being radii of the circle, are greater than the distances from <span data-ref="0042.g1:E">$E$</span> to the points in which <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ M N /">$MN$</span> cuts <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span>, <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span>, and therefore the latter points are nearer than <span data-ref="0042.g1:M">$M$</span>, <span data-ref="0042.g1:N">$N$</span> are to <span data-ref="0042.g1:L">$L$</span>, the foot of the perpendicular from <span data-ref="0042.g1:E">$E$</span> to <span data-ref="0042.g1:/ M N /">$MN$</span>.

Simson adds the converse of the proposition, proving it in the same way as he proves the proposition itself.

### PROPOSITION 16.
