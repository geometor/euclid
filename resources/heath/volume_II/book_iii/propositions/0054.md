*Proof.* Since <span data-ref="0054.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0054.g1:/ E D /">$ED$</span>,
the angles <span data-ref="0054.g1:< D C E )">$DCE$</span>, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< D E C )">$DEC$</span> are equal.

![Diagram for Heron's extension of Proposition 20](0054.g1.svg)

Therefore the exterior angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< G D E )">$GDE$</span> is equal to twice the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< D E C )">$DEC$</span>.
Similarly the exterior angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< F D E )">$FDE$</span> is equal to twice the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< D E B )">$DEB$</span>.
By addition, the angles <span data-ref="0054.g1:< G D E )">$GDE$</span>, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< F D E )">$FDE$</span> are double of the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B E C )">$BEC$</span>.

But
the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B D C )">$BDC$</span> is equal to the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< F D G )">$FDG$</span>,
therefore *the sum of the angles <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B D G )">$\text{BDG}$</span>, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< G D F )">$\text{GDF}$</span>, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< F D C )">$\text{FDC}$</span> is double of the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B E C )">$\text{BEC}$</span>*.

And Euclid has proved the first part of the proposition, namely that the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B D C )">$BDC$</span> is double of the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span>.

Now, says Heron, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span> is *any* angle in the segment <span data-ref="0054.g1:( B A C )">$BAC$</span>, and therefore *any* angle in the segment <span data-ref="0054.g1:( B A C )">$BAC$</span> is half of the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B D C )">$BDC$</span>.
Therefore all the angles in the segment <span data-ref="0054.g1:( B A C )">$BAC$</span> are equal.

Again, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B E C )">$BEC$</span> is *any* angle in the segment <span data-ref="0054.g1:( B E C )">$BEC$</span> and is equal to *half the sum of the angles* <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B D G )">$\text{BDG}$</span>, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< G D F )">$\text{GDF}$</span>, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< F D C )">$\text{FDC}$</span>.
Therefore all the angles in the segment <span data-ref="0054.g1:( B E C )">$BEC$</span> are equal.
Hence III. 21 is proved *generally*.

Lastly, says Heron,
since *the sum of the angles* <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B D G )">$\text{BDG}$</span>, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< G D F )">$\text{GDF}$</span>, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< F D C )">$\text{FDC}$</span> is double of the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B E C )">$BEC$</span>,
and the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B D C )">$BDC$</span> is double of the angle <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span>,
therefore, by addition, the *sum of four right angles* is double of the sum of the angles <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span>, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B E C )">$BEC$</span>.

Hence the angles <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span>, <span data-ref="0054.g1:< B E C )">$BEC$</span> are together equal to two right angles, and III. 22 is proved.

The above notes of Heron show conclusively, if proof were wanted, that Euclid had no idea of III. 20 applying *in terms* (either as a matter of enunciation or proof) to the case where the angle at the circumference, or the angle in the segment, is *obtuse*. He would not have recognised the "angle" greater than two right angles or the so-called "straight angle" as being an *angle* at all. This is indeed clear from his definition of an angle as the *inclination* κ.τ.ἑ., and from the language used by other later Greek mathematicians where there would be an opportunity for introducing the extension. Thus Proclus' notion of a "four-sided triangle" (cf. the note above on the definition of a triangle) shows that he did not count a re-entrant angle as an angle, and Zenodorus' application to the same figure of the word "hollow-angled" shows that in that case it was the exterior angle only which he would have called an angle. Further it would have been inconvenient to have introduced at the beginning of the *Elements* an "angle" equal to or greater than two right angles, because other definitions, e.g. that of a *right angle*, would have needed a qualification. If an "angle" might be equal to two right angles, one straight line in a straight line with another would have satisfied Euclid's definition of a right angle. This is noticed by Dodgson (p. 160), but it is practically brought out by Proclus on I. 13. "For he did not merely say that 'any straight line standing on a straight line either makes two right angles or angles equal to two right angles' but '*if it make angles*.'
