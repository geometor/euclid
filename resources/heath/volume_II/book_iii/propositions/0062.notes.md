## Textual, Lexical & Mathematical Observations
- **Greek Terminology (`προσαναγράψαι τὸν κύκλον`, `[^56_1]`)**:
  - Heath notes that `to describe the complete circle` translates `προσαναγράψαι τὸν κύκλον`, literally "to describe the circle *on to it*" (where the prefix `προσ-` denotes adding the missing arc onto the existing segment).
- **Historical & Methodological Comparison (Simson, Campanus, Clavius, De Morgan, H. M. Taylor)**:
  - **Simson** places the semicircle case ($\angle ABD = \angle BAD$) first and combines the other two cases by writing "produce $BD$, if necessary."
  - **Campanus and Peletarius** bisect two arbitrary non-parallel chords at right angles.
  - **Clavius, Billingsley, Barrow, and De Morgan** bisect two chords sharing a common endpoint (matching the second proof of III. 9 and the first proof of III. 10), which De Morgan proposes as a unified foundation making III. 1, III. 25, and IV. 5 corollaries of the perpendicular bisector theorem (III. 1 Porism).
  - **H. M. Taylor** chooses the base $AC$ and the chord $AB$ joining an endpoint of the base to the midpoint $B$ of the arc.
  - **Role of III. 9**: Heath emphasizes that Euclid's explicit appeal to III. 9 (`will also pass through the remaining points`) is essential to prove that the newly drawn circle actually contains the *entire continuous arc* of the given segment $ABC$, a step obscured when using four arbitrary points for two independent chords.
