Textual, Lexical & Mathematical Observations
--------------------------------------------

- **Mixed Angles ("Angle of a Segment")**: In ``[III. Def. 7]``, Euclid defines the "angle of a segment" as the mixed (curvilinear-rectilinear) angle contained by the chord and the arc of the segment. Here in III. 31, Euclid makes the only substantive use of that definition in the *Elements*:

  - Since $\angle BAC = 1\text{ right angle}$ and the mixed angle between arc $ABC$ and chord $AC$ contains $\angle BAC$ as a proper part, the angle of the greater segment is greater than a right angle.
  - Since $\angle CAF = 1\text{ right angle}$ and the mixed angle between chord $CA$ and arc $ADC$ is a proper part of $\angle CAF$, the angle of the less segment is less than a right angle.

- **Greek Citations in Aristotle Commentary**:

  - ``(τίνος ὄντος ὀρθή;)`` (*Anal. Post.* II. 11, 94a28: "In virtue of what is it right?")
  - ``(καθόλου)`` (*Metaph.* 1051a26: "universally / invariably")
  - ``(ἐκεῖνο`` (*Metaph.* 1051a26: "that [property]", referring to I. 32 that the angles of a triangle equal two right angles)

- **Pre-Euclidean Proof**: As shown in ``0069.g1.svg``, before Euclid generalized the proof to an arbitrary point $A$ on the semicircle via the exterior angle $\angle FAC$, the older proof placed the vertex at the *midpoint* of the semicircular arc, forming two congruent right-angled isosceles triangles whose base and vertex angles $\alpha$ each equal half a right angle ($45^\circ$), so the angle at the apex is $2\alpha = 1\text{ right angle}$, and then invoked III. 21 (angles in the same segment are equal) to extend the result to all points on the semicircle.
