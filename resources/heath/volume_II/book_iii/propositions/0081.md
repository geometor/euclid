Cf. note on the preceding proposition. Observe that, whereas it would be natural with us to prove first that, if <span data-ref="0080.g2:A">$A$</span> is an external point, and two straight lines <span data-ref="0080.g2:/ A E B /">$AEB$</span>, <span data-ref="0080.g2:/ A F C /">$AFC$</span> cut the circle in <span data-ref="0080.g2:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0080.g2:B">$B$</span> and <span data-ref="0080.g2:F">$F$</span>, <span data-ref="0080.g2:C">$C$</span> respectively, the rectangle <span data-ref="0080.g2:/ B A /">$BA$</span>, <span data-ref="0080.g2:/ A E /">$AE$</span> is equal to the rectangle <span data-ref="0080.g2:/ C A /">$CA$</span>, <span data-ref="0080.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span>, and thence that, the tangent from <span data-ref="0080.g2:A">$A$</span> being *a straight line like <span data-ref="0080.g2:/ A E B /">$AEB$</span> in its limiting position when <span data-ref="0080.g2:E">$E$</span> and <span data-ref="0080.g2:B">$B$</span> coincide*, either rectangle is equal to the square on the tangent (cf. Mr H. M. Taylor, p. 253), Euclid and the Greek geometers generally did not allow themselves to infer the truth of a proposition in a *limiting case* directly from the general case including it, but preferred a separate proof of the limiting case (cf. *Apollonius of Perga*, p. 40, 139—140). This accounts for the form of III. 36.
