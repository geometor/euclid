The remarks as to the number of ways in which Prop. 2 can be solved apply here also.

Euclid leaves us to satisfy ourselves that the three tangents *will* meet and form a triangle. This follows easily from the fact that each of the angles <span data-ref="0089.g1:< A K B )">$AKB$</span>, <span data-ref="0089.g1:< B K C )">$BKC$</span>, <span data-ref="0089.g1:< C K A )">$CKA$</span> is less than two right angles. The first two are so by construction, being the supplements of two angles of the given triangle respectively, and, since all three angles round <span data-ref="0089.g1:K">$K$</span> are together equal to four right angles, it follows that the third, the angle <span data-ref="0089.g1:< A K C )">$AKC$</span>, is equal to the sum of the two angles <span data-ref="0089.g1:< D E F )">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0089.g1:< D F E )">$F$</span> of the triangle, i.e. to the supplement of the angle <span data-ref="0089.g1:< E D F )">$D$</span>, and is therefore less than two right angles.

Peletarius and Borelli gave an alternative solution, first inscribing a triangle equiangular to the given triangle, by IV. 2, and then drawing tangents to the circle parallel to the sides of the inscribed triangle respectively. This method will of course give two solutions, since two tangents can be drawn parallel to each of the sides of the inscribed triangle.
