<p class="assertion">therefore the five angles <span data-ref="0106.g1:< D A C )">$DAC$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:< A C E )">$ACE$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:< E C D )">$ECD$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:< C D B )">$CDB$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:< B D A )">$BDA$</span><br />
are equal to one another.</p>

But equal angles stand on equal circumferences; <span class="justification">[III. 26]</span>
<p class="assertion">therefore the five circumferences <span data-ref="0106.g1:( A B )">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:( B C )">$BC$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:( C D )">$CD$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:( D E )">$DE$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:( E A )">$EA$</span> are<br />
equal to one another.</p>

But equal circumferences are subtended by equal straight
lines; <span class="justification">[III. 29]</span>
<p class="assertion">therefore the five straight lines <span data-ref="0106.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:/ E A /">$EA$</span> are<br />
equal to one another;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore the pentagon <span data-ref="0106.g1:< A B C D E >">$ABCDE$</span> is equilateral.</p>

I say next that it is also equiangular.

For, since the circumference <span data-ref="0106.g1:( A B )">$AB$</span> is equal to the circum-
ference <span data-ref="0106.g1:( D E )">$DE$</span>, let <span data-ref="0106.g1:( B C D )">$BCD$</span> be added to each;
<p class="assertion">therefore the whole circumference <span data-ref="0106.g1:( A B C D )">$ABCD$</span> is equal to the<br />
whole circumference <span data-ref="0106.g1:( E D C B )">$EDCB$</span>.</p>

And the angle <span data-ref="0106.g1:< A E D )">$AED$</span> stands on the circumference <span data-ref="0106.g1:( A B C D )">$ABCD$</span>,
and the angle <span data-ref="0106.g1:< B A E )">$BAE$</span> on the circumference <span data-ref="0106.g1:( E D C B )">$EDCB$</span>;
<p class="assertion">therefore the angle <span data-ref="0106.g1:< B A E )">$BAE$</span> is also equal to the angle <span data-ref="0106.g1:< A E D )">$AED$</span>. <span class="justification">[III. 27]</span></p>

For the same reason
each of the angles <span data-ref="0106.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:< B C D )">$BCD$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:< C D E )">$CDE$</span> is also equal to each
of the angles <span data-ref="0106.g1:< B A E )">$BAE$</span>, <span data-ref="0106.g1:< A E D )">$AED$</span>;
<p class="assertion">therefore the pentagon <span data-ref="0106.g1:< A B C D E >">$ABCDE$</span> is equiangular.</p>

But it was also proved equilateral;
<p class="qed">therefore in the given circle an equilateral and equi-<br />
angular pentagon has been inscribed. <span class="justification">Q. E. F.</span></p>

---

De Morgan remarks that "the method of IV. 11 is not so natural as making a direct use of the angle obtained in the last." On the other hand, if we look at the figure and notice that it shows the whole of the *pentagram-star* except one line (that connecting <span data-ref="0106.g1:B">$B$</span> and <span data-ref="0106.g1:E">$E$</span>), I think we shall conclude that the method is nearer to that used by the Pythagoreans, and therefore of much more historical interest.

Another method would of course be to use IV. 10 to describe a *decagon* in the circle, and then to join any vertex to the next alternate one, the latter to the next alternate one, and so on.
