Given three numbers _a_, _b_, _c_, to find the condition that they may have an<br />
integral fourth proportional.

The Greek text of part of this proposition is hopelessly corrupt. Accord-<br />
ing to it Euclid takes four cases.

(1) _a_, _b_, _c_ not in continued proportion, and _a_, _c_ prime to one another.

(2) _a_, _b_, _c_ in continued proportion, and _a_, _c_ not prime to one another.

(3) _a_, _b_, _c_ not in continued proportion, and _a_, _c_ not prime to one another.

(4) _a_, _b_, _c_ in continued proportion, and _a_, _c_ prime to one another.

(4) is the case dealt with in IX. 17, where it is shown that on hypothesis<br />
(4) a fourth proportional cannot be found.

The text now takes case (1) and asserts that a fourth proportional cannot<br />
be found in this case either. We have only to think of 4, 6, 9 in order to see<br />
that there is something wrong here. The supposed proof is also wrong. _If<br />
possible, says the text, let d be a fourth proportional to a, b, c, and let e<br />
be taken such that_
$$b : c = d : e.$$

Then, _ex aequali_,
$$a : c = c : e,$$
whence _a_ measures _c_ : <span class="justification">[VII. 20, 21]</span><br />
which is impossible, since _a_, _c_ are prime to one another.

But this does not prove that a fourth proportional _d_ cannot be found ; it<br />
only proves that, if _d_ is a fourth proportional, no integer _e_ can be found to<br />
satisfy the equation
$$b : c = d : e.$$

Indeed it is obvious from IX. 16 that in the equation
$$a : c = c : e$$
_e_ cannot be integral.

The cases (2) and (3) are correctly given, the first in full, and the other as<br />
a case to be proved “similarly” to it.

These two cases really give all that is necessary.

Let the product _bc_ be taken.

Then, if _a_ measures _bc_, suppose $bc = ad$ ;<br />
therefore
$$a : b = c : d,$$
and _d_ is a fourth proportional.

But, if _a_ does _not_ measure _bc_, no fourth proportional can be found.<br />
For, if _x_ were a fourth proportional, _ax_ would be equal to _bc_, and _a_ would<br />
measure _bc_.

The sufficient condition in any case for the possibility of finding a fourth<br />
proportional to _a_, _b_, _c_ is that _a_ should measure _bc_.

Theon appears to have corrected the proof by leaving out the incorrect<br />
portion which I have included between daggers and the last case (3) dealt<br />
with in the last lines. Also, in accordance with this arrangement, he does not<br />
distinguish four cases at the beginning but only two. “Either <span data-ref="0415.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g1:C">$C$</span> are<br />
in continued proportion and the extremes of them <span data-ref="0415.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g1:C">$C$</span> are prime to one<br />
another ; or not.” Then, instead of introducing case (2) by the words<br />
“Next let <span data-ref="0415.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0415.g1:C">$C$</span>...to find a fourth proportional to them,” immediately<br />
following the second dagger above, Theon merely says “_But, if not_,” [i.e.<br />
if it is not the case that _a_, _b_, _c_ are in G.P. and _a_, _c_ prime to one another] “let<br />
<span data-ref="0415.g1:B">$B$</span> by multiplying <span data-ref="0415.g1:C">$C$</span> make <span data-ref="0415.g1:D">$D$</span>,” and so on.
