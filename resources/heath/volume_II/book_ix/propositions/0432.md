I append a few details of discoveries of further perfect numbers after the<br />
first four. The next are as follows :

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| fifth, | $2^{12} (2^{13} - 1) = 33\ 550\ 336$ |
| sixth, | $2^{16} (2^{17} - 1) = 8\ 589\ 869\ 056$ |
| seventh, | $2^{18} (2^{19} - 1) = 137\ 438\ 691\ 328$ |
| eighth, | $2^{30} (2^{31} - 1) = 2\ 305\ 843\ 008\ 139\ 952\ 128$ |
| ninth, | $2^{60} (2^{61} - 1) = 2\ 658\ 455\ 991\ 569\ 831\ 744\ 654\ 692\ 615\ 953\ 842\ 176$ |
| tenth, | $2^{88} (2^{89} - 1)$. |

It has further been proved that $2^{107} - 1$ is prime, and so is $2^{127} - 1$. Hence<br />
$2^{106} (2^{107} - 1)$ and $2^{126} (2^{127} - 1)$ are two more perfect numbers.

The fifth perfect number may have been known to Iamblichus, though he<br />
does not give it ; it was however known, with all its factors, in the fifteenth<br />
century, as appears from a tract written in German which was discovered by<br />
Curtze (Cod. lat. Monac. 14908). The first eight perfect numbers were<br />
calculated by Jean Prestet (d. 1670). Fermat had stated, and Euler proved,<br />
that $2^{31} - 1$ is prime. The ninth perfect number was found by P. Seelhoff<br />
(*Zeitschrift für Math. u. Physik*, XXXI., 1886, pp. 174–8) and verified by<br />
E. Lucas (*Mathésis*, VII., 1887, pp. 45–6). The tenth was discovered by<br />
R. E. Powers (see *Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society*, XVIII., 1912,<br />
p. 162). $2^{107} - 1$ was proved to be prime by E. Fauquembergue and R. E.<br />
Powers (1914), while Fauquembergue proved that $2^{127} - 1$ is prime.

There have been attempts, so far unsuccessful, to solve the question<br />
whether there exist other “perfect numbers” than those of Euclid, and, in<br />
particular, perfect numbers which are odd. (Cf. several notes by Sylvester in<br />
*Comptes rendus*, CVI., 1888 ; Catalan, “Mélanges mathématiques” in *Mém. de<br />
la Soc. de Liége*, $2^{\text{e}}$ Série, xv., 1888, pp. 205–7 ; C. Servais in *Mathésis*, VII.,<br />
pp. 228–30 and VIII., pp. 92–93, 135 ; E. Cesàro in *Mathésis*, VII.,<br />
pp. 245–6 ; E. Lucas in *Mathésis*, X., pp. 74–6).

For the detailed history of the whole subject see L. E. Dickson, *History<br />
of the Theory of Numbers*, Vol. I., 1919, pp. iii–iv, 3–33.
