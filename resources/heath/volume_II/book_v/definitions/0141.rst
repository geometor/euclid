In order to distinguish the two senses, I have always used the word :term:`*componendo* <componendo>` where the sense is that of this definition, though this requires a slight departure from the literal rendering of some passages. Thus the enunciation of V. 17 says, literally, "if magnitudes compounded be in proportion they will also be in proportion separated" (ἐὰν συγκείμενα :term:`μεγέθη <megethos>` :term:`ἀνάλογον <proportional>` ᾖ, καὶ διαιρεθέντα :term:`ἀνάλογον <proportional>` ἔσται). This practically means that, if $A + B$ is to $B$ as $C + D$ is to $D$, then $A$ is to $B$ as $C$ is to $D$. I have accordingly translated as follows: "if magnitudes be proportional :term:`*componendo* <componendo>`, they will also be proportional :term:`*separando* <separando>`." (It will be observed that :term:`*separando* <separando>`, a term explained in the next note, is here used, not relatively to the proportion $A$ is to $B$ as $C$ is to $D$, but relatively to the proportion :term:`*componendo* <componendo>`, viz. $A + B$ is to $B$ as $C + D$ is to $D$.) The corresponding term for :term:`*componendo* <componendo>` in the Greek mathematicians is :term:`συνθέντι <componendo>`, literally "to one who has compounded," i.e. "if we compound." (For this absolute use of the dative of the participle cf. :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` I. 8, 9 ἀπὸ :term:`μονάδος <unit>`...κατὰ τὸν διπλάσιον :term:`λόγον <ratio>` προχωροῦντι μέχρις ἀπείρου, ὅσοι καὶ ἂν γένωνται, οὗτοι πάντες ἀρτιάκις ἄρτιοί εἰσιν. A very good instance from :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` is :cite:`*Eth. Nic.* I. 5, 1097 b 12 <aristotle:eth-nic:i-5-1097-b-12>` ἐπεκτείνοντι γὰρ ἐπὶ τοὺς γονεῖς καὶ τοὺς ἀπογόνους καὶ τῶν φίλων τοὺς φίλους εἰς ἄπειρον πρόεισιν.) A variation for :term:`συνθέντι <componendo>` found in :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` is :term:`κατὰ σύνθεσιν <componendo>`. Perhaps the more exclusive use of the form :term:`συνθέντι <componendo>` by geometers later than Euclid to denote the :term:`*composition of a ratio* <componendo>`, as compared with Euclid's more general use of :term:`σύνθεσις <synthesis>` and other parts of the verb συντίθημι or σύγκειμαι, may point to a desire to get rid of ambiguity of terms and to make the terminology of geometry more exact.

DEFINITION 15.
--------------

**Διαίρεσις λόγου ἐστὶ λῆψις τῆς ὑπεροχῆς, ᾗ ὑπερέχει τὸ ἡγούμενον τοῦ ἑπομένου, πρὸς αὐτὸ τὸ ἑπόμενον.**

As :term:`*composition of a ratio* <componendo>` means the transformation, e.g., of the ratio of $A$ to $B$ into the ratio of $A + B$ to $B$, so the :term:`*separation of a ratio* <separando>` indicates the transformation of it into the ratio of $A - B$ to $B$. Thus, as the new antecedent is in one case got by *adding* the original antecedent to the original consequent, so the antecedent in the other case is obtained by *subtracting* the original consequent from the original antecedent (it being assumed that the latter is greater than the former). Hence the literal translations of :term:`διαίρεσις λόγου <separando>`, "division of a ratio," and of :term:`διελόντι <separando>` (the corresponding term to :term:`συνθέντι <componendo>`) as :term:`*dividendo* <separando>`, scarcely give a sufficiently obvious explanation of the meaning. :person:`Heiberg <heiberg>` accordingly translates by "subtractio rationis," which again may be thought to depart too far from the Greek. Perhaps "separation" and :term:`*separando* <separando>` may serve as a compromise.

DEFINITION 16.
--------------

**Ἀναστροφὴ λόγου ἐστὶ λῆψις τοῦ ἡγουμένου πρὸς τὴν ὑπεροχήν, ᾗ ὑπερέχει τὸ ἡγούμενον τοῦ ἑπομένου.**

*Conversion* of a ratio means taking, e.g., instead of the ratio of $A$ to $B$, the ratio of $A$ to $A - B$ ($A$ being again supposed greater than $B$). As ἀναστροφή is used for *conversion*, so :term:`ἀναστρέψαντι <convertendo>` is used for :term:`*convertendo* <convertendo>` (corresponding to the terms :term:`συνθέντι <componendo>` and :term:`διελόντι <separando>`).
