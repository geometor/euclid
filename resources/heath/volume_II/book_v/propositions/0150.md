As will be seen, Simson's emendations amount to alterations of the text so considerable as to suggest doubt whether we should be justified in making them in the absence of MS. authority. The phrase "equimultiples of <span data-ref="0148.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:C">$C$</span> and other, chance, equimultiples of <span data-ref="0148.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:D">$D$</span>" recurs so constantly as to suggest that it was for Euclid a quasi-stereotyped phrase, and that it is equally genuine wherever it occurs. Is it then absolutely necessary to insert $\ddot{\alpha} \text{ } \ἔ\tau\upsilon\chi\epsilon$ in places where it does not occur, and to leave it out in the places where Simson holds it to be wrong? I think the text can be defended as it stands. In the first place to say "take equimultiples of <span data-ref="0148.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:C">$C$</span>" is a fair enough way of saying take *any* equimultiples *whatever* of <span data-ref="0148.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:C">$C$</span>. The other difficulty is greater, but may, I think, be only due to the adoption of *any whatever* as the translation of $\ddot{\alpha} \text{ } \ἔ\tau\upsilon\chi\epsilon$. As a matter of fact, the words only mean *chance* equimultiples, equimultiples which are the result of random selection. Is it not justifiable to describe the product of two chance numbers, numbers selected at random, as being a "*chance* number," since it is the result of two random selections? I think so, and I have translated $\ddot{\alpha} \text{ } \ἔ\tau\upsilon\chi\epsilon$ accordingly as implying, in the case in question, "other equimultiples whatever they may happen to be."

To this proposition Theon added the following:

"Since then it was proved that, if <span data-ref="0148.g1:K">$K$</span> is in excess of <span data-ref="0148.g1:M">$M$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:L">$L$</span> is also in excess of <span data-ref="0148.g1:N">$N$</span>, if it is equal, (the other is) equal, and if less, less,
it is clear also that,
if <span data-ref="0148.g1:M">$M$</span> is in excess of <span data-ref="0148.g1:K">$K$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:N">$N$</span> is also in excess of <span data-ref="0148.g1:L">$L$</span>, if it is equal, (the other is) equal, and if less, less;
and for this reason,

$$\text{as } G \text{ is to } E, \text{ so also is } H \text{ to } F.$$

PORISM. From this it is manifest that, if four magnitudes be proportional, they will also be proportional inversely."

Simson rightly pointed out that the demonstration of what Theon intended to prove, viz. that, if <span data-ref="0148.g1:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:G">$G$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:F">$F$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:H">$H$</span> be proportionals, they are proportional inversely, i.e. <span data-ref="0148.g1:G">$G$</span> is to <span data-ref="0148.g1:E">$E$</span> as <span data-ref="0148.g1:H">$H$</span> is to <span data-ref="0148.g1:F">$F$</span>, does not in the least depend upon this 4th proposition or the proof of it; for, when it is said that, "if <span data-ref="0148.g1:K">$K$</span> exceeds <span data-ref="0148.g1:M">$M$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:L">$L$</span> also exceeds <span data-ref="0148.g1:N">$N$</span> etc.," this is not proved from the fact that <span data-ref="0148.g1:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:G">$G$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:F">$F$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:H">$H$</span> are proportionals (which is the conclusion of Prop. 4), but from the fact that <span data-ref="0148.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:C">$C$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:D">$D$</span> are proportionals.

The proposition that, if <span data-ref="0148.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:C">$C$</span>, <span data-ref="0148.g1:D">$D$</span> are proportionals, they are also proportionals inversely is not given by Euclid, but Simson supplies the proof in his Prop. B. The fact is really obvious at once from the 5th definition of Book V. (cf. p. 127 above), and Euclid probably omitted the proposition as unnecessary.

Simson added, in place of Theon's corollary, the following:

"Likewise, if the first has the same ratio to the second which the third has to the fourth, then also any equimultiples whatever of the first and third have the same ratio to the second and fourth: And, in like manner, the first and the third have the same ratio to any equimultiples whatever of the second and fourth."
