## Textual, Lexical & Mathematical Observations
- **Heath's Defense of the Manuscript Tradition ($\ddot{\alpha} \text{ } \ἔ\tau\upsilon\chi\epsilon\nu$)**: Heath demonstrates that Simson's objection stems from translating $\ddot{\alpha} \text{ } \ἔ\tau\upsilon\chi\epsilon\nu$ as "any whatever" (universal quantification) rather than "chance" / "whatever they may happen to be" (an arbitrary or random instance). Because the product of two randomly chosen multipliers $q$ and $n$ is still a chance multiplier $qn$, Euclid's stereotyped phrase $\ddot{\alpha}\lambda\lambda\alpha, \ddot{\alpha} \text{ } \ἔ\tau\upsilon\chi\epsilon\nu, \ἰ\sigma\ά\kappa\iota\varsigma \text{ } \pi\omicron\lambda\lambda\alpha\pi\lambda\ά\sigma\iota\alpha$ is both philologically natural and logically sound without altering the Greek text.
- **Theon's Porism and Inversion of Ratios ($\ἀ\nu\ά\pi\alpha\lambda\iota\nu$, Simson's Prop. B)**: Theon appended a corollary/porism to V. 4 attempting to deduce that if four magnitudes are proportional, they are also proportional inversely ($A : B = C : D \implies B : A = D : C$). As Simson observed, this follows immediately from V. Def. 5 without V. 4, andSimson made it a separate proposition ("Prop. B").
- **Simson's Corollary to V. 4 (Case $m=1$ or $n=1$)**: Because Euclidean multiples ($ma$) ordinarily mean $m \ge 2$, the cases $ma : b = mc : d$ (where $n=1$) and $a : nb = c : nd$ (where $m=1$) are stated explicitly in Simson's corollary.
