Euclid's proof amounts to this.

Suppose a magnitude $x$ taken such that

$$ma - mb = mx, \text{ say.}$$

Add $mb$ to each side, whence (by V. 1)

$$ma = m(x + b).$$

Therefore

$$a = x + b, \text{ or } x = a - b,$$

so that

$$ma - mb = m(a - b).$$

Simson's proof, on the other hand, argues thus.

Take $x = m(a - b)$, the same multiple of $(a - b)$ that $mb$ is of $b$.

Then, by addition of $mb$ to both sides, we have <span class="justification">[V. 1]</span>

$$x + mb = ma,$$

or

$$x = ma - mb.$$

That is,

$$ma - mb = m(a - b).$$

### PROPOSITION 6.

<p class="enunciation">If two magnitudes be equimultiples of two magnitudes, and<br />
any magnitudes subtracted from them be equimultiples of the<br />
same, the remainders also are either equal to the same or equi-<br />
multiples of them.</p>

For let two magnitudes $AB, CD$ be equimultiples of two<br />
magnitudes $E, F$, and let $AG, CH$<br />
subtracted from them be equi-<br />
multiples of the same two $E, F$;<br />
I say that the remainders also, $GB$,<br />
$HD$, are either equal to $E, F$ or<br />
equimultiples of them.

![Proposition 6 Diagram](0153.g1.svg)

For, first, let $GB$ be equal to $E$;<br />
I say that $HD$ is also equal to $F$.

For let $CK$ be made equal to $F$.

Since $AG$ is the same multiple of $E$ that $CH$ is of $F$,<br />
<p class="sub-clause">while $GB$ is equal to $E$ and $KC$ to $F$,</p>
therefore $AB$ is the same multiple of $E$ that $KH$ is of $F$.<br />
<span class="justification">[V. 2]</span>

But, by hypothesis, $AB$ is the same multiple of $E$ that<br />
$CD$ is of $F$;
<p class="assertion">therefore $KH$ is the same multiple of $F$ that $CD$ is of $F$.</p>

Since then each of the magnitudes $KH, CD$ is the same<br />
multiple of $F$,
<p class="assertion">therefore $KH$ is equal to $CD$.</p>
