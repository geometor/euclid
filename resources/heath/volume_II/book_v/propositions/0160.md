If <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> is to <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span> as <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span> is to <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span>,
or if <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span> is to <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> as <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span> is to <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span>, then <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span>.

Simson gives a more explicit proof of this proposition which has the advantage of referring back to the fundamental 5th and 7th definitions, instead of quoting the results of previous propositions, which, as will be seen from the next note, may be, in the circumstances, unsafe.

"Let <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span> have each of them the same ratio to <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span> ;
<span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span>.

For, if they are not equal, one of them is greater than the other ;
let <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> be the greater.

Then, by what was shown in the preceding proposition, there are some equimultiples of <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> and <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span>, and some multiple of <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span>, such that the multiple of <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> is greater than the multiple of <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span>, but the multiple of <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span> is not greater than that of <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span>.

Let such multiples be taken, and let $D$, $E$ be the equimultiples of <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span>, and $F$ the multiple of <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span>, so that $D$ may be greater than $F$, and $E$ not greater than $F$.

But, because <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> is to <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span> as <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span> is to <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span>,
and of <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span> are taken equimultiples $D$, $E$, and of <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span> is taken a multiple $F$,
and $D$ is greater than $F$,
<p class="assertion">$E$ must also be greater than $F$. <span class="justification">[V. Def. 5]</span></p>

But $E$ is not greater than $F$ : which is impossible.

Next, let <span data-ref="0160.g1:C">$C$</span> have the same ratio to each of the magnitudes <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> and <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span> ;
<span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0160.g1:B">$B$</span>.

For, if not, one of them is greater than the other ;
let <span data-ref="0160.g1:A">$A$</span> be the greater.
