The proof now goes on thus :

"Nor again is <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> less than <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> ;
for, in that case, <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> would have had to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> a less ratio than <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> has to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> ; <span class="justification">[V. 8]</span>
but it has not ;
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> is not less than <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span>."</p>

It is here that the difficulty arises. As before, we must use Def. 7. "<span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> would have had to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> a less ratio than <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> has to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span>," or the equivalent statement that <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> would have had to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> a greater ratio than <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> has to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span>, means that there would have been *some* equimultiples of <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> and *some* multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> such that
(1) the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> is greater than the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span>, but
(2) the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> is *not* greater than the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span>,
and it ought to have been proved that this can never happen if the hypothesis of the proposition is true, viz. that <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> has to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> a greater ratio than <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> has to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span>: that is, it should have been proved that, in the latter case, the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> is *always* greater than the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> whenever the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> is greater than the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> (for, when this is demonstrated, it will be evident that <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> cannot have a greater ratio to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> than <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> has to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span>). But this is not proved (cf. the remark of De Morgan quoted in the note on V. Def. 7, p. 130), and hence it is not proved that the above inference from the supposition that <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> is less than <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> is inconsistent with the hypothesis in the enunciation. The proof therefore fails.

Simson suggests that the proof is not Euclid's, but the work of some one who apparently "has been deceived in applying what is manifest, when understood of magnitudes, unto ratios, viz. that a magnitude cannot be both greater and less than another."

The proof substituted by Simson is satisfactory and simple.

"Let <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> have to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> a greater ratio than <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> has to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> ;
<span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> is greater than <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span>.

For, because <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> has a greater ratio to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> than <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> has to <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span>, there are some equimultiples of <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> and some multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> such that
the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> is greater than the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span>, but the multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> is *not* greater than it. <span class="justification">[V. Def. 7]</span>

Let them be taken, and let $D$, $E$ be equimultiples of <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span>, and $F$ a multiple of <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span>, such that
<p class="assertion">$D$ is greater than $F$,</p>
<p class="sub-clause">but $E$ is not greater than $F$.</p>
<p class="assertion">Therefore $D$ is greater than $E$.</p>

And, because $D$ and $E$ are equimultiples of <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> and <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span>, and $D$ is greater than $E$,
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> is greater than <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span>. <span class="justification">[Simson's 4th Ax.]</span></p>

Next, let <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> have a greater ratio to <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> than it has to <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> ;
<span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> is less than <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span>.

For there is some multiple $F$ of <span data-ref="0161.g1:C">$C$</span> and some equimultiples $E$ and $D$ of <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> and <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span> such that
<p class="assertion">$F$ is greater than $E$ but not greater than $D$. <span class="justification">[V. Def. 7]</span></p>
<p class="assertion">Therefore $E$ is less than $D$ ;</p>
and, because $E$ and $D$ are equimultiples of <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> and <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span>,
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0161.g1:B">$B$</span> is less than <span data-ref="0161.g1:A">$A$</span>."</p>
