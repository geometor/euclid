Subtract from $m(a + b), (m + n)b$ the common part $mb$, and from<br />
$m(c + d), (m + n)d$ the common part $md$; and we have,<br />
according as $ma > = < nb, \quad mc > = < nd$.

But $ma, mc$ are any equimultiples of $a, c$, and $nb, nd$ any equimultiples of<br />
$b, d$,<br />
therefore, by V. Def. 5,

$$a \text{ is to } b \text{ as } c \text{ is to } d.$$

Smith and Bryant's proof follows, *mutatis mutandis*, their alternative proof<br />
of the next proposition (see pp. 173—4 below).

### PROPOSITION 18.

<p class="enunciation">If magnitudes be proportional separando, they will also be<br />
proportional componendo.</p>

Let $AE, EB, CF, FD$ be magnitudes proportional<br />
*separando*, so that, as $AE$ is<br />
to $EB$, so is $CF$ to $FD$;<br />
I say that they will also be<br />
proportional *componendo*, that<br />
is, as $AB$ is to $BE$, so is<br />
$CD$ to $FD$.

![Proposition 18 diagram of line segments](0175.g1.svg)

For, if $CD$ be not to $DF$ as $AB$ to $BE$,<br />
then, as $AB$ is to $BE$, so will $CD$ be either to some<br />
magnitude less than $DF$ or to a greater.

First, let it be in that ratio to a less magnitude $DG$.

Then, since, as $AB$ is to $BE$, so is $CD$ to $DG$,<br />
they are magnitudes proportional *componendo*;
<p class="assertion">so that they will also be proportional *separando*. <span class="justification">[V. 17]</span></p>

<p class="assertion">Therefore, as $AE$ is to $EB$, so is $CG$ to $GD$.</p>

But also, by hypothesis,
<p class="assertion">as $AE$ is to $EB$, so is $CF$ to $FD$.</p>

<p class="assertion">Therefore also, as $CG$ is to $GD$, so is $CF$ to $FD$. <span class="justification">[V. 11]</span></p>

But the first $CG$ is greater than the third $CF$;
<p class="assertion">therefore the second $GD$ is also greater than the fourth<br />
$FD$. <span class="justification">[V. 14]</span></p>

<p class="sub-clause">But it is also less: which is impossible.</p>

<p class="assertion">Therefore, as $AB$ is to $BE$, so is not $CD$ to a less<br />
magnitude than $FD$.</p>
