Let there be three magnitudes <span data-ref="0182.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0182.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0182.g1:C">$C$</span>, and others<br />
<span data-ref="0182.g1:D">$D$</span>, <span data-ref="0182.g1:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0182.g1:F">$F$</span> equal to them in multitude, which taken two and<br />
two are in the same ratio, so that,
<p class="assertion">as <span data-ref="0182.g1:A">$A$</span> is to <span data-ref="0182.g1:B">$B$</span>, so is <span data-ref="0182.g1:D">$D$</span> to <span data-ref="0182.g1:E">$E$</span>,</p>
and
<p class="assertion">as <span data-ref="0182.g1:B">$B$</span> is to <span data-ref="0182.g1:C">$C$</span>, so is <span data-ref="0182.g1:E">$E$</span> to <span data-ref="0182.g1:F">$F$</span>;</p>
and let <span data-ref="0182.g1:A">$A$</span> be greater than <span data-ref="0182.g1:C">$C$</span> *ex aequali* ;<br />
I say that <span data-ref="0182.g1:D">$D$</span> will also be greater than <span data-ref="0182.g1:F">$F$</span>; if <span data-ref="0182.g1:A">$A$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0182.g1:C">$C$</span>,<br />
equal ; and, if less, less.

![Proposition 20](0182.g1.svg)

For, since <span data-ref="0182.g1:A">$A$</span> is greater than <span data-ref="0182.g1:C">$C$</span>,<br />
and <span data-ref="0182.g1:B">$B$</span> is some other magnitude,<br />
and the greater has to the same a greater ratio than the less<br />
has, <span class="justification">[V. 8]</span>
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0182.g1:A">$A$</span> has to <span data-ref="0182.g1:B">$B$</span> a greater ratio than <span data-ref="0182.g1:C">$C$</span> has to <span data-ref="0182.g1:B">$B$</span>.</p>

But, as <span data-ref="0182.g1:A">$A$</span> is to <span data-ref="0182.g1:B">$B$</span>, so is <span data-ref="0182.g1:D">$D$</span> to <span data-ref="0182.g1:E">$E$</span>,<br />
and, as <span data-ref="0182.g1:C">$C$</span> is to <span data-ref="0182.g1:B">$B$</span>, inversely, so is <span data-ref="0182.g1:F">$F$</span> to <span data-ref="0182.g1:E">$E$</span> ;
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0182.g1:D">$D$</span> has also to <span data-ref="0182.g1:E">$E$</span> a greater ratio than <span data-ref="0182.g1:F">$F$</span> has to <span data-ref="0182.g1:E">$E$</span>. <span class="justification">[V. 13]</span></p>

<p class="sub-clause">But, of magnitudes which have a ratio to the same, that<br />
which has a greater ratio is greater ; <span class="justification">[V. 10]</span></p>
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0182.g1:D">$D$</span> is greater than <span data-ref="0182.g1:F">$F$</span>.</p>

Similarly we can prove that, if <span data-ref="0182.g1:A">$A$</span> be equal to <span data-ref="0182.g1:C">$C$</span>, <span data-ref="0182.g1:D">$D$</span> will<br />
also be equal to <span data-ref="0182.g1:F">$F$</span>; and if less, less.

<p class="qed">Therefore etc.<span class="justification">Q. E. D.</span></p>

Though, as already remarked, Euclid has not yet given us any definition of *compounded ratios*, Props. 20—23 contain an important part of the theory of such ratios. The term "compounded ratio" is not used, but the propositions connect themselves with the definitions of *ex aequali* in its two forms, the ordinary form defined in Def. 17 and that called *perturbed proportion* in Def. 18. The compounded ratios dealt with in these propositions are those compounded of successive ratios in which the consequent of one is the antecedent of the next, or the antecedent of one is the consequent of the next.

Prop. 22 states the fundamental proposition about the ratio *ex aequali* in its ordinary form, to the effect that,
$$\begin{aligned}
\text{if} \quad & a \text{ is to } b \text{ as } d \text{ is to } e, \\
\text{and} \quad & b \text{ is to } c \text{ as } e \text{ is to } f, \\
\text{then} \quad & a \text{ is to } c \text{ as } d \text{ is to } f,
\end{aligned}$$
