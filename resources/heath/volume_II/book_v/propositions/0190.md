Algebraically, if
$$a : c = d : f\text{,}$$
and
$$b : c = e : f\text{,}$$
then
$$(a + b) : c = (d + e) : f.$$

This proposition is of the same character as those which precede the<br />
propositions relating to compounded ratios; but it could not be placed earlier<br />
than it is because v. 22 is used in the proof of it.

Inverting the second proportion to
$$c : b = f : e\text{,}$$
it follows, by v. 22, that
$$a : b = d : e\text{,}$$
whence, by v. 18,
$$(a + b) : b = (d + e) : e\text{,}$$
and from this and the second of the two given proportions we obtain, by a<br />
fresh application of v. 22,
$$(a + b) : c = (d + e) : f.$$

The first use of v. 22 is important as showing that the opposite process to<br />
compounding ratios, or what we should now call *division* of one ratio by<br />
another, does not require any new and separate propositions.

Aristotle tacitly uses v. 24 in combination with v. 11 and v. 16, *Meteorologica*<br />
III. 5, 376 a 22—26.

Simson adds two corollaries, one of which (Cor. 2) notes the extension to<br />
any number of magnitudes.

"The proposition holds true of two ranks of magnitudes whatever be their<br />
number, of which each of the first rank has to the second magnitude the same<br />
ratio that the corresponding one of the second rank has to a fourth magnitude;<br />
as is manifest."

Simson's Cor. 1 states the corresponding proposition to the above with<br />
*separando* taking the place of *componendo*, viz., that corresponding to the<br />
algebraical form
$$(a - b) : c = (d - e) : f.$$

"<span class="font-variant: small-caps;">Cor.</span> 1. If the same hypothesis be made as in the proposition, the<br />
excess of the first and fifth shall be to the second as the excess of the third<br />
and sixth to the fourth. The demonstration of this is the same with that of<br />
the proposition if division be used instead of composition." That is, we use<br />
v. 17 instead of v. 18, and conclude that
$$(a - b) : b = (d - e) : e.$$
