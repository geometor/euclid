But $a > b$; therefore $(a - c) > (b - d)$. <span class="justification">[V. 16 and 14]</span>

Add to each $(c + d)$;<br />
therefore
<p class="assertion">$(a + d) > (b + c)$.</p>

There is an important particular case of this proposition, which is,<br />
however, not mentioned here, viz. the case where $b = c$. The result shows, in<br />
this case, that *the arithmetic mean between two magnitudes is greater than<br />
their geometric mean*. The truth of this is proved for straight lines in VI. 27<br />
by "geometrical algebra," and the theorem forms the διορισμός for equations<br />
of the second degree.

---

Simson adds at the end of Book v. four propositions, F, G, H, K, which,<br />
however, do not seem to be of sufficient practical use to justify their inclusion<br />
here. But he adds at the end of his notes to the Book the following<br />
paragraph which deserves quotation word for word.

"The 5th book being thus corrected, I most readily agree to what the<br />
learned Dr Barrow says, 'that there is nothing in the whole body of the<br />
elements of a more subtile invention, nothing more solidly established, and<br />
more accurately handled than the doctrine of proportionals.' And there is<br />
some ground to hope that geometers will think that this could not have been<br />
said with as good reason, since Theon's time till the present."

Simson's claim herein will readily be admitted by all readers who are<br />
competent to form a judgment upon his criticisms and elucidations of Book v.
