Now, if the ratio of <span data-ref="0204.g1:/ B A /">$BA$</span> to <span data-ref="0204.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> is given, and if <span data-ref="0204.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> is given, the points <span data-ref="0204.g1:D">$D$</span>, <span data-ref="0204.g1:E">$E$</span> on <span data-ref="0204.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> and <span data-ref="0204.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> produced are given, and therefore so is the circle on <span data-ref="0204.g1:D">$D$</span>, <span data-ref="0204.g1:E">$E$</span> as diameter. Hence *the locus of a point such that its distances from two given points are in a given ratio (not being a ratio of equality) is a circle*.

This locus was discussed by Apollonius in his *Plane Loci*, Book II., as we know from Pappus (VII. p. 666), who says that the book contained the theorem that, if from two given points straight lines inflected to another point are in a given ratio, the point in which they meet will lie on either a straight line or a circumference of a circle. The straight line is of course the locus when the ratio is one of equality. The other case is quoted in the following form by Eutocius (Apollonius, ed. Heiberg, II. pp. 180—4).

*Given two points in a plane and a proportion between unequal straight lines, it is possible to describe a circle in the plane so that the straight lines inflected from the given points to the circumference of the circle shall have a ratio the same as the given one.*

Apollonius' construction, as given by Eutocius, is remarkable because he makes no use of either of the points <span data-ref="0205.g1:D">$D$</span>, <span data-ref="0205.g1:E">$E$</span>. He finds <span data-ref="0205.g1:O">$O$</span>, the centre of the required circle, and the length of its radius directly from the data <span data-ref="0205.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> and the given ratio which we will call $h : k$.
