Todhunter remarks that "the manner in which the two triangles are to be<br />
placed is very imperfectly described; their bases are to be in the same straight<br />
line and contiguous, their vertices are to be on the same side of the base, and<br />
each of the two angles which have a common vertex is to be equal to the<br />
remote angle of the other triangle." But surely Euclid's description is<br />
sufficient, except for not saying that $B$ and $D$ must be on the same side<br />
of $BCE$.

VI. 4 can be immediately deduced from VI. 2 if we superpose one triangle<br />
on the other three times in succession, so that each angle successively<br />
coincides with its equal, the triangles being similarly situated, e.g. if ($A$, $B$, $C$<br />
and $D$, $E$, $F$ being the equal angles respectively) we apply the angle $DEF$ to<br />
the angle $ABC$ so that $D$ lies on $AB$ (produced if necessary) and $F$ on $BC$<br />
(produced if necessary). De Morgan prefers this method. "Abandon," he<br />
says, "the peculiar mode of construction by which Euclid proves two cases at<br />
once; make an angle coincide with its equal, and suppose this process repeated<br />
three times, one for each angle."

### PROPOSITION 5.

<p class="enunciation">If two triangles have their sides proportional, the triangles<br />
will be equiangular and will have those angles equal which the<br />
corresponding sides subtend.</p>

Let $ABC$, $DEF$ be two triangles having their sides<br />
proportional, so that,
<p class="assertion">as $AB$ is to $BC$, so is $DE$ to $EF$,</p>
<p class="assertion">as $BC$ is to $CA$, so is $EF$ to $FD$,</p>
<p class="assertion">and further, as $BA$ is to $AC$, so is $ED$ to $DF$;</p>
I say that the triangle $ABC$ is equiangular with the triangle<br />
$DEF$, and they will have those angles equal which the corre-<br />
sponding sides subtend, namely the angle $ABC$ to the angle<br />
$DEF$, the angle $BCA$ to the angle $EFD$, and further the<br />
angle $BAC$ to the angle $EDF$.
