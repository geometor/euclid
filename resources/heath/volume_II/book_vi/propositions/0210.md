This proposition is the complete converse, VI. 6 a partial converse, of VI. 4.

Todhunter, after Walker, remarks that the enunciation should make it clear that the sides of the triangles *taken in order* are proportional. It is quite possible that there should be two triangles <span data-ref="0209.g1:&lt; A B C &gt;">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0209.g2:&lt; D E F &gt;">$DEF$</span> such that
<p align="center"><span data-ref="0209.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is to <span data-ref="0209.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> as <span data-ref="0209.g2:/ D E /">$DE$</span> to <span data-ref="0209.g2:/ E F /">$EF$</span>,</p>
and
<p align="center"><span data-ref="0209.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> is to <span data-ref="0209.g1:/ C A /">$CA$</span> as <span data-ref="0209.g2:/ D F /">$DF$</span> is to <span data-ref="0209.g2:/ E D /">$ED$</span> (instead of <span data-ref="0209.g2:/ E F /">$EF$</span> to <span data-ref="0209.g2:/ F D /">$FD$</span>),</p>
so that
<p align="center"><span data-ref="0209.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is to <span data-ref="0209.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> as <span data-ref="0209.g2:/ D F /">$DF$</span> to <span data-ref="0209.g2:/ E F /">$EF$</span><br>(*ex aequali* in perturbed proportion);</p>
in this case the sides of the triangles are proportional, but not in the same order, and the triangles are not necessarily equiangular to one another. For a numerical illustration we may suppose the sides of one triangle to be 3, 4 and 5 feet respectively, and those of another to be 12, 15 and 20 feet respectively.

In VI. 5 there is the same *apparent* avoidance of indirect demonstration which has been noticed on I. 48.

### PROPOSITION 6.

*If two triangles have one angle equal to one angle and the sides about the equal angles proportional, the triangles will be equiangular and will have those angles equal which the corresponding sides subtend.*

Let <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; A B C &gt;">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; D E F &gt;">$DEF$</span> be two triangles having one angle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; B A C )">$BAC$</span> equal to one angle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; E D F )">$EDF$</span> and the sides about the equal angles proportional, so that,
<p align="center">as <span data-ref="0211.g1:/ B A /">$BA$</span> is to <span data-ref="0211.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, so is <span data-ref="0211.g1:/ E D /">$ED$</span> to <span data-ref="0211.g1:/ D F /">$DF$</span>;</p>
I say that the triangle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; A B C &gt;">$ABC$</span> is equiangular with the triangle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; D E F &gt;">$DEF$</span>, and will have the angle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; A B C )">$ABC$</span> equal to the angle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; D E F )">$DEF$</span>, and the angle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; A C B )">$ACB$</span> to the angle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; D F E )">$DFE$</span>.

For on the straight line <span data-ref="0211.g1:/ D F /">$DF$</span>, and at the points <span data-ref="0211.g1:D">$D$</span>, <span data-ref="0211.g1:F">$F$</span> on it, let there be constructed the angle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; F D G )">$FDG$</span> equal to either of the angles <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; B A C )">$BAC$</span>, <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; E D F )">$EDF$</span>, and the angle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; D F G )">$DFG$</span> equal to the angle <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; A C B )">$ACB$</span>; [I. 23]
therefore the remaining angle at <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; B )">$B$</span> is equal to the remaining angle at <span data-ref="0211.g1:&lt; G )">$G$</span>. [I. 32]
