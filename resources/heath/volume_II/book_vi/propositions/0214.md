<p class="assertion">Therefore the triangle <span data-ref="0213.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> is equiangular with the triangle<br />
<span data-ref="0213.g1:< D E F >">$DEF$</span>.</p>

<p class="qed">Therefore etc.<span class="justification">Q. E. D.</span></p>

---

Todhunter points out, after Walker, that some more words are necessary<br />
to make the enunciation precise: "If two triangles have one angle equal to one<br />
angle, the sides about other angles proportional <*so that the sides subtending*<br />
*the equal angles are homologous*>...."

This proposition is the extension to similar triangles of the *ambiguous case*<br />
already mentioned as omitted by Euclid in relation to *equality* of triangles in<br />
all respects (cf. note following I. 26, Vol. I. p. 306). The enunciation of VI. 7<br />
has suggested the ordinary method of enunciating the *ambiguous case* where<br />
*equality* and not similarity is in question. Cf. Todhunter's note on I. 26.

Another possible way of presenting this proposition is given by Todhunter.<br />
The essential theorem to prove is:

*If two triangles have two sides of the one proportional to two sides of the*<br />
*other, and the angles opposite to one pair of corresponding sides equal, the angles*<br />
*which are opposite to the other pair of corresponding sides shall either be equal or*<br />
*be together equal to two right angles.*

For the angles included by the proportional sides must be either equal or<br />
unequal.

If they are equal, then, since the triangles have two angles of the one<br />
equal to two angles of the other, respectively, they are equiangular to one<br />
another.

We have therefore only to consider the case in which the angles included<br />
by the proportional sides are unequal.

The proof is, except at the end, like that of VI. 7.

Let the triangles <span data-ref="0214.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:< D E F >">$DEF$</span> have the angle at <span data-ref="0214.g1:< B A C )">$A$</span> equal to the angle at <span data-ref="0214.g1:< E D F )">$D$</span>;
<p class="assertion">let <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> be to <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> as <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> to <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span>,</p>
but let the angle <span data-ref="0214.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span> be *not* equal to the angle <span data-ref="0214.g1:< D E F )">$DEF$</span>.

![Proposition 7 Todhunter alternative diagram](0214.g1.svg)

The angles <span data-ref="0214.g1:< A C B )">$ACB$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:< D F E )">$DFE$</span> shall be together equal to two right angles.

For one of the angles <span data-ref="0214.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:< D E F )">$DEF$</span> must be the greater.

Let <span data-ref="0214.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span> be the greater; and make the angle <span data-ref="0214.g1:< A B G )">$ABG$</span> equal to the angle<br />
<span data-ref="0214.g1:< D E F )">$DEF$</span>.

Then we prove, as in VI. 7, that the triangles <span data-ref="0214.g1:< A B G >">$ABG$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:< D E F >">$DEF$</span> are equiangular,<br />
whence
<p class="assertion"><span data-ref="0214.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is to <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ B G /">$BG$</span> as <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> to <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span>.</p>

But <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is to <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> as <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> to <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span>, by hypothesis.
<p class="assertion">Therefore <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ B G /">$BG$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0214.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>,<br />
and the angle <span data-ref="0214.g1:< B G C )">$BGC$</span> is equal to the angle <span data-ref="0214.g1:< B C A )">$BCA$</span>.</p>
