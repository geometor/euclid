title: "Proposition 7 (concl.)"
---

<p class="assertion">Therefore the triangle $ABC$ is equiangular with the triangle<br />
$DEF$.</p>

<p class="qed">Therefore etc.<span class="justification">Q. E. D.</span></p>

---

Todhunter points out, after Walker, that some more words are necessary<br />
to make the enunciation precise: "If two triangles have one angle equal to one<br />
angle, the sides about other angles proportional <*so that the sides subtending*<br />
*the equal angles are homologous*>...."

This proposition is the extension to similar triangles of the *ambiguous case*<br />
already mentioned as omitted by Euclid in relation to *equality* of triangles in<br />
all respects (cf. note following I. 26, Vol. I. p. 306). The enunciation of VI. 7<br />
has suggested the ordinary method of enunciating the *ambiguous case* where<br />
*equality* and not similarity is in question. Cf. Todhunter's note on I. 26.

Another possible way of presenting this proposition is given by Todhunter.<br />
The essential theorem to prove is:

*If two triangles have two sides of the one proportional to two sides of the*<br />
*other, and the angles opposite to one pair of corresponding sides equal, the angles*<br />
*which are opposite to the other pair of corresponding sides shall either be equal or*<br />
*be together equal to two right angles.*

For the angles included by the proportional sides must be either equal or<br />
unequal.

If they are equal, then, since the triangles have two angles of the one<br />
equal to two angles of the other, respectively, they are equiangular to one<br />
another.

We have therefore only to consider the case in which the angles included<br />
by the proportional sides are unequal.

The proof is, except at the end, like that of VI. 7.

Let the triangles $ABC, DEF$ have the angle at $A$ equal to the angle at $D$;
<p class="assertion">let $AB$ be to $BC$ as $DE$ to $EF$,</p>
but let the angle $ABC$ be *not* equal to the angle $DEF$.

![Proposition 7 Todhunter alternative diagram](0214.g1.svg)

The angles $ACB, DFE$ shall be together equal to two right angles.

For one of the angles $ABC, DEF$ must be the greater.

Let $ABC$ be the greater; and make the angle $ABG$ equal to the angle<br />
$DEF$.

Then we prove, as in VI. 7, that the triangles $ABG, DEF$ are equiangular,<br />
whence
<p class="assertion">$AB$ is to $BG$ as $DE$ to $EF$.</p>

But $AB$ is to $BC$ as $DE$ to $EF$, by hypothesis.
<p class="assertion">Therefore $BG$ is equal to $BC$,<br />
and the angle $BGC$ is equal to the angle $BCA$.</p>
