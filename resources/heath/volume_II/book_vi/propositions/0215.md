Now, since the triangles <span data-ref="0214.g1:< A B G >">$ABG$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:< D E F >">$DEF$</span> are equiangular,<br />
the angle <span data-ref="0214.g1:< B G A )">$BGA$</span> is equal to the angle <span data-ref="0214.g1:< E F D )">$EFD$</span>.

<p class="assertion">Add to them respectively the equal angles <span data-ref="0214.g1:< B G C )">$BGC$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:< B C A )">$BCA$</span>; therefore the<br />
angles <span data-ref="0214.g1:< B C A )">$BCA$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:< E F D )">$EFD$</span> are together equal to the angles <span data-ref="0214.g1:< B G A )">$BGA$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:< B G C )">$BGC$</span>, i.e. to two<br />
right angles.</p>

<p class="assertion">It follows therefore that the angles <span data-ref="0214.g1:< B C A )">$BCA$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:< E F D )">$EFD$</span> must be either equal or<br />
supplementary.</p>

But (1), if each of them is less than a right angle, they cannot be<br />
supplementary, and they must therefore be equal;

(2) if each of them is greater than a right angle, they cannot be<br />
supplementary and must therefore be equal;

(3) if one of them is a right angle, they are supplementary and also<br />
equal.

Simson distinguishes the last case (3) in his enunciation: "then, if<br />
each of the remaining angles be either less or not less than a right angle,<br />
*or if one of them be a right angle*...."

The change is right, on the principle of restricting the conditions to the<br />
minimum necessary to enable the conclusion to be inferred. Simson adds a<br />
separate proof of the case in which one of the remaining angles is a right<br />
angle.

"Lastly, let one of the angles at <span data-ref="0215.g1:< B C A )">$C$</span>, <span data-ref="0215.g1:< D F E )">$F$</span>, viz. the angle at <span data-ref="0215.g1:< B C A )">$C$</span>, be a right angle;<br />
in this case likewise the triangle <span data-ref="0215.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> is<br />
equiangular to the triangle <span data-ref="0215.g1:< D E F >">$DEF$</span>.

For, if they be not equiangular,<br />
make, at the point <span data-ref="0215.g1:B">$B$</span> of the straight<br />
line <span data-ref="0215.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, the angle <span data-ref="0215.g1:< A B G )">$ABG$</span> equal to the<br />
angle <span data-ref="0215.g1:< D E F )">$DEF$</span>; then it may be proved,<br />
as in the first case, that <span data-ref="0215.g1:/ B G /">$BG$</span> is equal<br />
to <span data-ref="0215.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>.

But the angle <span data-ref="0215.g1:< B C G )">$BCG$</span> is a right<br />
angle;
<p class="assertion">therefore the angle <span data-ref="0215.g1:< B G C )">$BGC$</span> is also a<br />
right angle;</p>
<p class="assertion">whence two of the angles of the tri-<br />
angle <span data-ref="0215.g1:< B G C >">$BGC$</span> are together not less than<br />
two right angles: which is impossible.</p>

<p class="assertion">Therefore the triangle <span data-ref="0215.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> is equiangular to the triangle <span data-ref="0215.g1:< D E F >">$DEF$</span>."</p>

### PROPOSITION 8.

<p class="enunciation">If in a right-angled triangle a perpendicular be drawn<br />
from the right angle to the base, the triangles adjoining the<br />
perpendicular are similar both to the whole and to one another.</p>

Let <span data-ref="0216.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> be a right-angled triangle having the angle<br />
<span data-ref="0216.g1:< B A C )">$BAC$</span> right, and let <span data-ref="0216.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span> be drawn from <span data-ref="0216.g1:A">$A$</span> perpendicular<br />
to <span data-ref="0216.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>;

I say that each of the triangles <span data-ref="0216.g1:< A B D >">$ABD$</span>, <span data-ref="0216.g1:< A D C >">$ADC$</span> is similar to<br />
the whole <span data-ref="0216.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> and, further, they are similar to one another.
