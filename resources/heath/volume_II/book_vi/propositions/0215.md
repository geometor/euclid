Now, since the triangles $ABG, DEF$ are equiangular,<br />
the angle $BGA$ is equal to the angle $EFD$.

<p class="assertion">Add to them respectively the equal angles $BGC, BCA$; therefore the<br />
angles $BCA, EFD$ are together equal to the angles $BGA, BGC$, i.e. to two<br />
right angles.</p>

<p class="assertion">It follows therefore that the angles $BCA, EFD$ must be either equal or<br />
supplementary.</p>

But (1), if each of them is less than a right angle, they cannot be<br />
supplementary, and they must therefore be equal;

(2) if each of them is greater than a right angle, they cannot be<br />
supplementary and must therefore be equal;

(3) if one of them is a right angle, they are supplementary and also<br />
equal.

Simson distinguishes the last case (3) in his enunciation: "then, if<br />
each of the remaining angles be either less or not less than a right angle,<br />
*or if one of them be a right angle*...."

The change is right, on the principle of restricting the conditions to the<br />
minimum necessary to enable the conclusion to be inferred. Simson adds a<br />
separate proof of the case in which one of the remaining angles is a right<br />
angle.

"Lastly, let one of the angles at $C, F$, viz. the angle at $C$, be a right angle;<br />
in this case likewise the triangle $ABC$ is<br />
equiangular to the triangle $DEF$.

![Proposition 7, Case 3](0215.g1.svg)

For, if they be not equiangular,<br />
make, at the point $B$ of the straight<br />
line $AB$, the angle $ABG$ equal to the<br />
angle $DEF$; then it may be proved,<br />
as in the first case, that $BG$ is equal<br />
to $BC$.

But the angle $BCG$ is a right<br />
angle;
<p class="assertion">therefore the angle $BGC$ is also a<br />
right angle;</p>
<p class="assertion">whence two of the angles of the tri-<br />
angle $BGC$ are together not less than<br />
two right angles: which is impossible.</p>

<p class="assertion">Therefore the triangle $ABC$ is equiangular to the triangle $DEF$."</p>

### PROPOSITION 8.

<p class="enunciation">If in a right-angled triangle a perpendicular be drawn<br />
from the right angle to the base, the triangles adjoining the<br />
perpendicular are similar both to the whole and to one another.</p>

Let $ABC$ be a right-angled triangle having the angle<br />
$BAC$ right, and let $AD$ be drawn from $A$ perpendicular<br />
to $BC$;

I say that each of the triangles $ABD, ADC$ is similar to<br />
the whole $ABC$ and, further, they are similar to one another.
