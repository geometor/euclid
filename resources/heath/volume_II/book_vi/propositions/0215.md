Now, since the triangles <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; A B G &gt;">$ABG$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; D E F &gt;">$DEF$</span> are equiangular,
the angle <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; B G A )">$BGA$</span> is equal to the angle <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; E F D )">$EFD$</span>.
Add to them respectively the equal angles <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; B G C )">$BGC$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; B C A )">$BCA$</span>; therefore the angles <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; B C A )">$BCA$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; E F D )">$EFD$</span> are together equal to the angles <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; B G A )">$BGA$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; B G C )">$BGC$</span>, i.e. to two right angles.

It follows therefore that the angles <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; B C A )">$BCA$</span>, <span data-ref="0214.g1:&lt; E F D )">$EFD$</span> must be either equal or supplementary.

But (1), if each of them is less than a right angle, they cannot be supplementary, and they must therefore be equal;

(2) if each of them is greater than a right angle, they cannot be supplementary and must therefore be equal;

(3) if one of them is a right angle, they are supplementary and also equal.

Simson distinguishes the last case (3) in his enunciation: "then, if each of the remaining angles be either less or not less than a right angle, *or if one of them be a right angle*...."

The change is right, on the principle of restricting the conditions to the minimum necessary to enable the conclusion to be inferred. Simson adds a separate proof of the case in which one of the remaining angles is a right angle.

"Lastly, let one of the angles at <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; B C A )">$C$</span>, <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; D F E )">$F$</span>, viz. the angle at <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; B C A )">$C$</span>, be a right angle; in this case likewise the triangle <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; A B C &gt;">$ABC$</span> is equiangular to the triangle <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; D E F &gt;">$DEF$</span>.

For, if they be not equiangular, make, at the point <span data-ref="0215.g1:B">$B$</span> of the straight line <span data-ref="0215.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, the angle <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; A B G )">$ABG$</span> equal to the angle <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; D E F )">$DEF$</span>; then it may be proved, as in the first case, that <span data-ref="0215.g1:/ B G /">$BG$</span> is equal to <span data-ref="0215.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>.

But the angle <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; B C G )">$BCG$</span> is a right angle; therefore the angle <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; B G C )">$BGC$</span> is also a right angle; whence two of the angles of the triangle <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; B G C &gt;">$BGC$</span> are together not less than two right angles: which is impossible.

Therefore the triangle <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; A B C &gt;">$ABC$</span> is equiangular to the triangle <span data-ref="0215.g1:&lt; D E F &gt;">$DEF$</span>."

### PROPOSITION 8.

*If in a right-angled triangle a perpendicular be drawn from the right angle to the base, the triangles adjoining the perpendicular are similar both to the whole and to one another.*

Let <span data-ref="0216.g1:&lt; A B C &gt;">$ABC$</span> be a right-angled triangle having the angle <span data-ref="0216.g1:&lt; B A C )">$BAC$</span> right, and let <span data-ref="0216.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span> be drawn from <span data-ref="0216.g1:A">$A$</span> perpendicular to <span data-ref="0216.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>;

I say that each of the triangles <span data-ref="0216.g1:&lt; A B D &gt;">$ABD$</span>, <span data-ref="0216.g1:&lt; A D C &gt;">$ADC$</span> is similar to the whole <span data-ref="0216.g1:&lt; A B C &gt;">$ABC$</span> and, further, they are similar to one another.
