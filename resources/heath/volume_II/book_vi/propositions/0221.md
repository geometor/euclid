### PROPOSITION 12.

<p class="enunciation">To three given straight lines to find a fourth proportional.</p>

Let *A*, *B*, *C* be the three given straight lines;<br />
thus it is required to find a fourth proportional to *A*, *B*, *C*.

![Proposition 12](0221.g1.svg)

Let two straight lines *DE*, *DF* be set out containing any<br />
angle *EDF*;<br />
let *DG* be made equal to *A*, *GE* equal to *B*, and further *DH*<br />
equal to *C*;<br />
let *GH* be joined, and let *EF* be drawn through *E* parallel<br />
to it. <span class="justification">[I. 31]</span>

<p class="sub-clause">Since, then, *GH* has been drawn parallel to *EF*, one of<br />
the sides of the triangle *DEF*,</p>

<p class="assertion">therefore, as *DG* is to *GE*, so is *DH* to *HF*. <span class="justification">[VI. 2]</span></p>

<p class="sub-clause">But *DG* is equal to *A*, *GE* to *B*, and *DH* to *C*;</p>

<p class="assertion">therefore, as *A* is to *B*, so is *C* to *HF*.</p>

<p class="qed">Therefore to the three given straight lines *A*, *B*, *C* a fourth<br />
proportional *HF* has been found.<span class="justification">Q. E. F.</span></p>

---

We have here the geometrical equivalent of the "rule of three."

It is of course immaterial whether, as in Euclid's proof, the first and<br />
second straight lines are measured on one of the lines forming the angle and<br />
the third on the other, or the first and third are measured on one and the<br />
second on the other.

If it should be desired that the first and the required fourth be measured<br />
on one of the lines, and the second and third on<br />
the other, we can use the following construction.<br />
Measure *DE* on one straight line equal to *A*, and<br />
on any other straight line making an angle with<br />
the first at the point *D* measure *DF* equal to *B*,<br />
and *DG* equal to *C*. Join *EF*, and through *G*<br />
draw *GH* anti-parallel to *EF*, i.e. make the angle<br />
*DGH* equal to the angle *DEF*; let *GH* meet<br />
*DE* (produced if necessary) in *H*.
