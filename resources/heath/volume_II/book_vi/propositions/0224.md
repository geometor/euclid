It is true that VI. 14 is a particular case of VI. 23, but, if either is out of<br />
place, it is rather the latter that should be placed before VI. 14, since most of<br />
the propositions between VI. 15 and VI. 23 depend upon VI. 14 and 15. But<br />
is perfectly consistent with Euclid's manner to give a particular case first<br />
and its extension later, and such an arrangement often has great advantages<br />
in that it enables the more difficult parts of a subject to be led up to more<br />
easily and gradually. Now, if De Morgan's view were here followed, we<br />
should, as it seems to me, be committing the mistake of explaining what is<br />
relatively easy to understand, viz. two ratios of which one is the inverse of<br />
the other, by a more complicated conception, that of compound ratio. In<br />
other words, it is easier for a learner to realise the relation indicated by the<br />
statement that the sides of equal and equiangular parallelograms are “recipro-<br />
cally proportional” than to form a conception of parallelograms such that<br />
“the ratio compounded of the ratio of their sides is one of equality.” For<br />
this reason I would adhere to Euclid's arrangement.

The conclusion that, since *DB*, *BE* are placed in a straight line, *FB*, *BG*<br />
are also in a straight line is referred to I. 14. The deduction is made clearer<br />
by the following steps.

<p class="sub-clause">The angle *DBF* is equal to the angle *GBE*;<br />
add to each the angle *FBE*;</p>

<p class="assertion">therefore the angles *DBF*, *FBE* are together equal to the angles *GBE*, *FBE*.<br />
<span class="justification">[C. N. 2]</span></p>

<p class="sub-clause">But the angles *DBF*, *FBE* are together equal to two right angles, <span class="justification">[I. 13]</span></p>

<p class="assertion">therefore the angles *GBE*, *FBE* are together equal to two right angles,<br />
<span class="justification">[C. N. 1]</span></p>

<p class="assertion">and hence *FB*, *BG* are in one straight line. <span class="justification">[I. 14]</span></p>

The result is also obvious from the converse of I. 15 given by Proclus<br />
(see note on I. 15).

The proposition VI. 14 contains a theorem and one partial converse of it;<br />
so also does VI. 15. To each proposition may be added the other partial<br />
converse, which may be enunciated as follows, the words in square brackets<br />
applying to the case of triangles (VI. 15).
