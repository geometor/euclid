$$\begin{align*}
\text{Now suppose that}\quad\quad\quad\quad A : B &= B : C\text{,} \\
D : E &= E : F\text{,} \\
\text{and}\quad\quad\quad\quad A : C &= D : F\text{.} \\
\text{To prove that}\quad\quad\quad\quad A : B &= D : E\text{.}
\end{align*}$$
If this is not so, suppose that
$$A : B = D : Z\text{.}$$
$$\begin{align*}
\text{Since}\quad\quad\quad\quad A : C &= D : F\text{,} \\
\text{therefore, inversely,}\quad\quad C : A &= F : D\text{.} \\
\text{Therefore, \textit{ex aequali},}& \\
C : B &= F : Z\text{,} &\text{[V. 22]} \\
\text{or, inversely,}\quad\quad\quad\quad B : C &= Z : F\text{.} \\
\text{Therefore}\quad\quad\quad\quad A : B &= Z : F\text{.} &\text{[V. 11]} \\
\text{But}\quad\quad\quad\quad A : B &= D : Z\text{, by hypothesis.} \\
\text{Therefore}\quad\quad\quad\quad D : Z &= Z : F\text{.} &\text{[V. 11]} \\
\text{Also, by hypothesis,}\quad\quad D : E &= E : F\text{;} \\
\text{whence, by the lemma,}\quad\quad E &= Z\text{.} \\
\text{Therefore}\quad\quad\quad\quad A : B &= D : E\text{.}
\end{align*}$$

De Morgan remarks that the best way of remedying the defect in Euclid<br />
is to insert the proposition (the lemma to the last proof) that *it is impossible<br />
that two different ratios can have the same duplicate ratio*, "which," he says,<br />
"immediately proves the second (or defective) case of the theorem." But this<br />
seems to be either too much or too little: too much, if we choose to make<br />
the *minimum* addition to Euclid (for that addition is a lemma which shall prove<br />
that, if a duplicate ratio is a ratio of equality, the ratio of which it is duplicate<br />
is also one of equality), and too little if the proof is to be altered in the more<br />
fundamental manner explained above.

I think that, if Euclid's attention had been drawn to the defect in his<br />
proof of VI. 22 and he had been asked to remedy it, he would have done so<br />
by supplying what I have called the minimum lemma and not by making the<br />
more fundamental alteration. This I infer from Prop. 24 of the *Data*, where<br />
he gives a theorem corresponding to the proposition that *ratios of which equal<br />
ratios are duplicate are equal*. The proposition in the *Data* is enunciated<br />
thus: *If three straight lines be proportional, and the first have to the third a<br />
given ratio, it will also have to the second a given ratio*.

*A*, *B*, *C* being the three straight lines, so that
$$A : B = B : C\text{,}$$
and *A* : *C* being a given ratio, it is required to prove that *A* : *B* is also a<br />
given ratio.

Euclid takes any straight line *D*, and first finds another, *F*, such that
$$D : F = A : C\text{,}$$
whence *D* : *F* must be a given ratio, and, as *D* is given, *F* is therefore given.

Then he takes *E* a mean proportional between *D*, *F*, so that
$$D : E = E : F\text{.}$$
