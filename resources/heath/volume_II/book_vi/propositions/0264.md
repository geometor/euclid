We have already (note on I. 44) seen the significance, in Greek geometry, of the theory of "the application of areas, their exceeding and their falling short." In I. 44 it was a question of "applying to a given straight line (exactly, without 'excess' or 'defect') a parallelogram equal to a given rectilinear figure, in a given angle." Here, in VI. 27—29, it is a question of parallelograms applied to a straight line but "*deficient (or exceeding) by parallelograms similar and similarly situated to a given parallelogram.*"

Apart from size, it is easy to construct any number of parallelograms "deficient" or "exceeding" in the manner described. Given the straight line <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> to which the parallelogram has to be applied, we describe on the base <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ C B /">$CB$</span>, where <span data-ref="0264.g1:C">$C$</span> is on <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, or on <span data-ref="0264.g1:[ B A ]">$BA$</span> produced beyond <span data-ref="0264.g1:A">$A$</span>, any parallelogram "similarly situated" and either equal or similar to the given parallelogram (Euclid takes the similar and similarly situated parallelogram on half the line), draw the diagonal <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>, take on it (produced if necessary) any points as <span data-ref="0264.g1:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0264.g1:K">$K$</span>, draw <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span>, or <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ K L /">$KL$</span>, parallel to <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> to meet <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> or <span data-ref="0264.g1:[ A B ]">$AB$</span> produced and complete the parallelograms, as <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; A H &gt;">$AH$</span>, <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; M L &gt;">$ML$</span>.

If the point <span data-ref="0264.g1:E">$E$</span> is taken on <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span> or <span data-ref="0264.g1:[ B D ]">$BD$</span> produced beyond <span data-ref="0264.g1:D">$D$</span>, it must be so taken that <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> meets <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> between <span data-ref="0264.g1:A">$A$</span> and <span data-ref="0264.g1:B">$B$</span>. Otherwise the parallelogram <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; A E &gt;">$AE$</span> would not be applied to <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> itself, as it is required to be.

The parallelograms <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; B D &gt;">$BD$</span>, <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; B E &gt;">$BE$</span>, being about the same diameter, are similar [VI. 24], and <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; B E &gt;">$BE$</span> is the defect of the parallelogram <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; A E &gt;">$AE$</span> relatively to <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>. <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; A E &gt;">$AE$</span> is then a parallelogram applied to <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> but deficient by a parallelogram similar and similarly situated to <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; B D &gt;">$BD$</span>.

If <span data-ref="0264.g1:K">$K$</span> is on <span data-ref="0264.g1:[ D B ]">$DB$</span> produced, the parallelogram <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; B K &gt;">$BK$</span> is similar to <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; B D &gt;">$BD$</span>, but it is the *excess* of the parallelogram <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; A K &gt;">$AK$</span> relatively to the base <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>. <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; A K &gt;">$AK$</span> is a parallelogram applied to <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> but exceeding by a parallelogram similar and similarly situated to <span data-ref="0264.g1:&lt; B D &gt;">$BD$</span>.

Thus it is seen that <span data-ref="0264.g1:[ B D ]">$BD$</span> produced both ways is the *locus* of points, such as <span data-ref="0264.g1:E">$E$</span> or <span data-ref="0264.g1:K">$K$</span>, which determine, with the direction of <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>, the position of <span data-ref="0264.g1:A">$A$</span>, and the direction of <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, parallelograms applied to <span data-ref="0264.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> and *deficient* or *exceeding* by parallelograms similar and similarly situated to the given parallelogram.

The importance of VI. 27—29 from a historical point of view cannot be overrated. They give the geometrical equivalent of the algebraical solution of the most general form of quadratic equation when that equation has a real and positive root. It will also enable us to find a real *negative* root of a quadratic equation; for such an equation can, by altering the sign of $x$, be turned into another with a real *positive* root, when the geometrical method again becomes applicable. It will also, as we shall see, enable us to represent *both* roots when both are real and positive, and therefore to represent both roots when both are real but either positive or negative.

The method of these propositions was constantly used by the Greek geometers in the solution of problems, and they constitute the foundation of Book X. of the *Elements* and of Apollonius' treatment of the conic sections. Simson's observation on the subject is entirely justified. He says namely on VI. 28, 29: "These two problems, to the first of which the 27th Prop. is necessary, are the most general and useful of all in the Elements, and are most frequently made use of by the ancient geometers in the solution of other problems; and therefore are very ignorantly left out by Tacquet and"
