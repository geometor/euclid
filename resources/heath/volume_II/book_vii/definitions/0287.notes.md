## Textual, Lexical & Mathematical Observations
- **Greek Enunciations**:
  - Def. 5: `Πολλαπλάσιος δὲ ὁ μείζων τοῦ ἐλάσσονος, ὅταν καταμετρῆται ὑπὸ τοῦ ἐλάσσονος.`
  - Def. 6: `῎Αρτιος ἀριθμός ἐστιν ὁ δίχα διαιρούμενος.`
  - Def. 7: `Περισσὸς δὲ ὁ μὴ διαιρούμενος δίχα ἢ [ὁ] μονάδι διαφέρων ἀρτίου ἀριθμοῦ.`
  - Def. 8: `᾿Αρτιάκις ἄρτιος ἀριθμός ἐστιν ὁ ὑπὸ ἀρτίου ἀριθμοῦ μετρούμενος κατὰ ἄρτιον ἀριθμόν.`
- **Philosophical and Mathematical Analysis**:
  - **Def. 6 & 7**: Heath highlights the Pythagorean definition preserved by Nicomachus: an even number is divided into the greatest parts in size (πηλικότητι) but least in quantity (ποσότητι), namely two halves.
  - Aristotle's criticism (*Topics* VI. 4) of defining odd by reference to even (coordinated coordinate differentiae) explains why the second half of Def. 7 ("or that which differs by an unit from an even number") is considered pre-Euclidean popular terminology.
  - **Def. 8**: Euclid defines "even-times even" as any number measured by an even number an even number of times (i.e. $2k \times 2m = 4km$), differing from the later neo-Pythagorean restriction to pure powers of 2 ($2^n$).
