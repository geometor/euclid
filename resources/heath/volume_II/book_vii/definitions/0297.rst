4. If the third dimension is less than the others, the number is *equal by equal by less* (:term:`ἰσάκις <isakis>` ἴσος ἐλαττονάκις) and is called a *plinth* (:term:`πλινθίς <plinthis>`), e.g. $8 \cdot 8 \cdot 3$.

5. If the third dimension is greater than the others, the number is *equal by equal by greater* (:term:`ἰσάκις <isakis>` ἴσος μειζονάκις) and is called a *beam* (:term:`δοκίς <dokis>`), e.g. $3 \cdot 3 \cdot 7$. Another name for this latter kind of number (according to :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>`) was στηλίς (diminutive of στήλη).

Lastly, in connexion with pyramidal numbers, :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` (:cite:`II. 14, 5 <nicomachus:intro-arith:ii-14-5>`) distinguishes numbers corresponding to *frusta* of pyramids. These are *truncated* (κόλουροι), *twice-truncated* (δικόλουροι), *thrice-truncated* (τρικόλουροι) pyramids, and so on, the term being used mostly in theoretic treatises (ἐν συγγράμμασι μάλιστα τοῖς θεωρηματικοῖς). The *truncated* pyramid was formed by cutting off the point forming the vertex. The *twice-truncated* was that which lacked the vertex and the next plane, and so on. :person:`Theon of Smyrna <theon-of-smyrna>` (p. 42, 4) only mentions the *truncated* pyramid as "that with its vertex cut off" (ἡ τὴν κορυφὴν ἀποτετμημένη), saying that some also called it a trapezium, after the similitude of a plane trapezium formed by cutting the top off a triangle by a straight line parallel to the base.

DEFINITION 18.
--------------

Τετράγωνος :term:`ἀριθμός <number>` ἐστιν ὁ :term:`ἰσάκις <isakis>` ἴσος ἢ [ὁ] ὑπὸ δύο ἴσων :term:`ἀριθμῶν <number>` περιεχόμενος.

A particular kind of square distinguished by :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` and the rest was the square number which ended (in the decimal notation) with the same number as its side, e.g. 1, 25, 36, which are the squares of 1, 5 and 6. These square numbers were called *cyclic* (κυκλικοί) on the analogy of circles in geometry which return again to the point from which they started.

DEFINITION 19.
--------------

Κύβος δὲ ὁ :term:`ἰσάκις <isakis>` ἴσος :term:`ἰσάκις <isakis>` ἢ [ὁ] ὑπὸ τριῶν ἴσων :term:`ἀριθμῶν <number>` περιεχόμενος.

Similarly cube numbers which ended with the same number as their sides, *and the squares of those sides also*, were called *spherical* (σφαιρικοί) or *recurrent* (ἀποκαταστατικοί). One might have expected that the term *spherical* would be applicable also to the cubes of numbers which ended with the same digit as the side but not necessarily with the same digit as the *square* of the side also. E.g. the cube of 4, i.e. 64, ends with the same digit as 4, but not with the same digit as 16. But apparently 64 was not called a spherical number, the only instances given by :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` and the rest being those cubed from numbers ending with 5 or 6, which end with the same digit *if squared*. A spherical number is in fact derived from a *circular* number only, and that by adding another equal dimension. Obviously, as :person:`Nesselmann <nesselmann>` says, the names *cyclic* and *spherical* applied to numbers appeal to an entirely different principle from that on which the figured numbers so far dealt with were formed.
