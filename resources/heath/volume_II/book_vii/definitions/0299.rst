For there are three means, of which one is arithmetic, one geometric and one harmonic." The last remark implies plainly enough that there is only one *proportion* (:term:`ἀναλογία <analogia>`) in the proper sense. So, too, says :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>` in another place (p. 104, 19): "the second, the geometric, mean has been called *proportion par excellence* because the terms contain the same ratio, being separated according to the same proportion (ἀνὰ τὸν αὐτὸν :term:`λόγον <ratio>` διεστῶτες)." The natural conclusion is that of :person:`Nesselmann <nesselmann>`, that originally the geometric proportion was called :term:`ἀναλογία <analogia>`, the others, the arithmetic, the harmonic, etc., *means*; but later usage had obliterated the distinction.

Of proportions in the ancient and Euclidean sense Theon (p. 82, 10) distinguished the *continuous* (συνεχής) and the *separated* (διῃρημένη), using the same terms as :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` (:cite:`*Eth. Nic.* 1131 a 32 <aristotle:eth-nic:1131-a-32>`). The meaning is of course clear: in the *continuous* proportion the consequent of one ratio is the antecedent of the next; in the *separated* proportion this is not so. :person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` (:cite:`II. 21, 5 <nicomachus:intro-arith:ii-21-5>`—6) uses the words *connected* (συνημμένη) and *disjoined* (διεζευγμένη) respectively. Euclid regularly speaks of numbers in continuous proportion as "proportional in order, or successively" (ἑξῆς :term:`ἀνάλογον <proportional>`).

DEFINITION 21.
--------------

Ὅμοιοι :term:`ἐπίπεδοι <plane-number>` καὶ στερεοὶ :term:`ἀριθμοί <number>` εἰσιν οἱ :term:`ἀνάλογον <proportional>` ἔχοντες τὰς πλευράς.

:person:`Theon of Smyrna <theon-of-smyrna>` remarks (p. 36, 12) that, among plane numbers, *all* squares are similar, while of :term:`ἑτερομήκεις <heteromekes-arithmos>` those are similar "whose sides, that is, the numbers containing them, are proportional." Here :term:`ἑτερομήκης <heteromekes-arithmos>` must evidently be used, not in the sense of a number of the form $n(n + 1)$, but. as synonymous with :term:`προμήκης <promekes-arithmos>`, *any* oblong number; so that on this occasion Theon follows the terminology of :person:`Plato <plato>` and (according to :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>`) of Euclid. Obviously, if the strict sense of :term:`ἑτερομήκης <heteromekes-arithmos>` is adhered to, no two numbers of that form can be similar unless they are also *equal*. We may compare :person:`Iamblichus <iamblichus>`' elaborate contrast of the square and the :term:`ἑτερομήκης <heteromekes-arithmos>`. Since the two sides of the square are equal, a square number might, as he says (p. 82, 9), be fitly called ἰδιομήκης (:person:`Nicomachus <nicomachus>` uses ταὐτομήκης) in contrast to :term:`ἑτερομήκης <heteromekes-arithmos>`; and the ancients, according to him, called square numbers "the same" and "similar" (ταὐτούς τε καὶ ὁμοίους), but :term:`ἑτερομήκεις <heteromekes-arithmos>` numbers "dissimilar and other" (ἀνομοίους καὶ θατέρους).

With regard to solid numbers, Theon remarks in like manner (p. 37, 2) that *all* cube numbers are similar, while of the others those are similar whose sides are proportional, i.e. in which, as length is to length, so is breadth to breadth and height to height.
