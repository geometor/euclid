Euclid's proposition is thus *identical* with the algebraical proposition as<br />

Nicomachus gives the same rule (though without proving it) when he<br />
shows how to determine whether two given *odd* numbers are prime or not<br />
prime to one another, and, if they are not prime to one another, what is their<br />
common measure. We are, he says, to compare the numbers in turn by<br />
continually taking the less from the greater as many times as possible,<br />
then taking the remainder as many times as possible from the less of the<br />
original numbers, and so on; this process "will finish either at an unit or at<br />
some one and the same number," by which it is implied that the division of a<br />
greater number by a less is done by *separate subtractions* of the less. Thus,<br />
with regard to 21 and 49, Nicomachus says, "I subtract the less from the<br />
greater; 28 is left; then again I subtract from this the same 21 (for this is<br />
possible); 7 is left; I subtract this from 21, 14 is left; from which I again<br />
subtract 7 (for this is possible); 7 will be left, but 7 cannot be subtracted from<br />
7." The last phrase is curious, but the meaning of it is obvious enough, as<br />
also the meaning of the phrase about ending "at one and the same number."

### PROPOSITION 3.

<p class="enunciation">Given three numbers not prime to one another, to find their<br />
greatest common measure.</p>

Let <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span> be the three given numbers not prime to<br />
one another ;<br />
thus it is required to find the greatest<br />
common measure of <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span>.

For let the greatest common measure,<br />
<span data-ref="0306.g1:D">$D$</span>, of the two numbers <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span> be taken ; <span class="justification">[VII. 2]</span><br />
then <span data-ref="0306.g1:D">$D$</span> either measures, or does not<br />
measure, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span>.

![Proposition 3 Diagram](0306.g1.svg)

First, let it measure it.

<p class="sub-clause">But it measures <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span> also ;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0306.g1:D">$D$</span> measures <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span> ;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0306.g1:D">$D$</span> is a common measure of <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span>.</p>

I say that it is also the greatest.
