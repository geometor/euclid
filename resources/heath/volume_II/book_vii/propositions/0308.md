<p class="sub-clause">Now, since <span data-ref="0306.g1:F">$F$</span> measures <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span>,<br />
it also measures <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span> ;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore it will also measure the greatest common measure<br />
of <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span>. <span class="justification">[VII. 2, Por.]</span></p>

<p class="sub-clause">But the greatest common measure of <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span> is <span data-ref="0306.g1:D">$D$</span> ;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0306.g1:F">$F$</span> measures <span data-ref="0306.g1:D">$D$</span>.</p>

<p class="sub-clause">And it measures <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span> also ;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0306.g1:F">$F$</span> measures <span data-ref="0306.g1:D">$D$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span> ;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore it will also measure the greatest common measure<br />
of <span data-ref="0306.g1:D">$D$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span>. <span class="justification">[VII. 2, Por.]</span></p>

<p class="sub-clause">But the greatest common measure of <span data-ref="0306.g1:D">$D$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span> is <span data-ref="0306.g1:E">$E$</span> ;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0306.g1:F">$F$</span> measures <span data-ref="0306.g1:E">$E$</span>, the greater the less : which is<br />
impossible.</p>

<p class="assertion">Therefore no number which is greater than <span data-ref="0306.g1:E">$E$</span> will measure<br />
the numbers <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span> ;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0306.g1:E">$E$</span> is the greatest common measure of <span data-ref="0306.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0306.g1:C">$C$</span>.</p>

<p class="qed"><span class="justification">Q. E. D.</span></p>

Euclid's proof is here longer than we should make it because he<br />
distinguishes two cases, the simpler of which is really included in the other.

Having taken the greatest common measure, say $d$, of $a$, $b$, two of the<br />
three given numbers $a$, $b$, $c$, he distinguishes the cases<br />
  (1) in which $d$ measures $c$,<br />
  (2) in which $d$ does not measure $c$.

In the first case the greatest common measure of $d$, $c$ is $d$ itself; in the<br />
second case it has to be found by a repetition of the process of VII. 2. In<br />
either case the greatest common measure of $a$, $b$, $c$ is the greatest common<br />
measure of $d$, $c$.

But, after disposing of the simpler case, Euclid thinks it necessary to<br />
prove that, if $d$ does not measure $c$, $d$ and $c$ must necessarily *have* a greatest<br />
common measure. This he does by means of the original hypothesis that<br />
$a$, $b$, $c$ are not prime to one another. Since they are not prime to one another,<br />
they must have a common measure; any common measure of $a$, $b$ is a measure<br />
of $d$, and therefore any common measure of $a$, $b$, $c$ is a common measure of<br />
$d$, $c$; hence $d$, $c$ must have a common measure, and are therefore not prime to<br />
one another.

The proofs of cases (1) and (2) repeat exactly the same argument as we<br />
saw in VII. 2, and it is proved separately for $d$ in case (1) and $e$ in case (2),<br />
where $e$ is the greatest common measure of $d$, $c$,<br />
  ($\alpha$) that it is a common measure of $a$, $b$, $c$,<br />
  ($\beta$) that it is the *greatest* common measure.

Heron remarks (an-Nairīzī, ed. Curtze, p. 191) that the method does<br />
not only enable us to find the greatest common measure of *three* numbers;<br />
it can be used to find the greatest common measure of as many numbers
