.. subclause::

   | Now, since :g:`$F$ <0306.g1:F>` measures :g:`$A$ <0306.g1:A>`, :g:`$B$ <0306.g1:B>`, :g:`$C$ <0306.g1:C>`,
   | it also measures :g:`$A$ <0306.g1:A>`, :g:`$B$ <0306.g1:B>` ;

.. assertion::

   | therefore it will also measure the greatest common measure
   | of :g:`$A$ <0306.g1:A>`, :g:`$B$ <0306.g1:B>`. :just:`[VII. 2, Por.]`

.. subclause::

   But the greatest common measure of :g:`$A$ <0306.g1:A>`, :g:`$B$ <0306.g1:B>` is :g:`$D$ <0306.g1:D>` ;

.. assertion::

   therefore :g:`$F$ <0306.g1:F>` measures :g:`$D$ <0306.g1:D>`.

.. subclause::

   And it measures :g:`$C$ <0306.g1:C>` also ;

.. assertion::

   therefore :g:`$F$ <0306.g1:F>` measures :g:`$D$ <0306.g1:D>`, :g:`$C$ <0306.g1:C>` ;

.. assertion::

   | therefore it will also measure the greatest common measure
   | of :g:`$D$ <0306.g1:D>`, :g:`$C$ <0306.g1:C>`. :just:`[VII. 2, Por.]`

.. subclause::

   But the greatest common measure of :g:`$D$ <0306.g1:D>`, :g:`$C$ <0306.g1:C>` is :g:`$E$ <0306.g1:E>` ;

.. assertion::

   | therefore :g:`$F$ <0306.g1:F>` measures :g:`$E$ <0306.g1:E>`, the greater the less : which is
   | impossible.

.. assertion::

   | Therefore no number which is greater than :g:`$E$ <0306.g1:E>` will measure
   | the numbers :g:`$A$ <0306.g1:A>`, :g:`$B$ <0306.g1:B>`, :g:`$C$ <0306.g1:C>` ;

.. assertion::

   therefore :g:`$E$ <0306.g1:E>` is the greatest common measure of :g:`$A$ <0306.g1:A>`, :g:`$B$ <0306.g1:B>`, :g:`$C$ <0306.g1:C>`.

.. qed::

   :just:`Q. E. D.`

----

| Euclid's proof is here longer than we should make it because he
| distinguishes two cases, the simpler of which is really included in the other.

| Having taken the greatest common measure, say $d$, of $a$, $b$, two of the
| three given numbers $a$, $b$, $c$, he distinguishes the cases
|   (1) in which $d$ measures $c$,
|   (2) in which $d$ does not measure $c$.

| In the first case the greatest common measure of $d$, $c$ is $d$ itself; in the
| second case it has to be found by a repetition of the process of VII. 2. In
| either case the greatest common measure of $a$, $b$, $c$ is the greatest common
| measure of $d$, $c$.

| But, after disposing of the simpler case, Euclid thinks it necessary to
| prove that, if $d$ does not measure $c$, $d$ and $c$ must necessarily *have* a greatest
| common measure. This he does by means of the original hypothesis that
| $a$, $b$, $c$ are not prime to one another. Since they are not prime to one another,
| they must have a common measure; any common measure of $a$, $b$ is a measure
| of $d$, and therefore any common measure of $a$, $b$, $c$ is a common measure of
| $d$, $c$; hence $d$, $c$ must have a common measure, and are therefore not prime to
| one another.

| The proofs of cases (1) and (2) repeat exactly the same argument as we
| saw in VII. 2, and it is proved separately for $d$ in case (1) and $e$ in case (2),
| where $e$ is the greatest common measure of $d$, $c$,
|   ($\alpha$) that it is a common measure of $a$, $b$, $c$,
|   ($\beta$) that it is the *greatest* common measure.

| Heron remarks (an-Nairīzī, ed. Curtze, p. 191) that the method does
| not only enable us to find the greatest common measure of *three* numbers;
| it can be used to find the greatest common measure of as many numbers
