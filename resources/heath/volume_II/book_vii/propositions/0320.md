$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{If} \quad & a : b = d : e, \\
\text{and} \quad & b : c = e : f, \\
\text{then, } \textit{ex aequali}, \quad & a : c = d : f;
\end{aligned}
$$

and the same is true however many successive numbers are so related.<br />
The proof is simplicity itself.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{By VII. 13, alternately,} \quad & a : d = b : e, \\
\text{and} \quad & b : e = c : f. \\
\text{Therefore} \quad & a : d = c : f, \\
\text{and, again alternately,} \quad & a : c = d : f.
\end{aligned}
$$

Observe that this simple method cannot be used to prove the corresponding<br />
proposition for magnitudes, V. 22, although V. 22 has been preceded by the<br />
two propositions in that Book corresponding to the propositions used here,<br />
viz. V. 16 and V. 11. The reason of this is that this method would only prove<br />
V. 22 for six magnitudes *all of the same kind*, whereas the magnitudes in V. 22<br />
are not subject to this limitation.

Heiberg remarks in a note on VII. 19 that, while Euclid has proved<br />
several propositions of Book V. over again, by a separate proof, for numbers,<br />
he has neglected to do so in certain cases; e.g., he often uses V. 11 in these pro-<br />
positions of Book VII., V. 9 in VII. 19, V. 7 in the same proposition, and so on.<br />
Thus Heiberg would apparently suppose Euclid to use V. 11 in the last step<br />
of the present proof (*Ratios which are the same with the same ratio are also the<br />
same with one another*). I think it preferable to suppose that Euclid regarded<br />
the last step as axiomatic; since, by the definition of proportion, the first<br />
number is the same multiple or the same part or the same parts of the second<br />
that the third is of the fourth: the assumption is no more than an assumption<br />
that the numbers or proper fractions which are respectively equal to the same<br />
number or proper fraction are equal to one another.

Though the proposition is only proved of six numbers, the extension to as<br />
many as we please (as expressed in the enunciation) is obvious.

### PROPOSITION 15.

<p class="enunciation">If an unit measure any number, and another number measure<br />
any other number the same number of times, alternately also,<br />
the unit will measure the third number the same number of<br />
times that the second measures the fourth.</p>
