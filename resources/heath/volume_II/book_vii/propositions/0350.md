For, if not, there will be some number less than *G* which will have the parts *A*, *B*, *C*.

Let it be *H*.

Since *H* has the parts *A*, *B*, *C*, therefore *H* will be measured by numbers called by the same name as the parts *A*, *B*, *C*. [VII. 38]

But *D*, *E*, *F* are numbers called by the same name as the parts *A*, *B*, *C*; therefore *H* is measured by *D*, *E*, *F*.

And it is less than *G*: which is impossible.

Therefore there will be no number less than *G* that will have the parts *A*, *B*, *C*.

Q. E. D.

---

This again is practically a restatement in another form of the problem of finding the L.C.M.

To find a number which has $\frac{1}{a}\text{th}$, $\frac{1}{b}\text{th}$ and $\frac{1}{c}\text{th}$ parts.

Let *d* be the L.C.M. of *a, b, c*.

Thus *d* has $\frac{1}{a}\text{th}$, $\frac{1}{b}\text{th}$ and $\frac{1}{c}\text{th}$ parts. [VII. 37]

If it is not the least number which has, let the least such number be *e*.

Then, since *e* has those parts,
*e* is measured by *a, b, c*; and *e* &lt; *d*:
which is impossible.
