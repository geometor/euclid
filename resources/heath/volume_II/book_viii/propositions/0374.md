If $a^2$ measures $b^2$, $a$ measures $b$; and, if $a$ measures $b$, $a^2$ measures $b^2$.

(1) $a^2$, $ab$, $b^2$ are in continued proportion in the ratio of $a$ to $b$.
$$\begin{array}{ll}
\text{Therefore, since} & a^2 \text{ measures } b^2\text{,} \\
& a^2 \text{ measures } ab\text{.} & \text{[VIII. 7]} \\
\text{But} & a^2 : ab = a : b\text{.} \\
\text{Therefore} & a \text{ measures } b\text{.}
\end{array}$$

(2) Since $a$ measures $b$, $a^2$ measures $ab$.<br />
And $a^2$, $ab$, $b^2$ are continuously proportional.
$$\begin{array}{ll}
\text{Thus} & ab \text{ measures } b^2\text{.} \\
\text{And} & a^2 \text{ measures } ab\text{.} \\
\text{Therefore} & a^2 \text{ measures } b^2\text{.}
\end{array}$$

It will be seen that Euclid puts the last step shortly, saying that, since<br />
$a^2$ measures $ab$, and $a^2$, $ab$, $b^2$ are in continued proportion, $a^2$ measures $b^2$.<br />
The same thing happens in VIII. 15, where the series of terms is one more<br />
than here.

### PROPOSITION 15.

<p class="enunciation">If a cube number measure a cube number, the side will also<br />
measure the side; and, if the side measure the side, the cube<br />
will also measure the cube.</p>

For let the cube number <span data-ref="0375.g1:A">$A$</span> measure the cube <span data-ref="0375.g1:B">$B$</span>,<br />
and let <span data-ref="0375.g1:C">$C$</span> be the side of <span data-ref="0375.g1:A">$A$</span> and <span data-ref="0375.g1:D">$D$</span> of <span data-ref="0375.g1:B">$B$</span>;<br />
I say that <span data-ref="0375.g1:C">$C$</span> measures <span data-ref="0375.g1:D">$D$</span>.
