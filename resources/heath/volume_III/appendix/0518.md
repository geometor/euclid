Let <span data-ref="0518.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> be a circle, <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> the side of the pentagon, <span data-ref="0518.g1:D">$D$</span> the centre; draw <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ D F /">$DF$</span> perpendicular to <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> and produce it to <span data-ref="0518.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0518.g1:E">$E$</span>; join <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ A E /">$AE$</span>.

I say that
<span data-ref="0518.g1:/ B A /">$BA$</span>$^2 + $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>$^2 = 5 $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>$^2$.

For, since <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span> $= 2 $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ E D /">$ED$</span>,
<span data-ref="0518.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span>$^2 = 4 $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ E D /">$ED$</span>$^2$.

And
<span data-ref="0518.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span>$^2 = $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ B A /">$BA$</span>$^2 + $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ A E /">$AE$</span>$^2$;
therefore
<span data-ref="0518.g1:/ B A /">$BA$</span>$^2 + $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ A E /">$AE$</span>$^2 + $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ E D /">$ED$</span>$^2 = 5 $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ E D /">$ED$</span>$^2$.

But
<span data-ref="0518.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>$^2 = $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>$^2 + $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ E A /">$EA$</span>$^2$; [Eucl. XIII. 10]
therefore
<span data-ref="0518.g1:/ B A /">$BA$</span>$^2 + $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>$^2 = 5 $ <span data-ref="0518.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span>$^2$.

"This being proved, it is required to prove that the same circle circumscribes both the pentagon of the dodecahedron and the triangle of the icosahedron inscribed in the same sphere."

Let <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span> be the diameter of the sphere, and let a dodecahedron and an icosahedron be inscribed.

![Book XIV, Prop. 2](0518.g2.svg)

Let <span data-ref="0518.g2:< C D E F G >">$CDEFG$</span> be one pentagon of the dodecahedron, and <span data-ref="0518.g2:< K L H >">$KLH$</span> one triangle of the icosahedron.

I say that the radii of the circles circumscribing them are equal.

Join <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ D G /">$DG$</span>; then <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ D G /">$DG$</span> is the side of a cube inscribed in the sphere. [Eucl. XIII. 17]

Take a straight line <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ M N /">$MN$</span> such that <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>$^2 = 5 $ <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ M N /">$MN$</span>$^2$.

Now the square on the diameter of the sphere is five times the square on the radius of the circle from which the icosahedron is described. [XIII. 16, Por.]

Therefore <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ M N /">$MN$</span> is equal to the radius of the circle passing through the five vertices of the icosahedron which form a pentagon.

Cut <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ M N /">$MN$</span> in extreme and mean ratio at <span data-ref="0518.g2:O">$O$</span>, <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ M O /">$MO$</span> being the greater segment.

Therefore <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ M O /">$MO$</span> is the side of the decagon in the circle with radius <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ M N /">$MN$</span>. [XIII. 9 and 5, *converse*]

Now
$5 $ <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ M N /">$MN$</span>$^2 = $ <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>$^2 = 3 $ <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ D G /">$DG$</span>$^2$. [XIII. 15]

But
$3 $ <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ D G /">$DG$</span>$^2 : 3 $ <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ C G /">$CG$</span>$^2 = 5 $ <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ M N /">$MN$</span>$^2 : 5 $ <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ M O /">$MO$</span>$^2$
(since, if <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ D G /">$DG$</span> is cut in extreme and mean ratio, the greater segment is equal to <span data-ref="0518.g2:/ C G /">$CG$</span>, and, if two straight lines are cut in extreme and mean ratio, their segments are in the same ratio: see lemma later, pp. 518–9).
