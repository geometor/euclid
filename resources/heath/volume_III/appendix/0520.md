Let <span data-ref="0520.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> be the circle circumscribing the pentagon of the dodecahedron and the triangle of the icosahedron, and let <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> be the side of the triangle, <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> that of the pentagon.

Let <span data-ref="0520.g1:E">$E$</span> be the centre, and <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span>, <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span> perpendiculars to <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>, <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>.

Produce <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span> to meet the circle in <span data-ref="0520.g1:B">$B$</span> and join <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>.

Set out <span data-ref="0520.g1:H">$H$</span> equal to the side of the cube inscribed in the same sphere.

I say that
$(\text{surface of dodecahedron}) : (\text{surface of icosahedron}) = $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:H">$H$</span> $: $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>.

![Book XIV, Prop. 6](0520.g1.svg)

For, since the sum of <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span>, <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> is divided at <span data-ref="0520.g1:B">$B$</span> in extreme and mean ratio, and <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span> is the greater segment, [XIII. 9]
and <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span> $= \frac{1}{2}($<span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span> $+ $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>$)$, [Prop. 1]
while <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> $= \frac{1}{2}$<span data-ref="0520.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span>, [see p. 513 above]
therefore, if <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span> is divided in extreme and mean ratio, the greater segment is equal to <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> [that is to say, since <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span> is the greater segment of <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span> $+ $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> divided in extreme and mean ratio, $\frac{1}{2}$<span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span> is the greater segment of $\frac{1}{2}($<span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span> $+ $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>$)$ similarly divided].

But, if <span data-ref="0520.g1:H">$H$</span> is also divided in extreme and mean ratio, the greater segment is equal to <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C A /">$CA$</span>. [XIII. 17, Por.]

Therefore
<span data-ref="0520.g1:H">$H$</span> $: $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C A /">$CA$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span> $: $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span>,
or
<span data-ref="0520.g1:/ F E /">$FE$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:H">$H$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C A /">$CA$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span>.

And, since
<span data-ref="0520.g1:H">$H$</span> $: $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ F E /">$FE$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:H">$H$</span> $: $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ F E /">$FE$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>,
and
<span data-ref="0520.g1:/ F E /">$FE$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:H">$H$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C A /">$CA$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span>,
therefore
<span data-ref="0520.g1:H">$H$</span> $: $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C A /">$CA$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span> $: $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ F E /">$FE$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> $= (\text{surface of dodecahedron}) : (\text{surf. of icos.})$. [Prop. 5]

*Another proof of the same theorem.*

*Preliminary.*

Let <span data-ref="0520.g2:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> be a circle and <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A C /">$AC$</span> sides of an inscribed regular pentagon. Join <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ B C /">$BC$</span>; take <span data-ref="0520.g2:D">$D$</span> the centre of the circle, join <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span> and produce it to meet the circle at <span data-ref="0520.g2:E">$E$</span>. Join <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ B D /">$BD$</span>.

Let <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ D F /">$DF$</span> be made equal to $\frac{1}{2}$<span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span>, and <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ C H /">$CH$</span> equal to $\frac{1}{3}$<span data-ref="0520.g2:/ C G /">$CG$</span>.

I say that
$\text{rect. }$ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span>, <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ B H /">$BH$</span> $= (\text{area of pentagon})$.

![Book XIV, Prop. 6 Alternative Proof Preliminary](0520.g2.svg)

For, since <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span> $= 2 $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ D F /">$DF$</span>,
<span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span> $= \frac{3}{2} $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span>.

And, since <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ G C /">$GC$</span> $= 3 $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ H C /">$HC$</span>,
<span data-ref="0520.g2:/ G C /">$GC$</span> $= \frac{3}{2} $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ G H /">$GH$</span>.

Therefore
<span data-ref="0520.g2:/ F A /">$FA$</span> $: $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ C G /">$CG$</span> $: $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ G H /">$GH$</span>,
so that
<span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A F /">$AF$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ G H /">$GH$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ C G /">$CG$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ A D /">$AD$</span> $\cdot $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:/ B G /">$BG$</span> $= 2 (\triangle $ <span data-ref="0520.g2:< A B D >">$ABD$</span>$)$.
