*Proof of theorem.*

This being clear, let the circle be set out which circumscribes the pentagon of the dodecahedron and the triangle of the icosahedron inscribed in the same sphere.

Let <span data-ref="0521.g1:( A B C )">$ABC$</span> be the circle, and <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> two sides of the pentagon; join <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>.

Take <span data-ref="0521.g1:E">$E$</span> the centre of the circle, join <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ A E /">$AE$</span> and produce it to <span data-ref="0521.g1:F">$F$</span>.

Let <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ A E /">$AE$</span> $= 2 $ <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span>, <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ K C /">$KC$</span> $= 3 $ <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ C H /">$CH$</span>.

Through <span data-ref="0521.g1:G">$G$</span> draw <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ D M /">$DM$</span> at right angles to <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ A F /">$AF$</span> meeting the circle at <span data-ref="0521.g1:D">$D$</span>, <span data-ref="0521.g1:M">$M$</span>;
<span data-ref="0521.g1:/ D M /">$DM$</span> is then the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle.

Join <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span>, <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ A M /">$AM$</span>, which are equal to <span data-ref="0521.g1:/ D M /">$DM$</span>.

"Next we have to prove that,

[Prop. 7] *If any straight line whatever be cut in extreme and mean ratio, then, as is (1) the straight line the square on which is equal to the sum of the squares on the whole line and on the greater segment to (2) the straight line the square on which is equal to the sum of the squares on the whole line and on the lesser segment, so is (3) the side of the cube to (4) the side of the icosahedron.*"

Let <span data-ref="0521.g2:( A H B )">$AHB$</span> be the circle circumscribing both the pentagon of the dodecahedron and the triangle of the icosahedron inscribed in the same sphere, <span data-ref="0521.g2:C">$C$</span> the centre of the circle, and <span data-ref="0521.g2:/ C B /">$CB$</span> any radius divided at <span data-ref="0521.g2:D">$D$</span> in extreme and mean ratio, <span data-ref="0521.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> being the greater segment.

<span data-ref="0521.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> is then the side of the decagon inscribed in the circle. [XIII. 9 and 5, converse]

Let <span data-ref="0521.g2:E">$E$</span> be the side of the icosahedron, <span data-ref="0521.g2:F">$F$</span> that of the dodecahedron, and <span data-ref="0521.g2:G">$G$</span> that of the cube, inscribed in the sphere.

Then <span data-ref="0521.g2:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0521.g2:F">$F$</span> are the sides of the equilateral triangle and pentagon inscribed in the circle, and, if <span data-ref="0521.g2:G">$G$</span> is divided in extreme and mean ratio, the greater segment is equal to <span data-ref="0521.g2:F">$F$</span>. [XIII. 17, Por.]
