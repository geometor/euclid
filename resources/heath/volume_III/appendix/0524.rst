This :person:`Isidorus <isidorus-of-miletus>` is no doubt :person:`Isidorus of Miletus <isidorus-of-miletus>`, the architect of the Church of St Sophia at Constantinople (about 532 A.D.), whose pupil :person:`Eutocius <eutocius>` also was; he is often referred to by :person:`Eutocius <eutocius>` (:cite:`*Comm. on Archimedes* <eutocius:in-archimedem>`) as ὁ Μιλήσιος μηχανικὸς Ἰσίδωρος ἡμέτερος διδάσκαλος. Thus the third portion of the Book at all events was written by a pupil of :person:`Isidorus <isidorus-of-miletus>` in the sixth century. :person:`Kluge <kluge>` (:cite:`*De Euclidis elementorum libris qui feruntur XIV et XV* <kluge:de-euclidis-xiv-xv>`, Leipzig, 1891) has closely examined the language and style of the three portions and conjectures that they may be the work of different authors; the first portion may, he thinks, date from the end of the third century (the time of :person:`Pappus <pappus>`), and the second portion too may be older than the third. :person:`Hultsch <hultsch>` however (art. "Eukleides" in Pauly-Wissowa's :cite:`*Real-Encyclopädie der classischen Altertumswissenschaft* <pauly-wissowa:real-encyclopaedie>`, 1907) does not think his arguments convincing.

It may be worth while to set out the particulars of :person:`Isidorus <isidorus-of-miletus>`' rules for constructing isosceles triangles with vertical angles equal respectively to the angles of inclination between faces meeting in an edge of the several regular solids. A certain base is taken, and then with its extremities as centres and a certain other straight line as radius two circles are drawn; their point of intersection determines the vertex of the particular isosceles triangle. In the case of the cube the triangle is of course right-angled; in the other cases the bases and the equal sides are as shown below.
