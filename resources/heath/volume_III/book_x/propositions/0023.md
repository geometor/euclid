divided, and so on. Now the greater segment in a line thus divided is greater<br />
than half the line, but it follows from XIII. 3 that it is less than twice the<br />
lesser segment, i.e. the lesser segment can never be marked off more than<br />
*once* from the greater. Our process of marking off the lesser segment from the<br />
greater continually is thus exactly that of finding the greatest common measure.<br />
If, therefore, the segments were commensurable, the process would stop. But<br />
it clearly does not; therefore the segments are incommensurable.

Allman expresses the opinion that it was rather in connexion with the line<br />
cut in extreme and mean ratio than with reference to the diagonal and side<br />
of a square that the Pythagoreans discovered the incommensurable. But the<br />
evidence seems to put it beyond doubt that the Pythagoreans did discover<br />
the incommensurability of $\sqrt{2}$ and devoted much attention to this particular<br />
case. The view of Allman does not therefore commend itself to me, though<br />
it is likely enough that the Pythagoreans were aware of the incommensura-<br />
bility of the segments of a line cut in extreme and mean ratio. At all events<br />
the Pythagoreans could hardly have carried their investigations into the in-<br />
commensurability of the segments of this line very far, since Theaetetus is<br />
said to have made the first classification of irrationals, and to him is also, with<br />
reasonable probability, attributed the substance of the first part of Eucl. XIII.,<br />
in the sixth proposition of which occurs the proof that the segments of a<br />
rational straight line cut in extreme and mean ratio are *apotomes*.

Again, the incommensurability of $\sqrt{2}$ can be proved by a method<br />
practically equivalent to that of X. 2, and without carrying the process very<br />
far. This method is given in Chrystal's *Text-<br />
book of Algebra* (I. p. 270). Let <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ A C /">$d$</span>, <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ A B /">$a$</span> be the<br />
diagonal and side respectively of a square<br />
<span data-ref="0023.g1:< A B C D >">$ABCD$</span>. Mark off <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ A F /">$AF$</span> along <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> equal to <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ A B /">$a$</span>.<br />
Draw <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ F E /">$FE$</span> at right angles to <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> meeting <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span><br />
in <span data-ref="0023.g1:E">$E$</span>.

![Incommensurability of diagonal and side of square](0023.g1.svg)

It is easily proved that
$$BE = EF = FC,$$
$$CF = AC - AB = d - a \dots\dots\dots\dots (1),$$
$$CE = CB - CF = a - (d - a)$$
$$\phantom{CE} = 2a - d \dots\dots\dots\dots (2).$$

Suppose, if possible, that <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ A C /">$d$</span>, <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ A B /">$a$</span> are commensurable. If <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ A C /">$d$</span>, <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ A B /">$a$</span> are both<br />
commensurably expressible in terms of any finite unit, each must be an<br />
integral multiple of a certain finite unit.

But from (1) it follows that <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ C F /">$CF$</span>, and from (2) it follows that <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span>, is an<br />
integral multiple of the same unit.

And <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ C F /">$CF$</span>, <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ C E /">$CE$</span> are the side and diagonal of a square <span data-ref="0023.g1:< C F E G >">$CFEG$</span>, the side of<br />
which is *less than half* the side of the original square. If <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ F C /">$a_1$</span>, <span data-ref="0023.g1:/ E C /">$d_1$</span> are the side and<br />
diagonal of this square,
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
a_1 &= d - a \\
d_1 &= 2a - d
\end{aligned}
\right\}.
$$

Similarly we can form a square with side $a_2$ and diagonal $d_2$ which are less<br />
than half $a_1$, $d_1$ respectively, and $a_2$, $d_2$ must be integral multiples of the same<br />
unit, where
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_2 &= d_1 - a_1, \\
d_2 &= 2a_1 - d_1;
\end{aligned}
$$
