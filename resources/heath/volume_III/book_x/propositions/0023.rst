| mark off from :g:`$AC$ <0022.g1:/ A C />` a portion equal to :g:`$CB$ <0022.g1:/ C B />`, and :g:`$AC$ <0022.g1:/ A C />` will then be similarly
| divided, and so on. Now the greater segment in a line thus divided is greater
| than half the line, but it follows from XIII. 3 that it is less than twice the
| lesser segment, i.e. the lesser segment can never be marked off more than
| *once* from the greater. Our process of marking off the lesser segment from the
| greater continually is thus exactly that of finding the greatest common measure.
| If, therefore, the segments were commensurable, the process would stop. But
| it clearly does not; therefore the segments are incommensurable.

| :person:`Allman <allman>` expresses the opinion that it was rather in connexion with the line
| cut in extreme and mean ratio than with reference to the diagonal and side
| of a square that the Pythagoreans discovered the incommensurable. But the
| evidence seems to put it beyond doubt that the Pythagoreans did discover
| the incommensurability of $\sqrt{2}$ and devoted much attention to this particular
| case. The view of :person:`Allman <allman>` does not therefore commend itself to me, though
| it is likely enough that the Pythagoreans were aware of the incommensura-
| bility of the segments of a line cut in extreme and mean ratio. At all events
| the Pythagoreans could hardly have carried their investigations into the in-
| commensurability of the segments of this line very far, since :person:`Theaetetus <theaetetus>` is
| said to have made the first classification of irrationals, and to him is also, with
| reasonable probability, attributed the substance of the first part of Eucl. XIII.,
| in the sixth proposition of which occurs the proof that the segments of a
| rational straight line cut in extreme and mean ratio are *apotomes*.

| Again, the incommensurability of $\sqrt{2}$ can be proved by a method
| practically equivalent to that of X. 2, and without carrying the process very
| far. This method is given in :person:`Chrystal <chrystal>`'s :cite:`*Text-book of Algebra* <chrystal:textbook-of-algebra>` (I. p. 270). Let :g:`$d$ <0023.g1:/ A C />`, :g:`$a$ <0023.g1:/ A B />` be the
| diagonal and side respectively of a square
| :g:`$ABCD$ <0023.g1:< A B C D >>`. Mark off :g:`$AF$ <0023.g1:/ A F />` along :g:`$AC$ <0023.g1:/ A C />` equal to :g:`$a$ <0023.g1:/ A B />`.
| Draw :g:`$FE$ <0023.g1:/ F E />` at right angles to :g:`$AC$ <0023.g1:/ A C />` meeting :g:`$BC$ <0023.g1:/ B C />`
| in :g:`$E$ <0023.g1:E>`.

.. picture:: 0023.g1.svg
   :alt: Incommensurability of diagonal and side of square

It is easily proved that

$$BE = EF = FC,$$

$$CF = AC - AB = d - a \dots\dots\dots\dots (1),$$

$$CE = CB - CF = a - (d - a)$$

$$\phantom{CE} = 2a - d \dots\dots\dots\dots (2).$$

| Suppose, if possible, that :g:`$d$ <0023.g1:/ A C />`, :g:`$a$ <0023.g1:/ A B />` are commensurable. If :g:`$d$ <0023.g1:/ A C />`, :g:`$a$ <0023.g1:/ A B />` are both
| commensurably expressible in terms of any finite unit, each must be an
| integral multiple of a certain finite unit.

| But from (1) it follows that :g:`$CF$ <0023.g1:/ C F />`, and from (2) it follows that :g:`$CE$ <0023.g1:/ C E />`, is an
| integral multiple of the same unit.

| And :g:`$CF$ <0023.g1:/ C F />`, :g:`$CE$ <0023.g1:/ C E />` are the side and diagonal of a square :g:`$CFEG$ <0023.g1:< C F E G >>`, the side of
| which is *less than half* the side of the original square. If :g:`$a_1$ <0023.g1:/ F C />`, :g:`$d_1$ <0023.g1:/ E C />` are the side and
| diagonal of this square,

$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
a_1 &= d - a \\
d_1 &= 2a - d
\end{aligned}
\right\}.
$$

| Similarly we can form a square with side $a_2$ and diagonal $d_2$ which are less
| than half $a_1$, $d_1$ respectively, and $a_2$, $d_2$ must be integral multiples of the same
| unit, where

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_2 &= d_1 - a_1, \\
d_2 &= 2a_1 - d_1;
\end{aligned}
$$
