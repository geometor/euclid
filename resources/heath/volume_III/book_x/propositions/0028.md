This proposition again corresponds exactly to VII. 3 for numbers. As<br />
there Euclid thinks it necessary to prove that, $a, b, c$ not being prime to one<br />
another, $d$ and $c$ are also not prime to one another, so here he thinks it<br />
necessary to prove that $d, c$ are commensurable, as they must be since any<br />
common measure of $a, b$ must be a measure of their greatest common<br />
measure $d$ (X. 3, Por.).

The argument in the proof that $e$, the greatest common measure of $d, c$, is<br />
the greatest common measure of $a, b, c$, is the same as that in VII. 3 and X. 3.

The Porism contains the extension of the process to the case of four<br />
or more magnitudes, corresponding to Heron's remark with regard to the<br />
similar extension of VII. 3 to the case of four or more *numbers*.

### PROPOSITION 5.

<p class="enunciation">Commensurable magnitudes have to one another the ratio<br />
which a number has to a number.</p>

Let <span data-ref="0028.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0028.g1:B">$B$</span> be commensurable magnitudes;<br />
I say that <span data-ref="0028.g1:A">$A$</span> has to <span data-ref="0028.g1:B">$B$</span> the ratio which a number has to a<br />
number.

For, since <span data-ref="0028.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0028.g1:B">$B$</span> are commensurable, some magnitude will<br />
measure them.<br />
Let it measure them, and let it be <span data-ref="0028.g1:C">$C$</span>.

![Proposition 5 diagram](0028.g1.svg)

And, as many times as <span data-ref="0028.g1:C">$C$</span> measures <span data-ref="0028.g1:A">$A$</span>, so many units let<br />
there be in <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span>;<br />
and, as many times as <span data-ref="0028.g1:C">$C$</span> measures <span data-ref="0028.g1:B">$B$</span>, so many units let there<br />
be in <span data-ref="0028.g1:E">$E$</span>.

<p class="sub-clause">Since then <span data-ref="0028.g1:C">$C$</span> measures <span data-ref="0028.g1:A">$A$</span> according to the units in <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span>,<br />
while the unit also measures <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span> according to the units in it,</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore the unit measures the number <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span> the same number<br />
of times as the magnitude <span data-ref="0028.g1:C">$C$</span> measures <span data-ref="0028.g1:A">$A$</span>;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore, as <span data-ref="0028.g1:C">$C$</span> is to <span data-ref="0028.g1:A">$A$</span>, so is the unit to <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span>; <span class="justification">[VII. Def. 20]</span></p>
<p class="assertion">therefore, inversely, as <span data-ref="0028.g1:A">$A$</span> is to <span data-ref="0028.g1:C">$C$</span>, so is <span data-ref="0028.g1:D">$D$</span> to the unit.<br />
