ratio duplicate of $m : n$.

Then, in order to establish his result, Euclid assumes that, *if two ratios are<br />
equal, the ratios which are their duplicates are also equal*. This is nowhere<br />
proved in Euclid, but it is an easy inference from V. 22, as shown in my note<br />
on VI. 22.

The converse has to be established in the same careful way, and Euclid<br />
assumes that *ratios the duplicates of which are equal are themselves equal*.<br />
This is much more troublesome to prove than the converse ; for proofs I refer<br />
to the same note on VI. 22.

The second part of the theorem, deduced by *reductio ad absurdum* from<br />
the first, requires no remark.

In the Greek text there is an addition to the Porism which Heiberg<br />
brackets as superfluous and not in Euclid’s manner. It consists (1) of a sort<br />
of proof, or rather explanation, of the Porism and (2) of a statement and<br />
explanation to the effect that straight lines incommensurable in length are<br />
not necessarily incommensurable in square also, and that straight lines<br />
incommensurable in square are, on the other hand, always incommensurable<br />
in length also.

The Lemma gives expressions for two numbers which have to one another<br />
the ratio of a square number to a square number. *Similar plane numbers*<br />
are of the form $pm \cdot pn$ and $qm \cdot qn$, or $mnp^2$ and $mnq^2$, the ratio of which is<br />
of course the ratio of $p^2$ to $q^2$.

The converse theorem that, if two numbers have to one another the ratio<br />
of a square number to a square number, the numbers are similar plane<br />
numbers is not, as a matter of fact, proved in the arithmetical Books. It is<br />
the converse of VIII. 26 and is used in IX. 10. Heron gave it (see note on<br />
VIII. 27 above).

Heiberg however gives strong reason for supposing the Lemma to be an<br />
interpolation. It has reference to the next proposition, X. 10, and, as we shall<br />
see, there are so many objections to X. 10 that it can hardly be accepted as<br />
genuine. Moreover there is no reason why, in the Lemma itself, numbers<br />
which are *not* similar plane numbers should be brought in as they are.
