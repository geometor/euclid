It would appear as though this proposition was intended to supply a<br />
justification for the statement in X. Def. 3 that it is *proved* that there are an<br />
infinite number of straight lines ($a$) incommensurable in length only, or<br />
commensurable in square only, and ($b$) incommensurable in square, with any<br />
given straight line.

But in truth the proposition could well be dispensed with ; and the<br />
positive objections to its genuineness are considerable.

In the first place, it depends on the following proposition, X. 11 ; for the<br />
last step concludes that, since
$$a^2 : y^2 = a : x,$$
and $a$, $x$ are incommensurable in length, therefore $a^2$, $y^2$ are incommensurable.<br />
But Euclid never commits the irregularity of proving a theorem by means of<br />
a later one. Gregory sought to get over the difficulty by putting X. 10 after<br />
X. 11 ; but of course, if the order were so inverted, the Lemma would still be<br />
in the wrong place.
