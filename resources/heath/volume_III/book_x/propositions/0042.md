**3, 5, 8, 10.** Euclid speaks of the square on the first (third) being greater than the square<br />
on the second (fourth) by the square on a straight line commensurable (incommensurable)<br />
"with *itself* ($\acute{\epsilon}\alpha\upsilon\tau\tilde{\eta}$)," and similarly in all like phrases throughout the Book. For clearness'<br />
sake I substitute "the first," "the third," or whatever it may be, for "*itself*" in these cases.

Suppose $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ to be straight lines such that
$$a : b = c : d \tag{1}$$
It follows [vi. 22] that
$$a^2 : b^2 = c^2 : d^2 \tag{2}$$
In order to prove that, *convertendo*,
$$a^2 : (a^2 - b^2) = c^2 : (c^2 - d^2)$$
Euclid has to use a somewhat roundabout method owing to the absence of a<br />
*convertendo* proposition in his Book v. (which omission Simson supplied by<br />
his Prop. E).

It follows from (2) that
$$\{(a^2 - b^2) + b^2\} : b^2 = \{(c^2 - d^2) + d^2\} : d^2,$$
whence, *separando*,
$$(a^2 - b^2) : b^2 = (c^2 - d^2) : d^2, \tag{v. 17}$$
and, inversely,
$$b^2 : (a^2 - b^2) = d^2 : (c^2 - d^2).$$
From this and (2), *ex aequali*,
$$a^2 : (a^2 - b^2) = c^2 : (c^2 - d^2). \tag{v. 22}$$
Hence
$$a : \sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = c : \sqrt{c^2 - d^2}. \tag{vi. 22}$$
According therefore as
$$\begin{aligned}
a &⌢\text{ or } ⌣ \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}, \\
c &⌢\text{ or } ⌣ \sqrt{c^2 - d^2}. &&\text{[x. 11]}
\end{aligned}$$

If $a ⌢ \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$, we may put $\sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = ka$, where $k$ is of the form $m/n$<br />
and $m$, $n$ are integers. And if $\sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = ka$, it follows in this case that<br />
$\sqrt{c^2 - d^2} = kc$.
