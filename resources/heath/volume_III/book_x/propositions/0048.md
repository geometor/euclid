On the other hand, on the assumption that the *numerical* solution of quadratic<br />
equations was an important part of the system of the Greek geometers,<br />
the distinction between the cases where the roots are commensurable and<br />
incommensurable respectively with a given length or unit becomes of great<br />
importance. Since the Greeks had no means of *expressing* what we call an<br />
irrational number, the case of an equation with incommensurable roots could<br />
*only* be represented by them geometrically; and the geometrical representations<br />
had to serve instead of what we can express by formulae involving surds.

Euclid proves in this proposition and the next that, $x$ being determined<br />
from the equation
$$x(a - x) = \frac{b^2}{4} \dots\dots\dots\dots\dots\dots\dots\dots\dots\dots (1),$$
$x$, $(a - x)$ are commensurable in length when $\sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$, $a$ are so, and incom-<br />
mensurable in length when $\sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$, $a$ are incommensurable; and conversely.

Observe the similarity of his proof to our algebraical method of solving<br />
the equation. $a$ being represented in the figure by <span data-ref="0046.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>, and $x$ by <span data-ref="0046.g1:/ D C /">$CD$</span>,
$$EF = ED = \frac{a}{2} - x$$
and
$$x(a - x) + \left(\frac{a}{2} - x\right)^2 = \frac{a^2}{4}, \qquad \text{by Eucl. II. 5.}$$

If we multiply throughout by 4,
$$4x(a - x) + 4\left(\frac{a}{2} - x\right)^2 = a^2,$$
whence, by (1),
$$b^2 + (a - 2x)^2 = a^2,$$
or
$$a^2 - b^2 = (a - 2x)^2,$$
and
$$\sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = a - 2x.$$

We have to prove in this proposition<br />
(1) that, if $x$, $(a - x)$ are commensurable in length, so are $a$, $\sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$,<br />
(2) that, if $a$, $\sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$ are commensurable in length, so are $x$, $(a - x)$.

(1) To prove that $a$, $a - 2x$ are commensurable in length Euclid employs<br />
several successive steps, thus.
