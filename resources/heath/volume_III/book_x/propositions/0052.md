There is a difficulty in the text of the enunciation of this proposition.<br />
The Greek runs τὸ ὑπὸ ῥητῶν μήκει συμμέτρων κατά τινα τῶν προειρημένων<br />
τρόπων εὐθειῶν περιεχόμενον ὀρθογώνιον ῥητόν ἐστιν, where the rectangle is<br />
said to be contained by "rational straight lines commensurable in length *in<br />
any of the aforesaid ways*." Now straight lines can only be commensurable<br />
in length *in one way*, the degrees of commensurability being commensurability<br />
in length and commensurability in square only. But a straight line may be<br />
rational *in two ways* in relation to a *given* rational straight line, since it may<br />
be either *commensurable in length*, or *commensurable in square only*, with the<br />
latter. Hence Billingsley takes κατά τινα τῶν προειρημένων τρόπων with ῥητῶν,<br />
translating "straight lines commensurable in length and rational in any of the<br />
aforesaid ways," and this agrees with the expression in the next proposition<br />
"a straight line once more rational in any of the aforesaid ways"; but the<br />
order of words in the Greek seems to be fatal to this way of translating<br />
the passage.

The best solution of the difficulty seems to be to reject the words "in<br />
any of the aforesaid ways" altogether. They have reference to the Lemma<br />
which immediately precedes and which is itself open to the gravest suspicion.<br />
It is very prolix, and cannot be called necessary; it appears moreover in<br />
connexion with an addition clearly spurious and therefore relegated by<br />
Heiberg to the Appendix. The addition does not even pretend to be Euclid's,<br />
for it begins with the words "for *he calls* rational straight lines those...."<br />
Hence we should no doubt relegate the Lemma itself to the Appendix.<br />
August does so and leaves out the suspected words in the enunciation, as I<br />
have done.

Exactly the same arguments apply to the Lemma added (without the<br />
heading "Lemma") to X. 23 and the same words "in any of the aforesaid<br />
ways" used with "medial straight lines commensurable in length" in the<br />
enunciation of X. 24. The said Lemma must stand or fall with that now in<br />
question, since it refers to it in terms: "And in the same way as was explained<br />
in the case of rationals...."

Hence I have bracketed the Lemma added to X. 23 and left out the<br />
objectionable words in the enunciation of X. 24.

If $\rho$ be one of the given rational straight lines (rational of course in the<br />
sense of X. Def. 3), the other can be denoted by $k\rho$, where $k$ is, as usual, of<br />
the form $m/n$ (where $m, n$ are integers). Thus the rectangle is $k\rho^2$, which is<br />
obviously rational since it is commensurable with $\rho^2$. <span class="justification">[X. Def. 4.]</span>

A rational rectangle may have any of the forms $ab$, $ka^2$, $kA$ or $A$, where<br />
$a, b$ are commensurable with the unit of length, and $A$ with the unit of area.

Since Euclid is not able to use $k\rho$ as a symbol for a straight line<br />
commensurable in length with $\rho$, he has to put his proof in a form corre-<br />
sponding to
$$\rho^2 : k\rho^2 = \rho : k\rho,$$
whence, $\rho, k\rho$ being commensurable, $\rho^2, k\rho^2$ are so also. <span class="justification">[X. 11]</span>
