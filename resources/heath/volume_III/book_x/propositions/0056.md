Our algebraical notation makes the result of this proposition almost self-<br />
evident. We have seen that the square of a medial straight line is of the form<br />
$\sqrt{k} \cdot \rho^2$. If we "apply" this area to another rational straight line $\sigma$, the<br />
breadth is $\frac{\sqrt{k} \cdot \rho^2}{\sigma}$.

This is equal to $\frac{\sqrt{k} \cdot \rho^2}{\sigma^2} \cdot \sigma = \sqrt{k} \cdot \frac{m}{n} \sigma$, where $m$, $n$ are integers. The latter<br />
straight line, which we may express, if we please, in the form $\sqrt{k'} \cdot \sigma$, is clearly<br />
commensurable with $\sigma$ in square only, and therefore rational but incom-<br />
mensurable in length with $\sigma$.

Euclid's proof, necessarily longer, is in two parts.

Suppose that the rectangle $\sqrt{k} \cdot \rho^2 = \sigma \cdot x$.

Then (1)
$$\sigma : \rho = \sqrt{k} \cdot \rho : x, \qquad \text{[VI. 14]}$$
whence
$$\sigma^2 : \rho^2 = k\rho^2 : x^2. \qquad \text{[VI. 22]}$$
But $\sigma^2 \sim \rho^2$, and therefore $k\rho^2 \sim x^2$. [x. 11]
