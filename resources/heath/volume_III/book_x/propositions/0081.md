Euclid takes the straight lines found in x. 30, viz. $\rho$, $\dfrac{\rho}{\sqrt{1+k^2}}$.

He then solves geometrically the equations
$$\left.\begin{aligned}
x + y &= \rho \\
xy &= \frac{\rho^2}{4(1+k^2)}
\end{aligned}\right\} \text{ .........................................(1).}$$

If $x, y$ are the values found, he takes $u, v$ such that
$$\left.\begin{aligned}
u^2 &= \rho x \\
v^2 &= \rho y
\end{aligned}\right\} \text{ .........................................(2),}$$
and $u, v$ are straight lines satisfying the conditions of the problem.

Solving algebraically, we get (if $x > y$)
$$x = \frac{\rho}{2}\left(1 + \frac{k}{\sqrt{1+k^2}}\right),\quad y = \frac{\rho}{2}\left(1 - \frac{k}{\sqrt{1+k^2}}\right),$$
whence
$$\left.\begin{aligned}
u &= \frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{1 + \frac{k}{\sqrt{1+k^2}}} \\
v &= \frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{1 - \frac{k}{\sqrt{1+k^2}}}
\end{aligned}\right\} \text{ .........................................(3).}$$

Euclid's proof that these straight lines fulfil the requirements is as follows.

($\alpha$) The constants in the equations (1) satisfy the conditions of x. 18 ;<br />
therefore
$$x \mathbin{\smile} y.$$
But $x : y = u^2 : v^2$.<br />
Therefore
$$u^2 \mathbin{\smile} v^2,$$
and $u, v$ are thus *incommensurable in square*.

($\beta$) $u^2 + v^2 = \rho^2$, which is *rational*.

($\gamma$) By (1),
$$\sqrt{xy} = \frac{\rho}{2\sqrt{1+k^2}}.$$
By (2),
$$\begin{aligned}
uv &= \rho\ .\ \sqrt{xy} \\
&= \frac{\rho^2}{2\sqrt{1+k^2}}.
\end{aligned}$$
