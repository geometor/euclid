<p class="sub-clause">And <span data-ref="0099.g1:/ E H /">$EH$</span> is not the same with <span data-ref="0099.g1:/ M N /">$MN$</span>.</p>

<p class="sub-clause">For the squares on <span data-ref="0099.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0099.g1:/ C B /">$CB$</span> are greater than the squares<br />

<p class="sub-clause">But the squares on <span data-ref="0099.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span>, <span data-ref="0099.g1:/ D B /">$DB$</span> are greater than twice the<br />

<p class="assertion">therefore also the squares on <span data-ref="0099.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0099.g1:/ C B /">$CB$</span>, that is, <span data-ref="0099.g1:< E G >">$EG$</span>, are much<br />

As the irrationality of the *second bimedial* straight line [x. 38] is proved by<br />
means of the irrationality of the binomial straight line [x. 36], so the present<br />
theorem is reduced to that of x. 42.

Suppose, if possible, that the second bimedial straight line can be divided<br />
into its terms as such in two ways, i.e. that
$$x + y = x' + y',$$
where $x', y'$ are nearer equality than $x, y$.

Apply $x^2 + y^2$, $2xy$ to a rational straight line $\sigma$, i.e. let
$$\begin{aligned}
x^2 + y^2 &= \sigma u, \\
2xy &= \sigma v.
\end{aligned}$$

Then, as in x. 38, the areas $x^2 + y^2$, $2xy$ are medial, so that $\sigma u, \sigma v$ are<br />
medial ;<br />
therefore $u, v$ are both rational and $\smile \sigma$ .....................................................(1).

Again, by hypothesis, $x, y$ are medial straight lines commensurable in<br />
square only;
therefore
$$x \smile y.$$

Hence
$$x^2 \smile xy.$$

And $x^2 \sim (x^2 + y^2)$, while $xy \sim 2xy$;
therefore
$$(x^2 + y^2) \smile 2xy,$$
or
$$\sigma u \smile \sigma v,$$
and hence
$$u \smile v$$ .................................................................(2).

Therefore, by (1) and (2), $u, v$ are rational straight lines commensurable<br />
in square only;<br />
therefore $u + v$ is a binomial straight line.

Similarly, if $x'^2 + y'^2 = \sigma u'$ and $2x'y' = \sigma v'$,<br />
$u' + v'$ will be proved to be a binomial straight line.

And, since $(x + y)^2 = (x' + y')^2$, and therefore $(u + v) = (u' + v')$, it follows that<br />
a binomial straight line is divided as such in two ways :<br />
which is impossible. [x. 42]

Therefore $x + y$, the given second bimedial straight line, can only be so<br />
divided in one way.

In order to prove that $u + v$, $u' + v'$ represent a *different* division of the<br />
same straight line, Euclid assumes that $x^2 + y^2 > 2xy$. This is of course an<br />
easy inference from II. 7; but the assumption of it here renders it probable<br />
that the Lemma after x. 59 is interpolated.
