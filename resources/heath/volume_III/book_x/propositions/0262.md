(4) *The major made up of three straight lines.*

The commentator describes this as “the line composed of three straight<br />
lines incommensurable in square and such that one of them gives with each<br />
of the other two a sum of squares (which is) rational, while the rectangle<br />
contained by the two lines is medial.”<br />
If $x$, $y$, $z$ are the three straight lines, this would indicate
$$\begin{aligned}
(x^2 + y^2) &\text{ rational,} \\
(x^2 + z^2) &\text{ rational,} \\
2yz &\text{ medial.}
\end{aligned}$$
Woepcke points out (pp. 696—8, note) the difficulties connected with this<br />
supposition or the supposition of
$$\begin{aligned}
(x^2 + y^2) &\text{ rational,} \\
(x^2 + z^2) &\text{ rational,} \\
2xy \text{ (or } 2xz) &\text{ medial,}
\end{aligned}$$
and concludes that what is meant is the supposition
$$\left. \begin{aligned}
(x^2 + y^2) &\text{ rational} \\
xy &\text{ medial} \\
xz &\text{ medial}
\end{aligned} \right\}$$
(though the text is against this).<br />
The assumption of $(x^2 + y^2)$ and $(x^2 + z^2)$ being concurrently rational is<br />
certainly further removed from Euclid, for X. 33 only enables us to find *one<br />
pair* of lines having the property, as $x$, $y$.<br />
But we will not pursue these speculations further.
