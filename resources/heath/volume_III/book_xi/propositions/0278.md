"And, because the points <span data-ref="0276.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0276.g1:C">$C$</span> are in this plane, the straight line <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> is<br />
in it. <span class="justification">[Simson's def.]</span>

<p class="assertion">Therefore there are two straight lines <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B C /">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B D /">$ABD$</span> in the same plane that<br />
have a common segment <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>:</p>
<p class="assertion">which is impossible."</p>

Simson, of course, justifies the last inference by reference to his Corollary<br />
to I. 11, which, however, as we have seen, is not a valid proof of the assump-<br />
tion, which is really implied in I. Post. 2.<br />
An alternative reading, perhaps due to Theon, says, after the words<br />
"which is impossible" in the Greek text, "for a straight line does not meet a<br />
straight line in more points than one; otherwise the straight lines will<br />
coincide." Simson (who however does not seem to have had the second<br />
clause beginning "otherwise" in the text which he used) attacks this alterna-<br />
tive reading in a rather confused note chiefly directed against a criticism by<br />
Thomas Simpson, without (as it seems to me) sufficient reason. It contains<br />
surely a legitimate argument. The supposed straight lines <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B C /">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B D /">$ABD$</span> meet<br />
in more than two points, namely in all the points between <span data-ref="0276.g1:A">$A$</span> and <span data-ref="0276.g1:B">$B$</span>. But two<br />
straight lines cannot have two points common without coinciding altogether;<br />
therefore <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B C /">$ABC$</span> must coincide with <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B D /">$ABD$</span>.

### PROPOSITION 2.

<p class="enunciation">If two straight lines cut one another, they are in one plane,<br />
and every triangle is in one plane.</p>

For let the two straight lines <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> cut one another at<br />
the point <span data-ref="0278.g1:E">$E$</span>;<br />
I say that <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> are in one plane,<br />
and every triangle is in one plane.

![Book XI, Proposition 2](0278.g1.svg)

For let points <span data-ref="0278.g1:F">$F$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:G">$G$</span> be taken at<br />
random on <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E C /">$EC$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span>,<br />
let <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ C B /">$CB$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span> be joined,<br />
and let <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ F H /">$FH$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ G K /">$GK$</span> be drawn across;<br />
I say first that the triangle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< E C B >">$ECB$</span> is<br />
in one plane.

<p class="sub-clause">For, if part of the triangle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< E C B >">$ECB$</span>,<br />
either <span data-ref="0278.g1:< F H C >">$FHC$</span> or <span data-ref="0278.g1:< G B K >">$GBK$</span>, is in the plane of reference, and the rest<br />
in another,</p>
<p class="assertion">a part also of one of the straight lines <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E C /">$EC$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span> will be in the<br />
plane of reference, and a part in another.</p>

<p class="sub-clause">But, if the part <span data-ref="0278.g1:< F C B G >">$FCBG$</span> of the triangle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< E C B >">$ECB$</span> be in the<br />
plane of reference, and the rest in another,</p>
<p class="assertion">a part also of both the straight lines <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E C /">$EC$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span> will be in the<br />
plane of reference and a part in another:</p>
<p class="assertion">which was proved absurd. <span class="justification">[XI. 1]</span></p>
