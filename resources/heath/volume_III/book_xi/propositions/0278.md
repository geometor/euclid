"And, because the points <span data-ref="0276.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0276.g1:C">$C$</span> are in this plane, the straight line <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> is in it. [Simson's def.]

"Therefore there are two straight lines <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B C /">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B D /">$ABD$</span> in the same plane that have a common segment <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>:
which is impossible."

Simson, of course, justifies the last inference by reference to his Corollary to I. 11, which, however, as we have seen, is not a valid proof of the assumption, which is really implied in I. Post. 2.

An alternative reading, perhaps due to Theon, says, after the words "which is impossible" in the Greek text, "for a straight line does not meet a straight line in more points than one; otherwise the straight lines will coincide." Simson (who however does not seem to have had the second clause beginning "otherwise" in the text which he used) attacks this alternative reading in a rather confused note chiefly directed against a criticism by Thomas Simpson, without (as it seems to me) sufficient reason. It contains surely a legitimate argument. The supposed straight lines <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B C /">$ABC$</span>, <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B D /">$ABD$</span> meet in more than two points, namely in all the points between <span data-ref="0276.g1:A">$A$</span> and <span data-ref="0276.g1:B">$B$</span>. But two straight lines cannot have two points common without coinciding altogether; therefore <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B C /">$ABC$</span> must coincide with <span data-ref="0276.g1:/ A B D /">$ABD$</span>.

### PROPOSITION 2.

*If two straight lines cut one another, they are in one plane, and every triangle is in one plane.*

For let the two straight lines <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> cut one another at the point <span data-ref="0278.g1:E">$E$</span>;
I say that <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> are in one plane,
and every triangle is in one plane.

For let points <span data-ref="0278.g1:F">$F$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:G">$G$</span> be taken at random on <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E C /">$EC$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span>,
let <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ C B /">$CB$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span> be joined,
and let <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ F H /">$FH$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ G K /">$GK$</span> be drawn across;
I say first that the triangle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< E C B >">$ECB$</span> is in one plane.

For, if part of the triangle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< E C B >">$ECB$</span>, either <span data-ref="0278.g1:< F H C >">$FHC$</span> or <span data-ref="0278.g1:< G B K >">$GBK$</span>, is in the plane of reference, and the rest in another,
a part also of one of the straight lines <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E C /">$EC$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span> will be in the plane of reference, and a part in another.

But, if the part <span data-ref="0278.g1:< F C B G >">$FCBG$</span> of the triangle <span data-ref="0278.g1:< E C B >">$ECB$</span> be in the plane of reference, and the rest in another,
a part also of both the straight lines <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E C /">$EC$</span>, <span data-ref="0278.g1:/ E B /">$EB$</span> will be in the plane of reference and a part in another:
which was proved absurd. [XI. 1]
