<p class="sub-clause">But the angle <span data-ref="0287.g1:< A B E )">$ABE$</span> is right;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore the angle <span data-ref="0287.g1:< E D A )">$EDA$</span> is also right;<br />

<p class="sub-clause">But it is also at right angles to each of the straight lines<br />
<span data-ref="0287.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span>;</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ E D /">$ED$</span> is set up at right angles to the three straight<br />

therefore the three straight lines <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D A /">$DA$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span> are in one<br />

<p class="sub-clause">But, in whatever plane <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D B /">$DB$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D A /">$DA$</span> are, in that plane is <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span><br />

<p class="assertion">therefore the straight lines <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span> are in one plane.</p>

If anyone wishes to convince himself of the real necessity for some<br />
general agreement as to the order in which propositions in elementary<br />
geometry should be taken, let him contemplate the hopeless result of too<br />
much independence on the part of editors in the matter of this proposition<br />
and its converse, XI. 8.

Legendre adopts a different, and elegant, method of proof; but he applies<br />
it to XI. 8, which he gives first, and then deduces XI. 6 from it by *reductio ad*<br />
*absurdum*. Dr Mehler uses Legendre's method of proof but applies it to<br />
XI. 6, and then gives XI. 8 as a deduction from it. Lardner follows Legendre.<br />
Holgate, the editor of a recent American book, gives Euclid's proof of XI. 6<br />
and deduces XI. 8 by *reductio ad absurdum*. His countrymen, Schultze and<br />
Sevenoak, give XI. 8 first, but put it after, and deduce it from, Eucl. XI. 10;<br />
they then give XI. 6, practically as a deduction from XI. 8 by *reductio ad*<br />
*absurdum*, after a proposition corresponding to Eucl. XI. 11 and 12, and a<br />
corollary to the effect that through a given point one and only one perpen-<br />
dicular can be drawn to a given plane.

We will now give the proof of XI. 6 by Legendre's method (adopted by<br />
Smith and Bryant as well as by Mehler).

Let <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> be both perpendicular to the<br />
same plane <span data-ref="0288.g1:< M N >">$MN$</span>.

Join <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>.

Now, since <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span> meets <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>, both of<br />
which are perpendicular to the plane <span data-ref="0288.g1:< M N >">$MN$</span> in<br />
which <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span> is,<br />
the angles <span data-ref="0288.g1:< A B D )">$ABD$</span>, <span data-ref="0288.g1:< C D B )">$CDB$</span> are right angles.

<span data-ref="0288.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> will therefore be parallel *provided<br />
that they are in the same plane*.
