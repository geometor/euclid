But the angle <span data-ref="0287.g1:< A B E )">$ABE$</span> is right;
therefore the angle <span data-ref="0287.g1:< E D A )">$EDA$</span> is also right;
therefore <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ E D /">$ED$</span> is at right angles to <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D A /">$DA$</span>.

But it is also at right angles to each of the straight lines <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span>;
therefore <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ E D /">$ED$</span> is set up at right angles to the three straight lines <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D A /">$DA$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span> at their point of meeting;
therefore the three straight lines <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D A /">$DA$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span> are in one plane.            [XI. 5]

But, in whatever plane <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D B /">$DB$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D A /">$DA$</span> are, in that plane is <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> also,
for every triangle is in one plane;            [XI. 2]
therefore the straight lines <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ D C /">$DC$</span> are in one plane.

And each of the angles <span data-ref="0287.g1:< A B D )">$ABD$</span>, <span data-ref="0287.g1:< B D C )">$BDC$</span> is right;
therefore <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is parallel to <span data-ref="0287.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>.            [I. 28]

Therefore etc.

Q. E. D.

If anyone wishes to convince himself of the real necessity for some general agreement as to the order in which propositions in elementary geometry should be taken, let him contemplate the hopeless result of too much independence on the part of editors in the matter of this proposition and its converse, XI. 8.

Legendre adopts a different, and elegant, method of proof; but he applies it to XI. 8, which he gives first, and then deduces XI. 6 from it by *reductio ad absurdum*. Dr Mehler uses Legendre's method of proof but applies it to XI. 6, and then gives XI. 8 as a deduction from it. Lardner follows Legendre. Holgate, the editor of a recent American book, gives Euclid's proof of XI. 6 and deduces XI. 8 by *reductio ad absurdum*. His countrymen, Schultze and Sevenoak, give XI. 8 first, but put it after, and deduce it from, Eucl. XI. 10; they then give XI. 6, practically as a deduction from XI. 8 by *reductio ad absurdum*, after a proposition corresponding to Eucl. XI. 11 and 12, and a corollary to the effect that through a given point one and only one perpendicular can be drawn to a given plane.

We will now give the proof of XI. 6 by Legendre's method (adopted by Smith and Bryant as well as by Mehler).

Let <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> be both perpendicular to the same plane <span data-ref="0288.g1:< M N >">$MN$</span>.

Join <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>.

Now, since <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span> meets <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>, both of which are perpendicular to the plane <span data-ref="0288.g1:< M N >">$MN$</span> in which <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span> is,
the angles <span data-ref="0288.g1:< A B D )">$ABD$</span>, <span data-ref="0288.g1:< C D B )">$CDB$</span> are right angles.

<span data-ref="0288.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0288.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span> will therefore be parallel *provided that they are in the same plane*.
