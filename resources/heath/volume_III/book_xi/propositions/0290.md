It is true that this proposition, in the form in which Euclid enunciates it,<br />
is hardly necessary if the plane is defined as a surface such that, if any two<br />
points be taken in it, the straight line joining them lies wholly in the surface.<br />
But Euclid did not give this definition; and, moreover, Prop. 2 would be<br />
usefully supplemented by a proposition which should prove that *two parallel<br />
straight lines determine a plane* (i.e. *one plane and one only*) *which also<br />
contains all the straight lines which join a point on one of the parallels to a point<br />
on the other*. That there cannot be *two* planes through a pair of parallels<br />
would be proved in the same way as we prove that two or three intersecting<br />
straight lines cannot be in two different planes, inasmuch as each transversal<br />
lying in one of the two supposed planes through the parallels would lie wholly<br />
in the other also, so that the two supposed planes must coincide throughout<br />
(cf. note on Prop. 2 above).

But, whatever be the value of the proposition as it is, Simson seems to<br />
have spoilt it completely. He leaves out the construction of a plane through<br />
<span data-ref="0289.g1:( E G F )">$EGF$</span>, which, as Euclid says, must cut the plane containing the parallels in<br />
a straight line; and, instead, he says, “In the plane <span data-ref="0289.g1:< A B C D >">$ABCD$</span> in which the<br />
parallels are draw the straight line <span data-ref="0289.g1:/ E H F /">$EHF$</span> from <span data-ref="0289.g1:E">$E$</span> to <span data-ref="0289.g1:F">$F$</span>.” Now, although we<br />
can easily draw a straight line from <span data-ref="0289.g1:E">$E$</span> to <span data-ref="0289.g1:F">$F$</span>, to claim that we can draw it *in<br />
the plane in which the parallels are* is surely to assume the very result which is<br />
to be proved. All that we could properly say is that the straight line joining<br />
<span data-ref="0289.g1:E">$E$</span> to <span data-ref="0289.g1:F">$F$</span> is in *some* plane which contains the parallels; we do not know that<br />
there is no more than *one* such plane, or that the parallels determine a plane<br />
*uniquely*, without some such argument as that which Euclid gives.

Nor can I subscribe to the remarks in Simson’s note on the proposition.<br />
He says (1) “This proposition has been put into this book by some unskilful<br />
editor, as is evident from this, that straight lines which are drawn from one<br />
point to another in a plane are, in the preceding books, supposed to be in that<br />
plane; and if they were not, some demonstrations in which one straight line<br />
is supposed to meet another would not be conclusive. For instance, in<br />
Prop. 30, Book I., the straight line <span data-ref="0289.g1:/ G K /">$GK$</span> would not meet <span data-ref="0289.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span>, if <span data-ref="0289.g1:/ G K /">$GK$</span> were not in<br />
the plane in which are the parallels <span data-ref="0289.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0289.g1:/ C D /">$CD$</span>, and in which, by hypothesis, the<br />
straight line <span data-ref="0289.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> is.” But the subject-matter of Book I. and Book XI. is quite<br />
different; in Book I. everything is in one plane, and when Euclid, in defining<br />
parallels, says they are straight lines *in the same plane* etc., he only does so<br />
because he must, in order to exclude non-intersecting straight lines which are<br />
*not* parallel. Thus in I. 30 there is nothing wrong in assuming that there may<br />
be three parallels in one plane, and that the straight line <span data-ref="0289.g1:/ G H K /">$GHK$</span> cuts all three.
