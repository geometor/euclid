Rausenberger's construction for the same problem is more elegant. Draw, he says, through each straight line a plane parallel to the other. Then draw through each straight line a plane perpendicular to the plane through the other. The two planes last drawn will intersect in a straight line, and this straight line is the common perpendicular required.

![Book XI, Proposition 19, Rausenberger's Construction Diagram](0311.g1.svg)

The form of the construction best suited for examination purposes, because the most self-contained, is doubtless that given by Smith and Bryant.

Let <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> be the two given straight lines.

Through any point <span data-ref="0311.g2:E">$E$</span> in <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> draw <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ E F /">$EF$</span> parallel to <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>.

From any point <span data-ref="0311.g2:G">$G$</span> in <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span> draw <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ G H /">$GH$</span> perpendicular to the plane <span data-ref="0311.g2:< C D F >">$CDF$</span>, meeting the plane in <span data-ref="0311.g2:H">$H$</span>.

Through <span data-ref="0311.g2:H">$H$</span> in the plane <span data-ref="0311.g2:< C D F >">$CDF$</span> draw <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ H K /">$HK$</span> parallel to <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ F E /">$FE$</span> or <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, to cut <span data-ref="0311.g2:/ C D /">$CD$</span> in <span data-ref="0311.g2:K">$K$</span>.
