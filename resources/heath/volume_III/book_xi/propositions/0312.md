After excluding the obvious case in which all three angles are equal,<br />
Euclid goes on to say “If not, let the angle <span data-ref="0311.g3:< B A C )">$BAC$</span> be greater,” without adding<br />
greater than *what*. Heiberg is clearly right in saying that he means greater<br />
than <span data-ref="0311.g3:< B A D )">$BAD$</span>, i.e. greater than *one* of the adjacent angles. This is proved by<br />
the words at the end “Similarly we can prove,” etc. Euclid thus excludes<br />
as obvious the case where one of the three angles is not greater than either of<br />
the other two, but proves the remaining cases. This is scientific, but he might<br />
further have excluded as obvious the case in which one angle is greater than<br />
one of the others but equal to or less than the remaining one.
