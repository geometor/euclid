The Greek text gives an alternative proof, which is relegated by Heiberg<br />
to the Appendix. Simson selected the alternative proof in preference to that<br />
given above; he objected however to words near the beginning, “If not, let<br />
the angles at the points <span data-ref="0317.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0317.g1:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0317.g1:H">$H$</span> be unequal and that at <span data-ref="0317.g1:B">$B$</span> greater than either<br />
of the angles at <span data-ref="0317.g1:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0317.g1:H">$H$</span>,” and altered the words so as to take account of the<br />
possibility that the angle at <span data-ref="0317.g1:B">$B$</span> might be equal to one of the other two.

As will be seen, Euclid takes no account of the relative magnitude of the<br />
angles except as regards the case when all three are equal. Having proved<br />
that *one* base is less than the sum of the two others, he says that “similarly<br />
we can prove” the same thing for the other two bases.

If a distinction is to be made according to the relative magnitude of the<br />
three angles, we may say, as in the corresponding place in XI. 21, that, if one<br />
of the three angles is either equal to or less than *either* of the other two, the<br />
bases subtending those two angles must obviously be together greater than the<br />
base subtending the first. Thus it is only necessary to prove, for the case in<br />
which one angle is *greater than either* of the others, that the sum of the bases<br />
subtending those others is greater than that subtending the first. This is<br />
practically the course taken in the interpolated alternative proof.

### PROPOSITION 23.

<p class="enunciation">To construct a solid angle out of three plane angles two of<br />
which, taken together in any manner, are greater than the<br />
remaining one: thus the three angles must be less than four<br />
right angles.</p>
