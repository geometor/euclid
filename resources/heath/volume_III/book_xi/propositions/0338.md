himself the two other possible cases shown in the subjoined figures. Euclid’s<br />
proof holds with a very slight change in each case. With the first figure, the

Simson, as usual, suspects mutilation by “some unskilful editor,” but gives<br />
a curious reason why the case in which the two parallelograms opposite to<br />
<span data-ref="0338.g1:< A B >">$AB$</span> have a side common ought not to have been omitted, namely that this<br />
case “is immediately deduced from the preceding 28th Prop which seems for<br />
this purpose to have been premised to the 29th.” But, apart from the fact that<br />
Euclid’s Prop. 28 does *not* prove the theorem which it enunciates (as we have<br />
seen), that theorem is not in the least necessary for the proof of this case of<br />
Prop. 29, as Euclid’s proof applies to it perfectly well.

### PROPOSITION 30.

<p class="enunciation">Parallelepipedal solids which are on the same base and of<br />
the same height, and in which the extremities of the sides which<br />
stand up are not on the same straight lines, are equal to one<br />
another.</p>
