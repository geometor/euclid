II. If the solids are proportional, take <span data-ref="0363.g1:/ S T /">$ST$</span> such that
$$AB : CD = EF : ST,$$
and on <span data-ref="0363.g1:/ S T /">$ST$</span> describe the parallelepiped <span data-ref="0363.g1:< S V >">$SV$</span> similar and similarly situated to
either of the parallelepipeds <span data-ref="0363.g1:< E M >">$EM$</span>, <span data-ref="0363.g1:< G N >">$GN$</span>.
    Then, by the first part,
$$\text{(solid } AK\text{)} : \text{(solid } CL\text{)} = \text{(solid } EM\text{)} : \text{(solid } SV\text{)},$$
whence it follows that
$$\text{(solid } GN\text{)} = \text{(solid } SV\text{)}.$$
    But these solids are similar and similarly situated;
therefore their faces are similar and equal; [XI. Def. 10]
therefore the corresponding sides <span data-ref="0363.g1:/ G H /">$GH$</span>, <span data-ref="0363.g1:/ S T /">$ST$</span> are equal.
    [For this inference cf. note on VI. 22. The equality of <span data-ref="0363.g1:/ G H /">$GH$</span>, <span data-ref="0363.g1:/ S T /">$ST$</span> may
readily be proved by application of the two parallelepipeds to one another,
since, being similar, they are equiangular.]
    Hence
$$AB : CD = EF : GH.$$

The text of the MSS. has here a proposition which is as badly placed as it
is unnecessary. *If a plane be at right angles to a plane, and from any one of the
points in one of the planes a perpendicular be drawn to the other plane, the
perpendicular so drawn will fall on the common section of the planes.* It is of
the nature of a lemma to XII. 17, where
alone the fact is made use of. Heiberg
observes that it is omitted in **b** and that the
copyist of **P** knew other texts which did not
contain it. From these facts it is fairly con-
cluded that the proposition was interpolated.
The truth of it is of course immediately
obvious by *reductio ad absurdum*. Let the plane <span data-ref="0364.g1:< C A D >">$CAD$</span> be perpendicular to
the plane <span data-ref="0364.g1:< A B >">$AB$</span>, and let a perpendicular be drawn to the latter from any point
<span data-ref="0364.g1:E">$E$</span> in the former.

![Interpolated lemma diagram](0364.g1.svg)

If it does not fall on <span data-ref="0364.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span>, the common section, let it meet the plane <span data-ref="0364.g1:< A B >">$AB$</span>
in <span data-ref="0364.g1:F">$F$</span>.

Draw <span data-ref="0364.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span> in <span data-ref="0364.g1:< A B >">$AB$</span> perpendicular to <span data-ref="0364.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span>, and join <span data-ref="0364.g1:/ E G /">$EG$</span>.

Then <span data-ref="0364.g1:/ F G /">$FG$</span> is perpendicular to the plane <span data-ref="0364.g1:< C A D >">$CAD$</span> [XI. Def. 4], and therefore
to <span data-ref="0364.g1:/ G E /">$GE$</span> [XI. Def. 3]. Therefore <span data-ref="0364.g1:< E G F )">$\angle EGF$</span> is right.

Also, since <span data-ref="0364.g1:/ E F /">$EF$</span> is perpendicular to <span data-ref="0364.g1:< A B >">$AB$</span>,
the angle <span data-ref="0364.g1:< E F G )">$EFG$</span> is right.

That is, the triangle <span data-ref="0364.g1:< E G F >">$EGF$</span> has two right angles:
which is impossible.
