We are not, however, to suppose that none of the *results* obtained by the method of exhaustion had been discovered before the time of :person:`Eudoxus <eudoxus>` (fl. about 368—5 B.C.). Two at least are of earlier date, those of Eucl. **xii.** 2 and **xii.** 7.

(*a*) :person:`Simplicius <simplicius>` (:cite:`*Comment. in Aristot. Phys.* p. 61 <simplicius:in-physica>`, ed. :person:`Diels <diels>`) quotes :person:`Eudemus <eudemus>` as saying, in his :cite:`*History of Geometry* <eudemus:history-of-geometry>`, that :person:`Hippocrates of Chios <hippocrates-of-chios>` (fl. say 430 B.C.) first laid it down (ἔθετο) that similar segments of circles are in the ratio of the squares on their bases and that he proved this (ἐδείκνυεν) by proving (ἐκ τοῦ δεῖξαι) that the squares on the diameters have the same ratio as the (whole) circles. We know nothing of the method by which :person:`Hippocrates <hippocrates-of-chios>` proved this proposition; but, having regard to the evidence from :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` quoted above, it is not permissible to suppose that the method was the fully developed :term:`*method of exhaustion* <method-of-exhaustion>` as we know it.

(*b*) As regards the two theorems about the volume of a pyramid and of a cone respectively, which :person:`Eudoxus <eudoxus>` was the first to prove, we now have authentic evidence in the short treatise by :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` discovered by :person:`Heiberg <heiberg>` in a MS. at Constantinople in 1906 and published in *Hermes* the following year (see now :cite:`*Archimedis opera omnia* <heiberg:archimedis-opera>`, ed. :person:`Heiberg <heiberg>`, 2. ed., Vol. II., 1913, pp. 425—507; T. L. Heath, :cite:`*The Method of Archimedes* <heath:method-of-archimedes>`, Cambridge, 1912). The said treatise, complete in all essentials, bears the title :cite:`Ἀρχιμήδους περὶ τῶν μηχανικῶν θεωρημάτων πρὸς Ἐρατοσθένην ἔφοδος <archimedes:methodus>`. This “Method” (or “Plan of attack”), addressed to :person:`Eratosthenes <eratosthenes>`, is none other than the :term:`ἐφόδιον <method>` on which, according to :person:`Suidas <suidas>`, :person:`Theodosius <theodosius>` wrote a commentary, and which is several times cited by :person:`Heron <heron>` in his :cite:`*Metrica* <heron:metrica>`; its discovery adds a new and important chapter to the history of the integral calculus. In the preface to this work :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` alludes to the theorems which he first discovered by means of mechanical considerations, but proved afterwards by geometry, because the investigation by means of mechanics did not constitute a *rigid* proof; he observes, however, that the mechanical method is of great use for the *discovery* of theorems, and it is much easier to provide the rigid proof when the fact to be proved has once been discovered than it would be if nothing were known to begin with. He goes on: “Hence too, in the case of those theorems the proof of which was first discovered by :person:`Eudoxus <eudoxus>`, namely those relating to the cone and the pyramid, that the cone is one third part of the cylinder, and the pyramid one third part of the prism, having the same base and equal height, no small part of the credit will naturally be assigned to :person:`Democritus <democritus>`, who was the first to make the statement (of the fact) regarding the said figure [i.e. property], though without proving it.” Hence the *discovery* of the two theorems must now be attributed to :person:`Democritus <democritus>` (fl. towards the end of 5th cent. B.C.). The words “without proving it” (χωρὶς ἀποδείξεως) do not mean that :person:`Democritus <democritus>` gave no sort of proof, but only that he did not give a proof on the rigorous lines required later; for the same words are used by :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` of his own investigations by means of mechanics, which, however, do constitute a reasoned argument. The character of :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>`’ mechanical arguments combined with a passage of :person:`Plutarch <plutarch>` about a particular question in infinitesimals said to have been raised by :person:`Democritus <democritus>` may perhaps give a clue to the line of :person:`Democritus <democritus>`’ argument as regards the pyramid.
