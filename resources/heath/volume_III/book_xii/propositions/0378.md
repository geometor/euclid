Though this theorem is said to have been proved by Hippocrates, we may<br />
with tolerable certainty attribute the proof of it given by Euclid to Eudoxus,<br />
to whom XII. 7 Por. and XII. 10 (which Euclid proves in exactly the same<br />
manner) are specifically attributed by Archimedes. As regards the lemma<br />
used herein (Eucl. X. 1) and the somewhat different lemma by means of which<br />
Archimedes says that the theorems of XII. 2, XII. 7 Por. and XII. 18 were<br />
proved, see my note on X. 1 above.

The first essential in this proposition is to prove that we can *exhaust* a<br />
circle, in the sense of X. 1, by successively inscribing in it regular polygons,<br />
each of which has twice as many sides as the preceding one. We take first<br />
an inscribed square, then bisect the arcs subtended by the sides and so form<br />
an equilateral polygon of eight sides, then do the same with the latter, forming<br />
a polygon of 16 sides, and so on. And we have to prove that what is left<br />
over when any one of these polygons is taken away from the circle is *more<br />
than half exhausted* when the next polygon is made and subtracted from the<br />
circle.

Euclid proves that the inscribed square is greater than half the circle and<br />
that the regular octagon when subtracted takes away more than half of what<br />
was left by the square. He then infers that the same<br />
thing will happen whenever the number of sides is<br />
doubled.

This can be seen generally by taking *any* arc of a<br />
circle cut off by a chord <span data-ref="0378.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>. Bisect the arc in <span data-ref="0378.g1:C">$C$</span>.<br />
Draw a tangent to the circle at <span data-ref="0378.g1:C">$C$</span>, and let <span data-ref="0378.g1:/ A D /">$AD$</span>, <span data-ref="0378.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span><br />
be drawn perpendicular to the tangent. Join <span data-ref="0378.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0378.g1:/ C B /">$CB$</span>.<br />
Then <span data-ref="0378.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> is parallel to <span data-ref="0378.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>, since<br />
$\angle$<span data-ref="0378.g1:< E C B )">$ECB$</span> $= \angle$<span data-ref="0378.g1:< C A B )">$CAB$</span>, in alternate segment, <span class="justification">[III. 32]</span><br />
$= \angle$<span data-ref="0378.g1:< C B A )">$CBA$</span>. <span class="justification">[III. 29, I. 5]</span><br />
Thus <span data-ref="0378.g1:< A B E D >">$ABED$</span> is a $\square$;<br />
and it is greater than the segment <span data-ref="0378.g1:( A C B )">$ACB$</span>.<br />
Therefore its half, the $\triangle$<span data-ref="0378.g1:< A C B >">$ACB$</span>, is greater than half the segment.

Thus, by X. 1, Euclid's construction of successive regular polygons in<br />
a circle, if continued far enough, will at length leave segments which are<br />
together less than any given area.
