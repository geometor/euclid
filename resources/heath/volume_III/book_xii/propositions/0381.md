Legendre proves this proposition by a method equally rigorous but not, I think, possessing any advantages over Euclid's. It depends on a lemma corresponding to Eucl. XII. 16, but with another part added to it.
_Two concentric circles being given, we can always inscribe in the greater a regular polygon such that its sides do not meet the circumference of the lesser, and we can also circumscribe about the lesser a regular polygon such that its sides do not meet the circumference of the greater._
Let <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ C A /">$CA$</span>, <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ C B /">$CB$</span> be the radii of the circles.
**I.** At <span data-ref="0381.g1:A">$A$</span> on the inner circle draw the tangent <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> meeting the outer circle in <span data-ref="0381.g1:D">$D$</span>, <span data-ref="0381.g1:E">$E$</span>.
Inscribe in the outer circle any of the regular polygons which we can inscribe, e.g. a square.
Bisect the arc subtended by a side, bisect the half, bisect that again, and so on, until we arrive at an arc less than the arc <span data-ref="0381.g1:( D B E )">$DBE$</span>.
Let this arc be <span data-ref="0381.g1:( M N )">$MN$</span>, and suppose it so placed that <span data-ref="0381.g1:B">$B$</span> is its middle point.
Then the chord <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ M N /">$MN$</span> is clearly more distant from the centre <span data-ref="0381.g1:C">$C$</span> than <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> is; and the regular polygon, of which <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ M N /">$MN$</span> is a side, does not anywhere meet the circumference of the inner circle.

![Concentric circles and tangent DE](0381.g1.svg)

**II.** Join <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ C M /">$CM$</span>, <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ C N /">$CN$</span>, meeting <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> in <span data-ref="0381.g1:P">$P$</span>, <span data-ref="0381.g1:Q">$Q$</span>.
Then <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ P Q /">$PQ$</span> will be the side of a polygon circumscribed about the inner circle and similar to the polygon inscribed in the outer;
and the circumscribed polygon of which <span data-ref="0381.g1:/ P Q /">$PQ$</span> is a side will not anywhere meet the outer circle.
Legendre now proves XII. 2 after the following manner.
For brevity, let us denote the area of the circle with radius <span data-ref="0381.g2:/ C A /">$CA$</span> by $(\text{circ. }$ <span data-ref="0381.g2:( C A )">$CA$</span>$)$.
Then it is required to prove that, if <span data-ref="0381.g2:/ O B /">$OB$</span> be the radius of a second circle,
$(\text{circ. }$ <span data-ref="0381.g2:( C A )">$CA$</span>$) : (\text{circ. }$ <span data-ref="0381.g2:( O B )">$OB$</span>$) =$ <span data-ref="0381.g2:/ C A /">$CA^2$</span> $:$ <span data-ref="0381.g2:/ O B /">$OB^2$</span>.

![Legendre's two circles](0381.g2.svg)

Suppose, if possible, that this relation is not true. Then <span data-ref="0381.g2:/ C A /">$CA^2$</span> will be to <span data-ref="0381.g2:/ O B /">$OB^2$</span> as $(\text{circ. }$ <span data-ref="0381.g2:( C A )">$CA$</span>$)$ is to an area greater or less than $(\text{circ. }$ <span data-ref="0381.g2:( O B )">$OB$</span>$)$.
**I.** Suppose, first, that
<span data-ref="0381.g2:/ C A /">$CA^2$</span> $:$ <span data-ref="0381.g2:/ O B /">$OB^2$</span> $= (\text{circ. }$ <span data-ref="0381.g2:( C A )">$CA$</span>$) : (\text{circ. }$ <span data-ref="0381.g2:( O D )">$OD$</span>$)$,
where <span data-ref="0381.g2:/ O D /">$OD$</span> is less than <span data-ref="0381.g2:/ O B /">$OB$</span>.
