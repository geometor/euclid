We observe the use in this proposition of the term "parallelepipedal<br />
prism," which recalls Heron's "parallelogrammic" or "parallel-sided prism."

The course of the proof is exactly the same as in XII. 2, except that an<br />
arithmetical fraction takes the place of a ratio which, being incommensurable,<br />
could only be expressed as a ratio. Consequently we do not need proportions<br />
in this proposition, as we did in XII. 2, and shall again in XII. 11, etc.

Euclid *exhausts* the cylinder and cone respectively by setting up prisms<br />
and pyramids of the same height on the successive regular polygons inscribed<br />
in the circle which is the common base, viz. the square, the regular polygon<br />
of 8 sides, that of 16 sides, etc.

If <span data-ref="0408.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> be the side of one polygon, we obtain two sides of the next by<br />
bisecting the arc <span data-ref="0408.g1:( A C B )">$ACB$</span> and joining <span data-ref="0408.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0408.g1:/ C B /">$CB$</span>. Draw the<br />
tangent <span data-ref="0408.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> at <span data-ref="0408.g1:C">$C$</span> and complete the parallelogram<br />
<span data-ref="0408.g1:< A B E D >">$ABED$</span>.

Now suppose a prism erected on the polygon of<br />
which <span data-ref="0408.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is a side, and of the same height as that of<br />
the cylinder.

To obtain the prism of the same height on the next<br />
polygon we add all the triangular prisms of the same<br />
height on the bases <span data-ref="0408.g1:< A C B >">$ACB$</span> and the rest.

Now the prism on <span data-ref="0408.g1:< A C B >">$ACB$</span> is half the prism of the<br />
same height on the $\square$<span data-ref="0408.g1:< A B E D >">$ABED$</span> as base.<br />
