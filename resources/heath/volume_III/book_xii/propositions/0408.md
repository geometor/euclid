We observe the use in this proposition of the term "parallelepipedal prism," which recalls Heron's "parallelogrammic" or "parallel-sided prism."

The course of the proof is exactly the same as in XII. 2, except that an arithmetical fraction takes the place of a ratio which, being incommensurable, could only be expressed as a ratio. Consequently we do not need proportions in this proposition, as we did in XII. 2, and shall again in XII. 11, etc.

Euclid *exhausts* the cylinder and cone respectively by setting up prisms and pyramids of the same height on the successive regular polygons inscribed in the circle which is the common base, viz. the square, the regular polygon of 8 sides, that of 16 sides, etc.

If <span data-ref="0408.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> be the side of one polygon, we obtain two sides of the next by bisecting the arc <span data-ref="0408.g1:( A C B )">$ACB$</span> and joining <span data-ref="0408.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0408.g1:/ C B /">$CB$</span>. Draw the tangent <span data-ref="0408.g1:/ D E /">$DE$</span> at <span data-ref="0408.g1:C">$C$</span> and complete the parallelogram <span data-ref="0408.g1:< A B E D >">$ABED$</span>.

Now suppose a prism erected on the polygon of which <span data-ref="0408.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> is a side, and of the same height as that of the cylinder.

To obtain the prism of the same height on the next polygon we add all the triangular prisms of the same height on the bases <span data-ref="0408.g1:< A C B >">$ACB$</span> and the rest.

Now the prism on <span data-ref="0408.g1:< A C B >">$ACB$</span> is half the prism of the same height on the $\square$<span data-ref="0408.g1:< A B E D >">$ABED$</span> as base. [cf. XI. 28]
