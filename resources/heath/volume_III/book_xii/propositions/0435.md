This proposition is of great length and therefore requires summarising in<br />
order to make it easier to grasp. Moreover there are some assumptions in it<br />
which require to be proved, and some omissions to be supplied. The figure<br />
also is one of some complexity, and, in addition, the text and the figure treat<br />
two points <span data-ref="0435.g1:Z">$Z$</span> and <span data-ref="0435.g1:V">$V$</span>, which are really one and the same, as different.

The first thing needed is to know that all sections of a sphere by planes<br />
through the centre are circles and equal to one another (great circles or<br />
"greatest circles" as Euclid calls them, more appropriately). Euclid uses his<br />
definition of a sphere as the figure described by a semicircle revolving about<br />
its diameter. This of course establishes that all planes through the particular<br />
diameter make equal circular sections; but it is also assumed that the same<br />
sphere is generated by *any other* semicircle of the same size and with its<br />
centre at the same point.

![Book XII, Proposition 17, Heath's Diagram](0435.g1.svg)

The construction and argument of the proposition may be shortly given<br />
as follows.

A plane through the centre of two concentric spheres cuts them in great<br />
circles of which <span data-ref="0435.g1:( B E )">$BE$</span>, <span data-ref="0435.g1:( G F )">$GF$</span> are quadrants.

A regular polygon with an even number of sides is inscribed (*exactly as in<br />
Prop. 16*) to the outer circle such that its sides do not touch the inner circle.<br />
<span data-ref="0435.g1:/ B K /">$BK$</span>, <span data-ref="0435.g1:/ K L /">$KL$</span>, <span data-ref="0435.g1:/ L M /">$LM$</span>, <span data-ref="0435.g1:/ M E /">$ME$</span> are the sides in the quadrant <span data-ref="0435.g1:( B E )">$BE$</span>.
