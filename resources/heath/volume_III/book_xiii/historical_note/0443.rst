:person:`Hypsicles <hypsicles>` proceeds (pp. 7 sqq.) to give a proof of this theorem. :person:`Allman <allman>` pointed out (:cite:`*Greek Geometry from Thales to Euclid*, 1889, pp. 201—2 <allman:greek-geometry>`) that this proof depends on eight theorems, six of which appear in Euclid's Book XIII. (in Propositions 8, 10, 12, 15, 16 with Por., 17); two other propositions not mentioned by :person:`Allman <allman>` are also used, namely XIII. 4 and 9. This seems, as :person:`Allman <allman>` says, to confirm the inference of :person:`Bretschneider <bretschneider>` (p. 171) that, as :person:`Aristaeus <aristaeus>`' work was the newest and latest in which, before Euclid's time, this subject was treated, we have in Eucl. XIII. at least a partial recapitulation of the contents of the treatise of :person:`Aristaeus <aristaeus>`.

After Euclid, :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>` wrote on the comparison of the dodecahedron and the icosahedron inscribed in one and the same sphere. This we also learn from :person:`Hypsicles <hypsicles>`, who says in the next words following those about :person:`Aristaeus <aristaeus>` above quoted: "But it is proved by :person:`Apollonius <apollonius>` in the second edition of his :cite:`*Comparison of the dodecahedron with the icosahedron* <apollonius:comparison-solids>` that, as the surface of the dodecahedron is to the surface of the icosahedron [inscribed in the same sphere], so is the dodecahedron itself [i.e. its volume] to the icosahedron, because the perpendicular is the same from the centre of the sphere to the pentagon of the dodecahedron and to the triangle of the icosahedron."
