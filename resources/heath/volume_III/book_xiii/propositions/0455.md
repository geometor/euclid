<p class="assertion">And <span data-ref="0454.g1:/ C A /">$CA$</span> was also proved to be an apotome.</p>

<p class="qed">Therefore etc. <span class="justification">Q. E. D.</span></p>

---

It seems certain that this proposition is an interpolation. P has it, but the<br />
copyist (or rather the copyist of its archetype) says that "this theorem is not<br />
found in most copies of the new recension, but is found in those of the old."<br />
In the first place, there is a scholium to XIII. 17 in P itself which proves the<br />
same thing as XIII. 6, and which would therefore have been useless if XIII. 6<br />
had preceded. Hence, when the scholium was written, this proposition had<br />
not yet been interpolated. Secondly, P has it before the alternative proof of<br />
XIII. 5; this proof is considered, on general grounds, to be interpolated, and<br />
it would appear that it must have been a *later* interpolation (XIII. 6) which<br />
divorced it from the proposition to which it belonged. Thirdly, there is cause<br />
for suspicion in the proposition itself, for, while the enunciation states that<br />
each segment of the straight line is an *apotome*, the proposition adds that the<br />
lesser segment is a *first apotome*. The scholium in P referred to has not this<br />
blot. What is actually wanted in XIII. 17 is the fact that the *greater* segment<br />
is an apotome. It is probable that Euclid assumed this fact as evident enough<br />
from XIII. 1 without further proof, and that he neither wrote XIII. 6 nor the<br />
quotation of its enunciation in XIII. 17.

### PROPOSITION 7.

<p class="enunciation">If three angles of an equilateral pentagon, taken either in<br />
order or not in order, be equal, the pentagon will be equiangular.</p>

For in the equilateral pentagon <span data-ref="0455.g1:< A B C D E >">$ABCDE$</span> let, first, three<br />
angles taken in order, those at <span data-ref="0455.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:C">$C$</span>,<br />
be equal to one another;<br />
I say that the pentagon <span data-ref="0455.g1:< A B C D E >">$ABCDE$</span> is<br />
equiangular.

![Book XIII, Proposition 7](0455.g1.svg)

For let <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ F D /">$FD$</span> be joined.

<p class="sub-clause">Now, since the two sides <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ C B /">$CB$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ B A /">$BA$</span><br />
are equal to the two sides <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ B A /">$BA$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ A E /">$AE$</span><br />
respectively,<br />
and the angle <span data-ref="0455.g1:< C B A )">$CBA$</span> is equal to the<br />
angle <span data-ref="0455.g1:< B A E )">$BAE$</span>,</p>
<p class="assertion">therefore the base <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span> is equal to the base <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span>,<br />
the triangle <span data-ref="0455.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> is equal to the triangle <span data-ref="0455.g1:< A B E >">$ABE$</span>,<br />
and the remaining angles will be equal to the remaining angles,<br />
namely those which the equal sides subtend, <span class="justification">[I. 4]</span><br />
that is, the angle <span data-ref="0455.g1:< B C A )">$BCA$</span> to the angle <span data-ref="0455.g1:< B E A )">$BEA$</span>, and the angle<br />
<span data-ref="0455.g1:< A B E )">$ABE$</span> to the angle <span data-ref="0455.g1:< C A B )">$CAB$</span>;</p>
<p class="assertion">hence the side <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ A F /">$AF$</span> is also equal to the side <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ B F /">$BF$</span>. <span class="justification">[I. 6]</span></p>
