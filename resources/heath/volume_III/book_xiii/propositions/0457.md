<p class="assertion">For the same reason<br />
the angle <span data-ref="0455.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span> is also equal to the angles at <span data-ref="0455.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:C">$C$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:D">$D$</span>.<br />
Therefore the pentagon <span data-ref="0455.g1:< A B C D E >">$ABCDE$</span> is equiangular.</p>

<p class="qed"><span class="justification">Q. E. D.</span></p>

---

This proposition is required in XIII. 17.<br />
The steps of the proof may be shown thus.

I. Suppose that the angles at <span data-ref="0455.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:C">$C$</span> are all equal.<br />
Then the isosceles triangles <span data-ref="0455.g1:< B A E >">$BAE$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:< A B C >">$ABC$</span> are equal in all respects;<br />
thus <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, $\angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< B C A )">$BCA$</span> $= \angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< B E A )">$BEA$</span>, $\angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< C A B )">$CAB$</span> $= \angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< E B A )">$EBA$</span>.<br />
By the last equality, <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ F A /">$FA$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ F B /">$FB$</span>,<br />
so that, since <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ F C /">$FC$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ F E /">$FE$</span>.<br />
The $\Delta\text{s}$ <span data-ref="0455.g1:< F E D >">$FED$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:< F C D >">$FCD$</span> are now equal in all respects, <span class="justification">[I. 8, 4]</span><br />
and $\angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< F C D )">$FCD$</span> $= \angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< F E D )">$FED$</span>.<br />
But $\angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< A C B )">$ACB$</span> $= \angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< A E B )">$AEB$</span>, from above,<br />
whence, by addition, $\angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< B C D )">$BCD$</span> $= \angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< A E D )">$AED$</span>.<br />
Similarly it may be proved that $\angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< C D E )">$CDE$</span> is also equal to any one of the<br />
angles at <span data-ref="0455.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:B">$B$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:C">$C$</span>.

II. Suppose the angles at <span data-ref="0455.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:C">$C$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:D">$D$</span> to be equal.<br />
Then the isosceles triangles <span data-ref="0455.g1:< A B E >">$ABE$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:< C B D >">$CBD$</span> are equal in all respects, and<br />
hence <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0455.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span> (so that $\angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< B D E )">$BDE$</span> $= \angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< B E D )">$BED$</span>),<br />
and $\angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< C D B )">$CDB$</span> $= \angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< A E B )">$AEB$</span>.<br />
By addition of the equal angles,<br />
$\angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< C D E )">$CDE$</span> $= \angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< D E A )">$DEA$</span>.<br />
Similarly it may be proved that $\angle$<span data-ref="0455.g1:< A B C )">$ABC$</span> is also equal to each of the angles<br />
at <span data-ref="0455.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:C">$C$</span>, <span data-ref="0455.g1:D">$D$</span>.

### PROPOSITION 8.

<p class="enunciation">If in an equilateral and equiangular pentagon straight<br />
lines subtend two angles taken in order, they cut one another<br />
in extreme and mean ratio, and their greater segments are equal<br />
to the side of the pentagon.</p>

For in the equilateral and equiangular pentagon <span data-ref="0457.g1:< A B C D E >">$ABCDE$</span><br />
let the straight lines <span data-ref="0457.g1:/ A C /">$AC$</span>, <span data-ref="0457.g1:/ B E /">$BE$</span>, cutting<br />
one another at the point <span data-ref="0457.g1:H">$H$</span>, subtend<br />
two angles taken in order, the angles<br />
at <span data-ref="0457.g1:A">$A$</span>, <span data-ref="0457.g1:B">$B$</span>;<br />
I say that each of them has been<br />
cut in extreme and mean ratio at<br />
the point <span data-ref="0457.g1:H">$H$</span>, and their greater seg-<br />
ments are equal to the side of the<br />
pentagon.

For let the circle <span data-ref="0457.g1:( A B C D E )">$ABCDE$</span> be<br />
circumscribed about the pentagon <span data-ref="0457.g1:< A B C D E >">$ABCDE$</span>. <span class="justification">[IV. 14]</span>
