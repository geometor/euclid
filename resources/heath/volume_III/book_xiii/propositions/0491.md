<span data-ref="0491.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span> *is the radius of the circular sections containing the pentagons.*  
[If $r$ is the radius of the sphere,  
since
<span data-ref="0491.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> $:$ <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span> $= $ <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>$^2 : $ <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> $\cdot$ <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>
$= $ <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>$^2 : $ <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>$^2$,
while
<span data-ref="0491.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span> $= 5$<span data-ref="0491.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>,
it follows that
<span data-ref="0491.g1:/ A B /">$AB$</span>$^2 = 5$<span data-ref="0491.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>$^2$,
or
$$\text{(radius of section)}^2 = \frac{4}{5} r^2.$$
Thus [XIII. 10, note] $\text{(side of pentagon)}^2 = \frac{r^2}{5} (10 - 2\sqrt{5})$.]

Inscribe the regular pentagon <span data-ref="0491.g1:< E F G H K >">$EFGHK$</span> in the circle <span data-ref="0491.g1:( E F G H K )">$EFGHK$</span> of radius equal to <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ B D /">$BD$</span>.  
Bisect the arcs <span data-ref="0491.g1:( E F )">$EF$</span>, <span data-ref="0491.g1:( F G )">$FG$</span>, ..., so forming a decagon in the circle.  
Joining successive points of bisection, we obtain another regular pentagon <span data-ref="0491.g1:< L M N O P >">$LMNOP$</span>.  
<span data-ref="0491.g1:< L M N O P >">$LMNOP$</span> *is one of the pentagons containing five edges of the icosahedron.*  
The other circle and inscribed pentagon are obtained by drawing perpendiculars from <span data-ref="0491.g1:E">$E$</span>, <span data-ref="0491.g1:F">$F$</span>, <span data-ref="0491.g1:G">$G$</span>, <span data-ref="0491.g1:H">$H$</span>, <span data-ref="0491.g1:K">$K$</span> to the plane of the circle, as <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ E Q /">$EQ$</span>, <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ F R /">$FR$</span>, <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ G S /">$GS$</span>, <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ H T /">$HT$</span>, <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ K U /">$KU$</span>, and making each of these perpendiculars equal to the radius of the circle, or, as Euclid says, the side of the regular hexagon in it.  
<span data-ref="0491.g1:< Q R S T U >">$QRSTU$</span> *is the second pentagon (of course equal to the first) containing five edges of the icosahedron.*  
Joining each angular point of one of the two pentagons to the two nearest angular points in the other pentagon, we complete ten triangles each of which has for one side a side of one or other of the two pentagons.  
<span data-ref="0491.g1:V">$V$</span>, <span data-ref="0491.g1:W">$W$</span> are the centres of the two circles, and <span data-ref="0491.g1:/ V W /">$VW$</span> is of course perpendicular to the planes of both.
