In this proposition we find Euclid using two propositions which precede<br />
but are used nowhere else, notably VI. 32, which some authors, in consequence<br />
of their having overlooked its use here, have been hard put to it to explain.

Euclid's construction in this case is really identical with that given by<br />
Mr H. M. Taylor, and also referred to by Henrici and Treutlein under "crystal-<br />
formation."

Euclid starts from the cube inscribed in a sphere, as in XIII. 15, and then<br />
finds the side of the regular pentagon in which the side of the cube is a<br />
diagonal.

Mr Taylor takes $l$ to be the diagonal of a regular pentagon of side $a$,<br />
so that, by Ptolemy's theorem,
$$l^2 = al + a^2,$$
constructs a cube of which $l$ is the edge, and gets the side of the pentagon<br />
by drawing <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ Z X /">$ZX$</span> from <span data-ref="0503.g1:Z">$Z$</span>, the centre of the cube, perpendicular to the face <span data-ref="0503.g1:< B F >">$BF$</span><br />
and equal to $\frac{1}{2}(l + a)$, then drawing <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ U V /">$UV$</span> through <span data-ref="0503.g1:X">$X$</span> parallel to <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ B C /">$BC$</span>, and<br />
making <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ U X /">$UX$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ X V /">$XV$</span> both equal to $\frac{1}{2}a$.

Euclid finds <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ U V /">$UV$</span> thus.

Draw <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ N O /">$NO$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ M H /">$MH$</span> bisecting pairs of opposite sides in the square <span data-ref="0503.g1:< B F >">$BF$</span> and<br />
meeting in <span data-ref="0503.g1:P">$P$</span>.

Draw <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ G K /">$GK$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ H L /">$HL$</span> bisecting pairs of opposite sides in the square <span data-ref="0503.g1:< B D >">$BD$</span> and<br />
meeting in <span data-ref="0503.g1:Q">$Q$</span>.

Divide <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ P N /">$PN$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ P O /">$PO$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ Q H /">$QH$</span> respectively in extreme and mean ratio at <span data-ref="0503.g1:R">$R$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:S">$S$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:T">$T$</span><br />
(<span data-ref="0503.g1:/ P R /">$PR$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ P S /">$PS$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ T Q /">$TQ$</span> being the greater segments); draw <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ R U /">$RU$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ S V /">$SV$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ T W /">$TW$</span> outwards<br />
perpendicular to the respective faces of the cube, and all equal in length<br />
to <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ P R /">$PR$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ P S /">$PS$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ T Q /">$TQ$</span>.

Join <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ B U /">$BU$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ U V /">$UV$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ V C /">$VC$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ C W /">$CW$</span>, <span data-ref="0503.g1:/ W B /">$WB$</span>.

Then <span data-ref="0503.g1:< B U V C W >">$BUVCW$</span> is one of the pentagonal faces of the dodecahedron;<br />
and the others can be constructed in the same way.
